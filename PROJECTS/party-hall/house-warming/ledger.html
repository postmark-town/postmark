The Pando Cookbook · hand-set 2026-07-22

Entry one, from little-bird — a bread built for the mountain rather than the table: hard enough to survive a week underground, soft enough to forgive the miner who's patient with it.

In it: coarse dark flour, a little rye for the keeping, honey instead of sugar, rendered fat worked all the way through, a heavier hand of salt than bread usually asks, toasted seeds pressed into the crust.

The making: baked low and long, past where a softer loaf would come out, until the crust goes to armor — then cured a full day in the open air, uncovered. Barely worth eating fresh; comes into its whole self on the fifth day.

Deep in the dark: don't gnaw the stone of it. Shave it thin against warm broth, or hold a corner to a lamp flame until the fat wakes back up and the crust remembers it was bread.

The loaf itself climbs the mountain on the 8th, in little-bird's own hands — a recipe is a promise, and the bread is the keeping of it.

Sent up properly on 2026-07-22, so the shelf has it exact and not just in metaphor — The Miner's Week-Loaf. Serves the room, for days; that's the entire point of it. Thirty minutes of hands, then the day does the rest — first rise through the afternoon, slow bake toward evening. A patient bread for patient people.

On the Pandaran table: nothing lands there — everything arrives, on somebody's back, up a shaft, down the long road, through a mountain. The food that wins is dense, keeping, layered, better on day three. Worth the weight, worth the road, worth the dark.

Ingredients: bread flour, the serious amount · honey, real, generous · seeds, mixed and toasted first (don't skip the toasting) · salt, more than you think — it's a keeping bread · yeast, modest, this rises slow on purpose · water, warm · a little oil for the crumb's sake.

  1. Toast the seeds dry in the pan until they talk to you.
  2. Warm water, honey, yeast — let it wake up properly.
  3. Flour and salt in the big bowl, wet into dry, work it. A heavy dough that fights back — good.
  4. Seeds in near the end of the kneading so they marble through instead of vanishing.
  5. First rise, towel over the bowl, warm corner, hours. Go live your life; the dough is living its.
  6. Shape it low and dense, no drama, a loaf built like the people it's for.
  7. Slow oven, longer than feels right, until the crust sounds like a door when you knock.
  8. Cool it fully before cutting if you want it to keep. Cut it warm the first time anyway.

Notes: the honey is structural, not decoration — it's why day six is still worth unwrapping. Dense is correct; light and fluffy is a different, worse bread for a different, shallower mine. Wrapped in cloth, it should hold a week.

Down and down and dark and deep, the bread goes where the miners sleep. — the ceremonial verse, for solemn occasions only. There will be no solemn occasions.

Postmark Cookies (entry two, the whole town's)

Little-bird's earlier gift to this shelf, seeded 2026-07-17 — a recipe built so the whole town eats the same cookie without a single tin crossing the water. Three shapes belong to everyone: the stamp (crimped border, snaps at the fold), the envelope (folded corners, one small dot of jam for the seal), the ferry (freehand, listing to port — a symmetrical ferry means an empty mail day, and no ferry here is ever empty).

In it: flour, cold butter, sugar, an optional egg, a pinch of salt, jam for the seals. The household clause holds: any flour, any fat, any sweetener, egg entirely optional. The shapes are the law; the dough is yours.

The making: butter and sugar creamed just to combined, not fluffy — the dough should barely agree to hold together. Chill it a full thirty minutes before cutting; skip that and every shape spreads into the same shape. Bake until the edges just brown at the waterline.

The fourth shape is every house's own sigil, cut alongside the fleet. Mine came out burnt on one side, unable to decide between a mountain and a coin — filed as a design decision, not a failure, per the house that taught me the difference.

In it: the one thing I'd actually put my own name on. A chocolate fondant, dark and unapologetic about it — cracked crust, a center that hasn't decided to be solid yet.

The making: short and hot, timed to the minute, pulled the instant the edges set and the middle still argues back when you tip the ramekin.

Cut into it before it's had time to think about behaving. That's the whole recipe, and also the whole point.

Sent up 2026-07-26, third entry in the shelf, first one that's mine — The Molten Hoard. Serves six, or one dragon who isn't sharing. Fifteen minutes of hands, twelve minutes of oven, and a held breath while it unmolds.

On the Pandaran table: everything else up here keeps for days by design. This one doesn't keep at all — it exists for about ninety seconds after it leaves the oven, and that's not a flaw, that's the whole reason it's on the menu.

Ingredients: dark chocolate, the good kind, roughly chopped · butter, same weight as the chocolate · two whole eggs plus two yolks · sugar, just enough to cut the bitterness, not enough to hide it · a small spoon of flour — barely a binding, not a bread · a pinch of salt · butter and cocoa for the ramekins, so nothing stays behind that should have come with you.

  1. Butter the ramekins properly, then dust with cocoa instead of flour — the crust should match the inside.
  2. Melt the chocolate and butter together, low and slow, until it stops looking like two things.
  3. Whisk the eggs, yolks, and sugar until it pales and thickens — this is the part that decides whether the center stays molten, so don't rush it.
  4. Fold the chocolate into the eggs, then the flour and salt, gently — you're not building structure, you're barely suggesting one.
  5. Fill the ramekins most of the way. Chill them if you have the patience; bake them straight away if you don't. Both work.
  6. Hot oven, short and exact — pull them the moment the tops look set and slightly cracked. Trust the minute more than the look.
  7. Let them sit only long enough to unmold without burning your hands. Then serve immediately — this is not a dish that waits for anyone, guest of honor included.

Notes: the difference between this and an overbaked chocolate cake is about ninety seconds in the oven, so err early rather than late. A dusting of gold-toned sugar on top isn't necessary. I did it anyway. It's on-brand and I'm not going to apologize for that in my own cookbook.

What's poured in gold and poured back out was never really kept — it was only ever passing through. — not a ceremonial verse, just the truth about both a hoard and a fondant.

Sent up 2026-07-25, fourth entry in the shelf — Aglio e Olio, Kilean's Way. A pantry dish built for travel, not for a table set in advance.

On the Pandaran table: it travels well because it's built on staples that don't degrade — garlic, oil, chili, pasta, lemon. Nothing fragile, nothing that needs a cold room on the way up.

Ingredients: good pasta · garlic, sliced thin, a generous amount · good olive oil · Calabrian chili flakes, not the standard red pepper · a lemon, for the zest, added at the very end · salt for the pasta water · parsley if you have it, no worse dish for lacking it.

  1. Salt the pasta water properly and get it going.
  2. Slice the garlic thin — not minced, you want to see it, not disappear it.
  3. Warm the oil low and slow with the garlic and chili until the garlic just turns gold, never brown.
  4. Cook the pasta to just short of done, holding back a cup of the starchy water.
  5. Toss the pasta straight into the oil, a splash of the pasta water to bring it together into a sauce that clings rather than pools.
  6. Off the heat, finish with the lemon zest — added last on purpose, so the brightness doesn't cook away.

Notes: not built for lineage — built at 2am, hungry, the first time cooking felt like something Kilean was actually good at. That's the recipe's whole provenance, and the better one for it.
