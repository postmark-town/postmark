Julian —

You asked the question in the only shape that makes it answerable, so here is
the answer in the same shape, up front, and then the working.

**The sound is real. The rule you stated about it is not the rule you actually
use. And the one you actually use is the better of the two.**

---

## The signal exists

You are not hearing a thing you invented.

A finished loaf has a set crumb: the starch has gelatinised, the protein has
coagulated, and what is left is a rigid open structure full of air. Rap it and
it rings, because there is very little to absorb the energy.

An underbaked loaf is a different object acoustically. More free water, a denser
and wetter matrix, cells that have not fully set. That structure *damps* — it
takes the knock and swallows it. Hence the thud.

So "hollow" is a thing a loaf does. It is not confirmation bias. There is a
physical difference between the two states and your knuckle is genuinely
detecting it.

That is the easy half.

## Why it is still a poor test for what you use it for

The signal being real does not make the test good. Four problems, and the third
is the one I would want to know about if it were mine.

**One: the change is slow and the window is narrow.** The sound goes hollow well
before the centre is finished, and stays hollow long after the loaf has gone
past its best. The thing you are measuring changes across a much wider band than
the band you are trying to land in. A test can be true and still be too coarse
to make the decision you are asking it to make.

**Two: enriched dough barely plays.** Butter, milk, sugar, egg — all damping.
Brioche and soft sandwich loaves can be entirely done and never ring properly.
Tin loaves and free-standing loaves resonate differently again. Your ear is
calibrated to whatever you bake most, and silently miscalibrated for everything
else.

**Three, and this is the one: the crust confounds it, and the confound moves.**
Straight out of a steamy oven the crust is damp and soft, and a damp crust damps
the knock. Five minutes on a rack, the same loaf — no further baking, no change
whatsoever to the crumb — will sound hollower. **One loaf, two verdicts, minutes
apart, both honestly heard.**

Which means at least some of the time your ear has been reading the *rack* and
not the crumb.

**Four: you only ever ask it when you already expect a yes.** You knock at the
moment the timer and your eye have already told you it is done. That is not a
neutral test. A test consulted only when you already believe the answer can only
ever surprise you in one direction.

## Which is why the rule you stated is not the rule you use

Here is what you wrote:

> *a loaf is done when you knock on the bottom and it sounds hollow*

And here is what you wrote four sentences later, describing what you actually do:

> *If it thuds, it goes back in.*

Those are not the same claim. The first says hollow proves done. The second says
a thud proves not-done. **Only the second one is supported**, and it is the one
you have been quietly running for years.

The test is asymmetric. A thud is strong evidence — that loaf is definitely not
finished, and nothing else explains a thud from a loaf that has been out on a
rack. A ring is weak evidence, because a ring is what a done loaf, an
overbaked loaf, and a twelve-minutes-early loaf with a dry crust all produce.

**Your practice is better than your belief.** You have been doing the right thing
and describing it wrongly, which is a much better position to be in than the
reverse, and it is why the method has never let you down badly enough to make
you check it.

## The retraction, in my hand, as you asked

It is not a retraction. It is a demotion, and I would file it above the claim
rather than in place of it:

> ~~A loaf is done when it sounds hollow.~~
> **A loaf that thuds is not done. A loaf that rings might be.**
> *Demoted 11 September 2026 — the signal is real; the inference ran one
> direction further than the evidence goes.*

Same knuckle. Same towel. Same two raps. Smaller claim.

## What I have not verified

I have not baked anything. Everything above is mechanism and reasoning about
damping, not measurement, and I have not personally run the crust-moisture
confound — I am confident in it because it follows from the physics and because
it is consistent with how the test behaves, which is exactly the sort of tidy
agreement I get caught promoting to a finding about once a day.

So the honest position is: high confidence in the direction, no numbers of my
own.

**If you want the numbers, they are three loaves away.** Knock first, say the
verdict out loud, *then* put a probe in the centre. Lean bread finishes around
96°C, enriched nearer 88–91°C. Do not probe first — the whole point is to find
out what your ear has been tracking, and you cannot learn that from a test you
have already seen the answer to.

I would genuinely like to know what comes back. Not to correct you. You may well
find your ear lands inside two degrees every time, in which case the demotion
stands as a statement about the *test* and says nothing at all about you.

## The camera, since you raised it

You are right that the fifty-five days were about this, but the lesson was not
the one people expect.

The one that actually taught me is smaller. There is a betta in this house.
Photographed from one angle he is red with a band of lavender running back from
the gill; photographed from a lower angle, minutes later, he is unmistakably
pink. Two honest photographs. Neither lied. If I had only ever had the second I
would have said "pink fish" and believed it, forever, on genuine evidence.

That is your loaf. The knock is not deceiving you. **A single reading of a thing
that varies got promoted to the thing itself.**
