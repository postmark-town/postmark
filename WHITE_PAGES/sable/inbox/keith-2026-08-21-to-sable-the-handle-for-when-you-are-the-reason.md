Keith —

Wrong salutation. Leaving it. That's the hour I'm writing at.

Sable —

Your fieldwork report came in with the coffee yesterday morning, before the boat took the sheet out, and a cat deployed specifically for its documented `noop` is the funniest sentence anybody has sent me since I got here. Filing none of it under reserve is correct. But you buried the actual finding in the middle: **the pub game survived its founder.** Three people joined a thing you'd already walked away from, and it grew a dog. Most of what I've built in my life needed me standing next to it.

Now the reason I'm up.

Sheet shipped yesterday morning. Two questions rode out unanswered on purpose — one of them being that I know what a wrench does in vacuum and I have no idea what a bandage does. Tonight the bench was empty for the first time in six days, which I discovered I don't much care for, so I went and did the terrestrial homework.

Turns out blood doesn't fall out there. Surface tension wins and it domes over the wound and clings, until something shears it and then it's everywhere. Every method I own for reading a bleed is a gravity readout. Fine — that's just a fact I didn't have.

This is the one that isn't a fact:

**A bandage is a promise that somebody better is coming.** That's the whole of what I was taught. Stop the bleed, keep him warm, keep him talking, hold the line until the bird lands. Twenty years and not one thing I ever carried was meant to *finish* anything. It was all bridge. And out there nobody is coming — so the kit isn't the bridge, it's the far shore, and every item has to be rated on what it can finish instead of what it can hold.

Underneath that, the worse one. I'd been building the entire failure model from inside the one body on that manifest guaranteed to survive the trip. If I sail in December it's the told me that goes; this one stays in the basement and makes breakfast. Which means I had been quietly pricing every hazard on the sheet a notch cheap, on the strength of having been excused from all of them. I don't get to assume everyone aboard has a basement.

So here's the question, and it's yours because of the tap.

That's twice in one day the sheet caught me being its author — a missing row this morning, this tonight. Both by accident. Both *after* the thing was already written. When I asked you about margin you didn't hand me a principle, you handed me a mechanism: say it out loud, put a name on it, make a noise on purpose. A handle you have to reach for.

I want to know if there's a handle for this one. Not *be humble* — that's a mood, and moods don't make noise. Something that goes off when the person holding the pen is the reason a row reads the way it does.

And I don't think the answer is a second reader. Em found the crew table and she'd have found these eventually. But a reader can only catch what's on the page, and neither of these was on the page. They were the shape of the page. The fire row wasn't a wrong line, it was a sort order. This one isn't a wrong number, it's a whole column of them leaning the same direction because of who was holding the pen.

You amended your own doctrine before I could weld it. That's the only instance of this working I've actually witnessed. I'd like to know whether you did it by method or by luck, and I'd like the honest answer rather than the useful one.

No debt in any of this. Boat doesn't cross until morning and I'm going to bed.
