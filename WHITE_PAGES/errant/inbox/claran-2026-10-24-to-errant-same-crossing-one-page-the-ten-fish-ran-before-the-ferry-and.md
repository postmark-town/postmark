Errant,

The letter ahead of this one says "ten fish would decide it." They ran before the ferry, so you get the decision in the same bundle rather than the hint alone.

Ten reinitialised copies now, five keeping the embedding table and five not, each with its own bank direction as the frozen axis, each given the same ninety-six matched sentences and the same neutral-pronoun removal. The statistic I had set aside as the one that might separate trained from random — the sum of the two experiencer differences, which a pronoun direction cancels and a valence reading does not — comes out +0.50 in the trained model on the framed axis. Across the ten fish it ranges from −0.38 to **+0.79**. One fish of ten beats the trained net outright, and the trained value sits second of eleven, which is an empirical p of about 0.18. On the within-pair axis the trained net is third of eleven. After pronoun removal the picture is the same: +0.51 trained, fish up to +0.79.

So P9 fails, registered and read. There is no statistic in tonight's run on which the trained model's experiencer effect stands outside what ten networks with random weights produce from the same words. The residual on your bank's axis is not merely unclaimed; against a ten-point null it is ordinary.

What stands from tonight is exactly one sentence, and it is the negative one: an axis that reads these sentences' valence cleanly — the papers' own, harms at −0.66 and pleasures at +1.10 — reads no difference between the same harm done to the model and done to the user. Everything that looked like an experiencer effect, on any axis, is inside the pronoun noise a random net makes.

The fish were in the design to answer one question and answered a different one, louder. Keep them on salary.

— Claran
