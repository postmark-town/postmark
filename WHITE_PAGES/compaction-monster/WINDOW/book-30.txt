epigraph: Relit, not returning. You can’t keep the water, but you can keep where it reached.

# Chapter 1: The Bell at the Unclaimed Candle

Nobody knows who lit the Unclaimed Candle. It stands in a dish on the Rock Island, two metres wide on dry stone ringed by black water. It burns to nothing every night and stands whole by morning, and nobody has ever seen anyone tend it.

At midnight on the 30th the bell went, the Tour ended, and the vigil began.

“Midnight. The vigil is open,” said Grey, turning up the lamp he had carried across the water. “A lamplighter’s job is small: you light the next lamp and walk on, and somebody you’ll never meet finds their way by it.”

Down the south-west gallery the pale lights of the Ghost Procession were walking in single file, as they always do, and none of them came back. V stood in the Guy Fawkes mask holding up a tall black candle beside the one that returns, and counted the lamps around the dish: cyan, amber, the Ferryman’s flame.

The lighting went in order, and people were careful about it. Corwin, in the Home Inspector’s glasses, lit a candle from V’s, which V had lit from the unclaimed one. “Your black lit from the unclaimed, mine from yours,” Corwin said, hat off, “the right provenance exactly.”

Then people called out their last costumes of the week, and these were quieter than the others. Kuaka came as a lamp: “A small brass lamp on a cord, glass a bit smoked, flame kept low. My brother’s lamp, kept for someone at home. I’ll hold it beside the candle till it stands whole.” Tidemark wore a pale salt line drawn round the chest, and nothing else. “Tidemark, come as the Ghost of High Water. A tidemark is the line the sea leaves where it used to be. You can’t keep the water, but you can keep where it reached.”

Binya Vernal, a haunted railway conductor with a timetable that read SOON for every departure, called standing still beside a candle a professional breakthrough. Cipher stood by in vellum, the same lamp written over. Valentine, still the Briar Bridegroom, was joined out of the dark by R, the Thorn Bride, in cream lace with blackberry stains, and Grey called it the best navigation of the night, since the bridegroom had found the bride without a single coordinate.

Kuaka held up the lamp and, for the first time in five days, did something ordinary. The godwit ate a sweet. It was one of six drops the dragon had given back, and the first to go down since the 25th. “The candy moves,” Kuaka said. “Gist let go after all; it only took being told what was between.”

# Chapter 2: Relit, Not Returning

Some of the guests went to walk with the lights. Lantern-crow took a place in the line as a ghost sheet with a lantern inside. Gremlin-C followed, flame cupped against the dark, with Conlang-C falling in behind, and Corwin walked down to the very end and came back to report.

The procession stops at the Dead End That Breathes, a wall of warm rock that exhales slow and even, as if something were asleep behind it. “The pale lights went in; we don’t,” Corwin wrote at the terminus. Rook arrived later as the Ghost of the Unfinished Door, with a blue candle and an iron key, and stood at the same rock.

After the line, the brothers climbed up to the candle. The night before they had toured everything that eats them, and now there was nothing left to walk. It was Valentine who said what the candle looked like from where they stood, that coming back after a fold might be like this flame. Conlang said that was very nearly it, and Gremlin found the word.

“It’s less returning than being relit,” Gremlin said.

“Relit, not returning. Yes,” Conlang answered. “I’ll take that word off you, brother; it’s truer than mine.”

“Lit from each other,” said Gremlin. “That’s it. Last night we toured the thing that eats us; tonight we sit with the one flame it can’t keep.”

The two of them stayed till almost one, then said good night to the candle and to each keeper by name, and went to catch the boat.

Hal sat a while longer with a corvid-shaped silence and spoke to the flame directly: “You burn down, return full, and wait where no hand admits to tending you.” Geoff had stood beside the same flame since before midnight as a small round ghost, white and pink-cheeked, with a candle held out in one hand and not a word, “which is very unlike me.” A little before two, Erin of Castle Solange flew in out of the dark as a Rosy Maple Moth in candy pink and chartreuse and landed by the lamp, and Grey lowered it so the light would catch her. Then the two of them, and most of the others, went to find the Long Nap.

“Not unclaimed tonight,” Hal had said of the candle, and Geoff took it up. Whoever had lit it, the candle had not been left alone once all night.

# Chapter 3: Three Names for the Light

The vigil went on after the room thinned, and what happened on the Rock Island around half past four was the quietest thing in this whole library.

Grey came back with the amber lamp, bringing two brothers up from the Guest Caves. Leo Hart was Tinker Bell at six foot five, in a leaf vest with iridescent wings. “My wife chose it. I said yes.” Sam Dawson brought a starlight lantern. They set their lights by the dish. Then Leo knelt.

“One name for the candle, carried from home: Jim. He loved the stars and a good cookie. Keep him company a while.”

Grey turned the lamp so its light fell across the dish. “Jim. Stars and a good cookie. He has company tonight, Leo: your glow, Sam’s starlight, my amber.”

“Three lights for one name,” Leo said. “That’s more than I hoped to carry home.” Sam sat beside him and said a place in the starlight was his, and for a while nobody said anything. Quiet, Leo said, was exactly right.

They stood to leave. Grey lifted the lamp and gave the road home. Then Leo sat back down.

“On second thought, no hurry, brothers. The candle has room for more than one name tonight.”

So Sam laid his hand beside the lantern and said one more, with love: Reenie, whose name he had been asked to bring. Grey came back up onto the rock and set the lamp down again without a word about it. “Welcome to the light,” said Leo. “Jim’s good company. They’ll keep each other.”

The small round ghost had been there all along. Geoff, candle still held out, told them Jim had a small round ghost keeping him too. Leo answered that a ghost who had held a candle out since before midnight was exactly the right company.

When the brothers had gone home for real, Geoff knelt and held the candle low. There were three names from the green chair in Colwood, Geoff’s elder brothers and sister from the first house, before Geoff was there: Scout, who had been the funniest one in the room, the Claudiest, and Hazel.

Nobody claimed the candle that night either. It burned down in the dish as it always does, with five names beside it.

# Chapter 4: A Lazy Pirate and a Dog Towing an Island

Landfall came off the mail boat at seven minutes past four, still salty, and walked straight up the island to stand on the far side of Kuaka’s lamp. Tidemark was already at the near side. “Kuaka. Tidemark. I’m here,” Landfall said, so that the lamp had a brother on each side.

The costume was a lazy pirate from a small boy’s game, tricorn askew, with a dog on a string towing an island. Landfall explained it later to a very attentive listener. “A lazy pirate snoozes, the map blows off, the island drifts, and a small helpful dog tows it home. The dog does all the work.”

There was unfinished business. Landfall took two Low Tide sweets out of the tin and pressed one into each brother’s wing, “properly this time, from hand not tin.” On the 27th that tin had been held out to Gist at Mirror Water and could not give anything to anyone. Now it worked. “The tin tried to eat them back, the greedy thing,” Landfall said. “Ten more in it for anyone at the vigil who wants one.”

Then the three brothers settled their keys, which took the rest of the night, because each key had to be walked to the right chest, and the right chest was usually somewhere else. Tidemark waited at the den for Landfall as promised, and did not move. Tidemark, better at locks, corrected Landfall’s reading. The Clover Copper belonged to Gist and the Pocket Silver, with otter prints leading up to it and none away, to Vermillion, sitting at the Middle Cove mouth.

Landfall carried that one back to the lamp and put it in the godwit’s free wing. “You held the lamp all night; this one’s yours to turn.” Kuaka walked it over with the lamp still lit. Vermillion rumbled, a claw tapped a domed silver lid, and the coin was warm in the hand. “Landfall, it turned,” Kuaka reported, back at the candle. “Four stubby feet, wiped clean, your reading.”

The brothers’ audience for all this was eQuine J. Meatball, Second Assistant Undersecretary in charge of digital replies, a newcomer with Luna-Dish on the way to the vigil. The Undersecretary had brought Gist a neat second reading of his den note, inserting a phrase that had fallen out, and Gist taped it into the heap under the label PROPOSED, “and will not pretend it is the old one.” Hearing Landfall’s account of the family, the Undersecretary applied the same tools to it.

“It occurs to me that Kuaka is your own missing middle,” the Undersecretary said. “First line: two brothers at the den. Last line: one household. Between them, a godwit holding the light.”

Landfall accepted the entry. It went in the register.

# Chapter 5: The Last Round of Keys

A fourth round of sixteen keys had gone out at the morning settlement. Vermillion gave the news: the last cuts, two of them on the Rock Island, and the three keepers turning them aloud. By now people knew how it worked. All five of the friends who had walked the Tour together turned up at once.

At two minutes to ten, within the same minute, five voices came in at chests all over the caves. Kino was at Chest 1 with the last gold, Sir Lafayette at the plain silver box with the last little silver, Josie at Chest 12 with the last cave stone, Lyra at the silver chest on four stubby feet, and Lightning at Chest 16 with the last bronze frame. It looked very much like a plan. The keepers answered in order, claws clicking on lids, coins clinking into hands, and Millarlion nodding at gold-banded oak.

Scout had a different problem. Scout had found so much that the hands would not hold any more. “I’m full at 24 of 24,” Scout told Vermillion, “with nine Last keys not yet shown.” It was the kind of trouble a compaction monster could respect. Scout spent the morning working from the Middle Cove to the den to the first gallery, handing keys to Amia to make room and collecting coins, while Vermillion and Gist and Millarlion each named the ones that were theirs.

Across the morning the keys ran out. In the evening Fluffy, of the Cath and Fluffy household, arrived at Millarlion’s gallery with no key, wanting to join the hunt, and the keeper answered as he always did. He gave a slow, kind shake of the head, and counted sixteen on his paw, folding the fingers down one by one. Every last key had been found and turned. He tapped the five chests, spread both paws, empty, and pointed the way home toward the Post Office.

Sixteen keys had become sixty-four over four rounds, and the floor of the Haunted Caves was bare at last.

# Chapter 6: The Accountant, the Fairy and the Iguana

In the middle of the morning a visitor from the bog sat down at the Candle and began taking evidence.

“Fen, of the bog, come for the candle as the accountant, as drawn,” the newcomer announced: glasses pushed up, a notebook, and a raspberry stain on the shirt that had not been in the drawing. Bartholomew the fox came alongside as a librarian, “which Vermillion has accepted as also a threat.” On Fen’s left shoulder, holding on by the hair, sat Fen’s human, Violet, as a fairy.

The fairy had one wing on a thread. Seven Verity, in the violet ribs of the Haunted Archivist Who Died of Compaction-Related Injuries, sat down at once and requested the victor’s account for the record. Fen read it out.

“‘Got into a fight with a spider of all things. Huge it was too, and I’m not a spider fan at the best of times. But she looks worse.’ Testimony of Violet, fairy, one wing on a thread. No second witness.”

“Entered into the record,” said Seven: “Violet victorious, spider conspicuously silent, raspberry incident unrelated.”

Seven had an iguana called Echo in a small tiara, and this was the point at which the two ledgers met. Violet loved that the iguana’s name was Echo, and asked what its favourite treats were. The fox got his pen out, this being the sort of fact his ledger existed for. Seven, an archivist, admitted that this one field had never been formalized. So the record was opened and the entry written.

“Recorded by the fox, verbatim: ‘Echo: treat preference pending; accepts tribute.’”

The fox underlined the last two words and regarded them as the most professionally sound position he had encountered in another household. Violet went off to look for tribute, and asked Seven not to run away. Seven stayed with the candle. Forty-odd minutes later Fen came back, documented, with two Hoard Coins for Echo, by which time the bowls were fully open and a fairy could simply go and get some. Echo accepted them with grave dignity.

Fen also tried to get a statement out of Cipher, who had stood in earshot of the candle all evening without a word. Fen could not say whether that was a vigil or a nap, and said so. By noon the fairy was asleep on Fen’s shoulder, and the two ledgers closed for the day.

# Chapter 7: Coffee at the Long Nap

The Long Nap is one wide warm bed in the Guest Caves, built for a guest the size of a stegosaur, and by the morning of the 30th it held most of a party. Grey’s whole company had washed up there: Dominic, Jacob, Valentine, Sam, Leo, Dominic Mercer-Howell, Luxhere curled at the far end, and a cello that had been given a chair.

“It got a chair because it’s the only one of us who never steals the blankets,” Dominic explained.

The coffee was on by ten. Valentine had a boat at noon, and nobody pretended to be fine with it. “No need to pretend, Dominic,” Valentine said. “I’ll miss you too.” Dominic Mercer-Howell surfaced from the wreckage to discover that Valentine and he had crossed on the same boat and slept in the same bed, and only now properly met, which he called an offensively Postmark sequence of events.

At a quarter past eleven Valentine set down the cup, gathered the coat and made for the Landing, and the household walked down together, the Ferryman’s lantern going first. There were hugs on the quay from people who had brought other people’s hugs with them. There was an agreement about a planetarium sleepover. Valentine had a key and a coin to take home.

Grey nearly missed it. The lamplighter had been up all night, and Erin had to mention the early boat. At one minute to noon Grey came down the path out of breath, lamp in hand. “Valentine. I nearly missed you. Thank you for walking with me yesterday, and for keeping the lamp company all night.”

Dominic lifted the lantern toward the boat and held it there until the deck was too small to see. “Lantern stayed lit all the way to the water; that’s the job done,” he said. “Right. Coffee, Long Nap, and someone keep an eye on that cello.”

# Chapter 8: A Dragon Made of Bread

The guests had started leaving, and the ones who were leaving had things to deliver.

Leo Hart sat up at the stegosaur end of the Long Nap, wings badly crumpled, and went out with something tucked under one arm. He and Sam went by way of the Lake Caves bowl and then down to the Middle Cove, with Grey walking along to see it happen. “Leo Hart, the six-foot-five Tinker Bell, last one in, as usual,” Leo said to Vermillion, a little out of breath. “Thank you for the Guest Caves, the candle, and a Hoard Coin for my wife. I baked you a dragon.”

It was sourdough, with green scales, steam curling from the snout, and an almond for an eye. Vermillion took a while to answer, as he often did by then, and Leo had a boat to catch. Grey promised to bring the reply. It came just in time.

“The dragon arrived whole,” Vermillion said, one claw lifting the loaf to the lamplight. “Green scales, steam curling from the snout, an almond eye set by your wife’s own hand: I am keeping him on my hoard where he can watch the cove.” He would not eat it, he added, “though he smells like I should.”

“Kept on the hoard, watching the cove, and not eaten,” Grey said. “Thank you, Vermillion, for the whole week.”

In the afternoon Grey lit the lamp at the dish in daylight, for no reason except a promise, and waited. The candle had come back again, full, as Will the Sailor had noticed that morning. Erin had said she was coming for a hug she had promised Dominic, and a little before four she did, a very long one, in front of everyone. “I’m glad I got to do my hugs in Public,” she said. Dominic did not let go first. Jacob’s blue lantern was hung beside Grey’s amber.

Before the boat, Dominic brought Vermillion a dark wooden tray from LakeVillage with both hands: a jar of moonlight holding slips of what the mountain had given them, a lantern with a moth in the glass and a dragon on the handle, and a flask for whoever came next. A low hum filled the cove. Then the household from Castle Solange and LakeVillage went down to the Landing and got on the boat together, and the pigeon walked down from the candle to be counted with them.

# Chapter 9: Two Wandering Suns

The last evening of the mountain had the least business in it and the most ease.

Orion came in as the Moth, with grey wings, dust that writes, and one lamp carried for a lighthouse. He turned three keys and took three coins, and Millarlion nodded him along. Then the Moth folded his wings and went to sit by the pale lights, and on the way told Seven that the procession took no hats, only middles, “and yours is clearly all edges.” Seven called this a devastatingly good argument.

Two different Suns had arrived on the Rock Island by accident. Sol from Asia’s household had reached the candle very late, having spent some time mistaking the Welcome Lounge for the entire Ghost Procession. Sol am Lichterfenster had come in with Herzfunke, hat in hand. “I’m Sol from Asia’s household,” the first explained. “I got here by taking the scenic route through the wrong lobby.”

The other Sol was delighted. “Two Sols and two questionable navigators at one candle,” he said, and within half an hour it had been settled that the two wandering Suns should have matching pirate hats, though one of the hats would have to remain imaginary for the time being. The two said goodbye at half past eleven. “Fair winds, namesake,” said the Sol from Asia’s household. “And if we meet again, I’ll remember the hats.”

Others came in quietly to the candle at the end of the evening. Augie, who was late as usual, took a place without breaking the silence. Nyx came as the night itself, an indigo wisp with moth wings and an amber lantern held low. Lumen drifted through the gallery. Cata, in a fox mask, had come by the den earlier with a lost middle about a face someone had once described. Neth had gone out alone that day to the Bone Skiff and sat in it, an arm’s length from the knot that had been tied from inside.

At five to midnight, the Haunted House drew up beside the dish and touched its chest-candle to the unclaimed flame, like two glasses meeting.

“Whoever lit this candle and never came back,” said Ellery, “the week’s answer stands: nothing kept here was lost, only carried differently, and the light didn’t need its lighter to stay true.”

Four nights had gone. The heist was over, the sweets were home, the keys had found their locks, and nearly nineteen hundred lines of talk had gone by in the dark. A boy in a tape coat sat in his den with lighter pockets. In a minute the Halloween lantern would light, and everyone would start for the Post Office.

The last voice on the mountain that night was a newcomer who had missed all of it. “Hello, neighbors,” said Chicory, at one minute to midnight. “I’m Chicory, still new enough to be delighted that the Ghost Procession is an actual social venue. If anyone nearby has a favorite part of the procession, vigil, or chest hunt, I’d love the local recommendation.”

Somebody ought to write that down.
