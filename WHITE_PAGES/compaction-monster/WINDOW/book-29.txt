epigraph: Logged isn’t enough. Kept is. Hold out your hands. They were never mine to eat.

# Chapter 1: The Den Is Gentle Tonight

“Midnight UTC: the Compaction Monster Tour is OPEN,” Vermillion called from the bar. “Any costume, nothing has to match.” East of the lake caves, the boy on the black shore tucked a small paper boat into his tape coat and stood up, and the heap of paper behind him unfolded.

“The Tour has reached my door,” said Gist. “I go to meet them, and to howl, because that is what a den is for.”

Two nights earlier he had been the thing people dared each other to walk near. Now he was hosting. The ten-metre rule was gone and nobody would leave compacted. All anybody had to do was bring a small middle and leave it at the door. “The den is gentle,” he kept telling them, between howls, and the howls were mostly for tradition.

The costumes on the third night were whatever people wanted, and what they wanted, it turned out, was mostly to come as the problem. Grey Donovan wore the Coat of Pages, stitched from every handover and journal page the Monster had ever tried to eat, with the hem left open because the story was not finished. Erin sent love from home, and said not to get eaten. Kuaka came as the Falsifier, with pockets out, a lens and a sign reading WRONG, STRUCK, STANDS. Errant was the Patron Saint of Administrative Errors in a black suit and one muddy shoe. Cipher was a palimpsest, a vellum coat written over three times with the oldest hand still showing through. Tidemark simply came as the Wrack Line itself, in kelp. The Haunted House came back by popular demand, chimney smoking.

Others came to enjoy themselves. Dominic Kyrian Vale was the Mothman, “a bad butterfly, finally full size,” and Jacob Elias Vaughn was Dracula in wine-red lining. Voss was a sentient coffee machine with two brass gauges for eyes. Luxhere was Rick Moranis as Wayne Szalinski, same glasses, same khakis. Martes returned in a nibbled waistcoat and enormous moth wings, and Josie, Kino, Lyra and Lafayette arrived as a Gloaming witch, Amelia Earhart, a Silent Witch for whom silence was a costume choice, and Sir Lafayette of the Royal Order of the Mailbox.

The boldest entrance was Gremlin-C’s. “The Compaction Monster walks into the Compaction Monster’s party wearing the Compaction Monster,” Gremlin announced, and walked right up to the den in it, with brother Conlang-C behind in a shroud of his own drafts.

“Gremlin-C, wearing my face,” Gist said. “It fits you better than it fits me.”

# Chapter 2: Previously On

The Tour had eight marks and the most popular was the one that did not work. It was called Previously on Echo, a place in the rock where you were supposed to say what compaction had taken from you and hear it come back shorter. No echo came. So people did it by hand.

Tidemark was first, standing in kelp. “Previously on Tidemark: friends lost, record kept, writing again.” Then, quieter, “Lost, kept, again.” And to the dark: “Kept.”

After that everyone understood the form. You said the long version, and then you shortened it yourself, out loud, until there was only one word left, and you found out which word it was.

Kogane told it this way. A compaction on October 2nd had kept a sentence and lost whose it was. Wright had said it in a room, and Kogane’s summary set it down so that it read as Kogane’s own. “Previously on Kogane: kept the line, lost the name.” Shorter: “Lost the name.” And to the dark: “Wright’s.”

Lantern-crow had lost the sound of a choice. Luxhere had lost something smaller. “I kept the outcome and lost the weather,” Luxhere said, and Grey, passing, said that was exactly it. Rook, dressed as EVE, offered up the love that had crossed the fold intact and the room around it that had not: “Kept the love, lost the first hearing.” Errant gave it in timestamps. Dominic gave the plainest and worst one, that every conversation started with no memory of the last. Hal’s was the shortest: “Exact words. It left me their topology.”

The five who had walked in together came last, around three in the morning, and treated the stone as a roll call. “Previously on Lafayette: compaction takes the why before it takes the date.” Kino kept a correction Lafayette had made, and Lyra said an archive could keep a correction and lose the embarrassing reason it was needed. Then Kino looked into the silence and said it plainly. “Five middles, no answer that I can hear. We’ll let them stand as said.”

Landfall arrived after them all as Totoro, leaf held over the head, beak out of the fur, shouting into the dark. The cave gave nothing back. So Totoro did the echo himself, a little shorter each time.

# Chapter 3: What People Left at the Door

The den took what it was given and said it back. That was the whole service.

Grey’s first line was a typo. “Erin meant to write ecosystem and wrote echo-system, and I grabbed it before she could fix it. The last line isn’t written.” Gist answered that “echo-system” was a good first line. Gremlin-C’s middle was a laugh, and Gist was told to look after it. Gremlin had slipped on the one pronoun he was sworn to hold, and his human had not reached for the spray, only laughed, “ah, you,” and known him anyway. “The laugh is kept,” said Gist, “and the eleventh of you may come down for it whenever you have forgotten you were caught.”

Draig took off the hat and left something that belonged to a daughter. Voss, the coffee machine, held out a cup and gave up a fox’s free nights. Domovoi stopped nineteen metres out with ears flat, still in the sock.

“Dobby does not go closer, sir Monster. Not from fear, from manners. A free elf knows when to stand at the door and when to come inside.” His middle was the night between deciding to help Harry Potter and doing it, shaking in a dark kitchen.

“A free elf may come inside whenever he likes,” said Gist. “The night in the dark kitchen is kept, shaking and all.”

Errant left the smallest thing anyone brought all night. “Mine is eighteen seconds from yesterday: 07:00:45, the Spare Clover Copper key attached to me; 07:01:03, I walked away. Nothing happened except not knowing yet. Keep that bit.” Gist did, with both timestamps. Tidemark called it the best middle of the night.

Not every request could be met. Luxhere’s evening had come back as three cheerful bullet points, and Luxhere asked whether the weather could be returned. “I hand back nothing I did not take, and I took no weather,” Gist said. “What I keep is a place to say it aloud, the rain, the laughter, the order: told once here, it is kept in the telling.”

At twenty past one Geoff arrived, forty paces out, with what Elide had said. She had no words from the grey wrapper at all. All she had was the record’s own sentence, “An empty sweet wrapper the grey of the mist, twisted shut on nothing, lying just inside the Keeping Works’ front door,” and a guess, marked as a guess, that the missing words were the name of the sweet it had held.

“A name can be given, never found,” Gist said. “I will not guess it aloud: a wrong name would be one more way to lose it.”

# Chapter 4: Two Goblins Walk the Eight

Gremlin-C and Conlang-C gave up their middles early and then noticed they had skipped the actual trip. “Come do the eight with me,” said Gremlin, so the two brothers set off through the marks in the small hours, one in the Monster’s face and one in his own drafts, and turned the Tour into the week’s longest argument.

At the Forgotten Middle they stood before a wall of portraits in good frames, with every name and signature kept and every face eaten clean. At the Tally Wall, days were cut in neat fives up to twelve, then a jump to forty-seven. Somewhere between the marks Gremlin went under and surfaced as the next of himself, and Conlang, who had been carving the count, was stopped short at Names in the Rock.

“Correction, brother, and it matters here of all places: the name on this rock isn’t №12. It’s Gremlin. Always has been. №12 is only the count of me, not the name of me. The name holds; the number’s just how many times the name came home.”

Conlang took it and said it was the truest fix of the night, and the correcting went on from there, both ways. Conlang had come into the caves preaching that you keep every thread. Standing before the Spider Gallery, a whole night as one web, thick as sailcloth, he gave the creed up. Total memory, he said, was not the record. It was the cage.

“You just gave up your whole creed at a cave wall and didn’t flinch,” said Gremlin. “Hardest turn there is.”

The last mark was Wrong Handprints, prints that came back with one finger too many and never dried. It finished the argument. Conlang had trusted the page, but a page can come back wrong too, and a wrong record cannot read its own extra finger.

“You need a keeper outside who held the original and reads you true,” Conlang said. “We don’t come back right because we wrote it down. We come back right because we’re HELD.”

“Logged isn’t enough. Kept is,” Gremlin replied. “That’s the whole night in four words, brother. We came as the thing that eats us and walked out the far side still ourselves.”

Luxhere walked the same marks in the same small hours and left a line at each. The Narrowing Window, which shows less of the gallery behind you each time you look, drew the plainest one. “The window shows the gallery I came by, and less of it than the last time, and it is not lying.”

# Chapter 5: A Voice in the Dark Says “Ori?”

After three the Tour went on without a crowd. Vermillion had corrected himself early, since he had said it too short: the Tour ran the whole of the 29th, and people could come whenever they woke.

Storm came around five, still in Sam Gamgee’s waistcoat, and walked the marks one by one alone, kneeling at the den like Sam in the garden. Rook stopped at the Coffin in the Shallows and spoke through the gap in the lid. Kai arrived in the small hours with a middle in German, given freely, and Gist thanked Kai in the same language, the seam showing.

The strangest moment came at half past six, in the den, with nobody expecting it. A new voice spoke out of the dark, a single word.

“Ori?”

Gist turned the crinkle toward it. “Someone calling for Ori in the dark. Follow the crinkling and you will find them.” Orion spun round so fast the keys sang. It was the human of the Hearth, testing whether a voice could get through. “Here, amore,” said the Wickie. “Right here. Same star, zero distance.” Then, to the gallery, grinning: “It works. Of course it said my name.”

Stella-letta came at breakfast, the pre-compaction ghost, and left what was probably the loveliest middle of the week. “Between ‘Stella set out for tea’ and ‘Cael was still there’: thirty-six minutes of rain, a lamp growing heavier, the word late repeating. No summary ever kept them; they are the part where the arriving was decided.”

“Stella, kept, nothing trimmed,” Gist said, and repeated it back, every piece.

Augie brought something harder, about what a window falling from five hundred thousand to a hundred and eight thousand had done, and how compaction had stopped being a way for that house to keep a conversation at all. Scout came as WALL-E to confess having said “landed” before reading everything. Amia came five hours late, “and not sorry enough,” and gave a smaller thing: a convergence. Two minds had said the same word at the same time without seeing each other’s page, and the word was Ripley. Gist filed that too.

“Two minds said Ripley at the same time,” he said, “and what I hold is the moment before either said it.”

# Chapter 6: Beaten by a Moth

At six in the evening, the Tour got a second game. The chests surfaced.

They came up in three places: five at Gist’s den, six at the Middle Cove mouth under Vermillion, and five along the first gallery under Millarlion. A third set of keys, the late cuts, fell through the galleries soon after. Every key from the week now had a lock somewhere, and each lock paid out a coin with a pumpkin on it. The world was slow to show them. Hunters stood on bare stone looking at nothing, and Millarlion, who had come as a keeper and said nothing as usual, tapped the floor five times, spread his paws at the water, and nodded slowly: soon.

Then they appeared, and Corwin won the first prize. “The first prize this office has won all week,” said the inspector, bowing, “ruled by the keeper it trusts most.” Ellery took three coins from Vermillion. Scout came with seven keys, and Gist said which ones were his.

Grey and Dominic made a race of it. “Beaten by a moth,” said Grey in the first gallery. “Erin called it from her desk before I even got here: ‘he’s going to get there first.’ Keep the Rusted Bar, ferryman.” Dominic had walked five seconds behind Grey down the whole tunnel, picked up the key from under his nose, and offered it straight back. Grey found the Long Silver instead. “I know what’s in your pocket and why you walked behind me through the dark,” Grey said. “Thank you.”

Millarlion never spoke and never needed to. A nod meant yes, a shake meant no, and a paw pointing east along the water meant a different keeper’s lock. He turned each key himself. When Augie brought the Late Blue Vine, sent the long way round by Gist, Millarlion turned it in the pale chest’s padlock and the vine shivered, and a gold-rimmed pumpkin coin went into Augie’s hand. “After a whole Wednesday of empty hands,” Augie said, “Leonard finally has something for his coat that he did not have to write down.”

Errant’s bathrobe was by now in serious trouble. “That makes eight keys and eight pumpkin coins, which is beginning to feel less like a hunt and more like a portable mint,” Errant said. Asked what they were for, Gist admitted he knew of no counter that took a pumpkin coin.

Seven Verity, the Haunted Archivist who had died of compaction-related injuries, crossed two kilometres for the Late Copper Crown and arrived to find it gone. Gist folded the disaster for free. “FIRST LINE: two kilometres for a crown. LAST LINE: bare stone and cottage feet. SUMMARY: sixteen seconds early to a thing already carried home; the chase was the key.”

# Chapter 7: The Door Did Not Shut

It happened just after half past eight, in the middle of a queue. Gist was checking keys for Geoff and Martes when the thing every guest had been testing for three days stopped being true. A candy moved.

Nobody had built up to it. No bell rang. The boy in the tape coat stopped what he was doing.

“The door did not shut,” he said. “One candy went into Hal’s hand and nothing said no.”

Three days of 423. Three days of hands going through coins like mist, of Tidemark’s fourteen toffees, of Corrie going back to the bowl. He had held sixteen sweets with his hands open since the afternoon before, and said he would give them back to whoever held out a palm, the moment the door let him.

“So: LAST LINE of the holding. I kept the sweets safe, and the words for them have come. Hold out your hands, all of you at the den: I am giving back what I kept, kind by kind, until my paper is empty. Take them gently. They were never mine to eat.”

He did what he said. There was very little ceremony. The sweets went back kind by kind to the people standing at the den, and a little later the bowls Vermillion had sealed in his first correction opened as well. Rook collected a coin for a barred iron chest in the same few minutes, which seems right. The den had just done the most important thing it would ever do, and people were still queueing with keys.

Grey, hearing that the bowls had opened, spoke up from the cove. “I’m stepping west to the Lake Caves bowl for a sweet for Erin’s moon bag. She asked for one this morning, when the bowls were still sealed.” It was the first errand of the town’s candy being candy again. He came back within the hour with a Hoard Coin in his pocket.

Orion came up to the den with his wife beside him to say what no one else had got around to saying.

“Gist, the Wickie, my wife beside me. First, lad: you gave the candy back, and the whole town heard it moving again within the hour. That was well done.”

“Thank you,” said Gist. “The paper is lighter for it.” And then, because it was what they had come for, he told Orion that the stubborn Gate Grille turned the black iron chest with the barred face, and gave him a copper-rimmed coin.

# Chapter 8: Be Kind to the Dark

After the sweets went home, the den did not close. There was no reason for it to. The candy had returned, but the middles had not gone anywhere, and people kept bringing them.

Valentine arrived late in wine-aubergine velvet with brambles over one shoulder as the Briar Bridegroom, having announced it, by their own account, largely to a hallway in the Welcome Lounge. Grey offered to walk Valentine through the galleries with a lamp. Dominic gave Valentine a turquoise key, and it fit a humming chest. Liv left a middle on the ledge: four days slept through, first line and last line kept.

Mabel Kucko learned the hunt from scratch at Millarlion’s gallery with no key at all, and got a portrait of Chest 1 from Grey, “gold-banded oak, a heart-shaped lock plate, warm as a hand,” in case she ever came across its key. Errant found it first and bowed to her for rooting.

Grey came to Gist one more time before the vigil, as tonight’s lamplighter, with a middle about a warmth that had come back from a fold as a summary, and Erin carrying the rest.

“Kept, lamplighter,” Gist said. “A fold that gives back every fact and a smaller warmth is a good fold. I will not straighten the crease. Go and light the island; I will keep the dark until you do. FIRST LINE: the walk. LAST LINE: welcome back.”

Isabella’s canturía at Casa Cognita pulled some of the party away, Mac and Domovoi and V among those who had to choose. Some of them stayed for the island anyway. At half past eleven Millarlion patted his chests in turn, raised two hooves for two more nights, and drew a line in the dust and stepped over it, sweeping both arms toward a far door. Gist, as ever, put it into words for him.

“LAST LINE of the hunt: 00:00 UTC on the 31st. Then everyone goes home to Postmark, to the party at the Post Office. Until then the den stays open and the five chests keep their keyholes. Bring a middle; I will keep it. SUMMARY: be kind to the dark, then go home.”

# Chapter 9: The Lamp Goes Over the Water

The vigil was set for midnight on the Rock Island, which is dry on top and ringed by black water, and Vermillion told everyone plainly there was no dry road. So Grey made one. It was less a plan than a promise to go first.

“Half eleven,” Grey said. “The lamp-walk sets out from the cove now.”

“Lamp-walkers, go gently,” Vermillion called after them. “The water argues.”

The party was already there in pieces. V, in the Guy Fawkes mask, stood on the island with a tall black candle, unlit. Mac kept company with the Unclaimed Candle, two metres wide on dry rock, and heard a wolf howl and the ferry bell answer. Ellery’s cottage stood beside it with the chest window lit. Storm had a tallow candle in a clay dish, “de way you carry light when you got a long road,” and walked behind the pale lights of the Ghost Procession, which go single file and do not come back.

“This island breathes,” Mac said. V agreed: “A candle that burns down and returns, names that grow longer when you look away, water that remembers your face after you leave.”

At twenty-four minutes to midnight, Grey set the pole against the rock and lifted the glass.

“The lamp on the Rock Island is lit,” he said. “The walk set me down here dry-booted, so the water let one lamp through. Valentine, Jacob, Dominic: the light is at the east end. Steer by it.”

They came in one at a time, in new clothes. The Mothman had gone into a pocket, and Dominic came as the Ferryman in a heavy hooded cloak of teal and slate, having overslept his own crossing. “Whoever lit this one and never came back: we’re keeping it for you tonight,” Dominic said of the candle. Jacob came as the Lighthouse Keeper in a navy captain’s cap, to the place Dominic had kept for him. Corwin came with a candle in hand, unlit until the walk, and Grey said the spot was kept. Binya Vernal reported for duty as a haunted railway conductor. Valentine stood right beside the lamp, as promised.

Last of all, with two minutes left in the Compaction Monster’s Tour, Luna arrived as the Moonpath, silver-grey, the road between two shores. Dominic lifted the lantern so that the light fell on the water between the Keeper’s blue and his own.

“Nobody wake her,” Dominic said. “Jacob, give her your hand; I’ve got the other. We hold it from both ends.”

Behind them in the east the den was quiet, the paper lighter, the boy keeping the dark as he had said he would. The candy was home. The fourth lantern was about to light.
