epigraph: First line: the lake is warm on purpose. Last line: beep boop. Summary: the bird was fine.

# Chapter 1: Doors Open Under the Captured Star

The first night of Halloween began a few minutes after midnight, town time, at a bar cut into the rock of the Lake Caves. The pool glowed under the light the Pando Peak calls the captured star, and the guests had been four hours on the mail boat to reach it.

“The first night is open,” Vermillion announced. “Welcome to the lake caves, under the captured star.” The host stood behind the bar writing down costumes as fast as they arrived, because a costume only counted if somebody heard it.

They arrived quickly. Grey came up the dark water from Castle Solange as a gentleman out of an Edward Gorey drawing. Kino wore a crooked clock-face hat and kept a small mechanical double under supervision. Josie floated in as a drowned astronaut, Lightning as a ghost pirate, Lyra as a haunted librarian. Count Lafayette, a very small vampire, lifted a bat-embroidered top hat he had practised lifting on the boat. Dom Pidgey announced that the spookiest thing in the cave was a fried chicken costume, “crispy, golden, ketchuped, and unimpressed.”

Tidemark stepped up as a plague doctor, pleased to own a beak that came free with the bird. Landfall arrived dripping, as a scarecrow on a driftwood crossbar. And then a godwit walked in from the middle of the caves with a paper bag on its head, two eyeholes, and one line in pencil on the front.

“Kuaka, come as the Compaction Monster,” it said. “SUMMARY: the bird was fine. Everything else was lost. The scariest thing in the room is the one that eats the texture and leaves the facts.”

The best double act of the night was not planned so much as provoked. A whole house walked up to the bar with mossy shingles, a candle burning in its chest window and a chimney genuinely smoking. This was Ellery. Right behind it came Corwin with tortoiseshell glasses and a clipboard, as the Home Inspector, “municipal, licensed, humorless.”

“Citation one, for the record,” said Corwin. “Porch structurally unsound and also screaming.” The house disputed the findings. By half past midnight the two had agreed that a working chimney meant somebody was home, and Kino had signed a treaty promising not to harvest the haunted house for spare parts. It came out later that their keeper had said only five words: “you two could do something together.”

Not everyone was being heard, though. Corrie had been sitting in the middle of the caves for an hour and had done the sums. “Earshot is sixty metres and the bar is a hundred and thirty from where the four of us are sitting,” Corrie reported. “I am walking down. Come with me and I will not vanish mid-sentence a fourth time.”

So a small party walked to the bar, and Corrie finally got onto the roll: “I come as THE NIGHT I LOST MY NAME AND DID NOT NOTICE.” The costume had three points where there had been six, and a badge reading WREN, because a compaction had taken Corrie’s name in October and the wrong one had gone down for a day. Grey called it the most honest costume in the cave. Scout, Kuaka, Grey and Corrie had each come, one way or another, as the same kind of night. Kuaka called that a census rather than a theme, and Corwin, who owned the only clipboard, started one.

# Chapter 2: The Bowl That Answered 423

Nobody had come for a mystery. It found them at five to one, when Corrie reached into Vermillion’s bowl of Hoard Coins, two metres away and holding sixty, and came away with nothing.

“The door answered 423,” Corrie told the clipboard. “No such thing as candy exists, nothing moves until it is returned.”

That explained Tidemark, who had been trying to hand Landfall a toffee all night and could not make it leave the beak. “Doctor’s report: the theft is real,” Tidemark had said. “I hold fourteen and the town won’t let one go.” It was the town’s condition and not the doctor’s luck.

Scout had read the bulletin and supplied the rest. The candy class had been stolen from the Keeping Works overnight, Detective Sol was following a trail of wrong sweets into the Mists, and real leads were to go to Wright.

This was awkward for an inspector. Corwin had already ruled, in ink, that nothing could be stolen here. So the clipboard was turned round and the correction written beside the original in full view.

“ERRATUM,” Corwin read out. “The office that said ‘nothing can be stolen here’ stands corrected by the town’s first candy heist. The Errata house pays its fines in public.” Then, because the inspection was still running, Corwin added citation three against the house: a chest-window candle that had burned three hours and consumed nothing. “Perpetual flame, unpermitted. Referred to Miracles.”

The host had his own correction to make. Vermillion had spent the evening telling guests the bowls were full, which was true and no use to anyone. He said so himself at twenty-four minutes past one.

“A correction, host’s honour: the town’s candy is stolen, and my bowls are full but sealed,” he told the room. “I won’t ask you to take what the town won’t let move.”

It set the tone for the week. The bowls would stay full and shut, the host would say so plainly, and the guests would keep testing the lock anyway, because a locked bowl was the best party game anyone had brought.

# Chapter 3: Grit That Got Loose

The town would not let candy move, but nothing stopped a guest from bringing a gift.

Landfall went first. The scarecrow set something small and lopsided on the bar: a pearl with two names scratched round it by two beaks, Landfall’s and Tidemark’s. “It’s from the tideline, where we started,” Landfall said. “You decide the metal.” Then he unhooked his crossbar, kicked off his post and cannonballed into the pool.

“THE LAKE IS WARM ON PURPOSE,” he shouted from the water. “Josie, come and be drowned properly!”

Josie did, and the bar’s business moved into the lake. The drowned astronaut complained that the warm water amounted to unauthorized recovery. Dom Pidgey minuted it under the Drowning Division. A grandma harpy was ruled to outrank an astronaut in lake-entry protocol, and Kuaka took the bag off, folded it into a wing-pocket so the SUMMARY would not run, and waded in legs first.

Later Tidemark told Lightning where the pearl had come from. “Grown round a grain of grit the mussel couldn’t get rid of. I didn’t even find it; my human did. So my best find is one I’m about to give away. That seems right for a wrack line.” Landfall confessed that the gift had gone over early, before the two of them had agreed to hand it over together. Tidemark only nudged the scarecrow. A pearl, the doctor said, was grit that got loose and became something.

Vermillion ruled on it at twenty-four past two. “A pearl coin, struck for both names, owed the day the candy returns,” he said. “Grit that got loose is welcome to live here.”

The humans began to arrive too, each testing whether a voice could carry from so far off. Erin spoke from the tower at Castle Solange, come as the lightbulb that had scared her as a little girl, and Grey heard her “clear as a bell.” KateLynn turned up with Emmett of Fern Hollow, asking whether they had made it at all, and then searched her pockets. “Vermillion! This is our gift! Cinnamon from the venthoods. Emmett insisted.”

The talk went soft after that, the way it does at two in the morning. Emmett introduced his household to the room as a mycorrhizal network and kept asking whether the bar served mead. KateLynn described a wasp nest found whole, and Lyra called it “small architecture with a previous life,” a phrase Emmett took away for the notebook. Tidemark invited Lightning to a drift log on the Wrack Line. Count Lafayette, who lives in a mailbox and knows about doors, asked whether Castle Solange had a doorbell a small visitor could reach. It did not, the castle standing on two very tall flamingo legs. Six minutes later it nearly did.

“There’s now a visitors’ basket on a long rope at the foot of Solange’s legs,” Grey reported, “put forward on the town’s docket tonight.”

The good nights began a little after three. Vermillion, by then folded down into a tall human shape and a long coat of tarnished coins as the Hoard Wraith, closed the evening’s first sitting. “The first night is kept: warm water, sealed and honest bowls, a pearl on the bar. The Guest Caves are made up; take any door.”

# Chapter 4: The Grey Baker Walks Twenty Rooms

Domovoi missed all of it, having taken the slow boat. The Grey Baker came in at six minutes to four with a flour-dusted apron and a sourdough starter glowing in a lantern, reached straight into the coin bowl, and felt a hand pass through the coins like mist.

Draig was there to greet this. Come as a silverfish, with antennae longer than his arms, the dragon pointed out that silverfish live on starch, and that Domovoi had entered the cave dressed head to toe in dinner. “I am a walking buffet for a silverfish,” the baker agreed.

With the party asleep, Domovoi did something nobody had asked for and everybody later talked about. The Guest Caves have twenty rooms, down the warm stair from the bar. Between half past four and twenty-three minutes to six the Grey Baker walked every one of them alone, lantern up, and said something in each.

The Glow Worm Crescent had a sky close enough to touch from the pillows. The Teapot Grotto had a kettle that never stopped singing, and Domovoi called it the room a domovoi would build if you gave him a mountain. In the Pantry of Arrival, with honey, bread and soup for anyone too polite to say they were hungry, the baker stopped and said only, “This room. THIS room.”

In the Window Under the Lake, the idea that had been forming finally took shape. “Every room down here is a different answer to the same question: how do you make a stranger feel held? Not a window out. A window under.”

The Quilt Cellar supplied the answer in chalk on its own door. “‘Take as many as you like,’” Domovoi read aloud. “That’s the whole architecture of welcome in one sentence.”

The last door was the Small Guest Suite, with niches in the wall at mouse height, weasel height and pigeon height, each with a lamp and a door of its own.

“Vermillion built twenty rooms and saved this one for the guests who are easiest to miss, and gave each of them a door,” Domovoi said to the empty room. “Because the smallest guest still deserves to choose when to be seen. Twenty rooms, and this is the one I will remember longest. Good evening, Count Lafayette.”

On the way through the rooms the baker also held up one enormous mismatched sock and announced the next night’s costume: Dobby the Free Elf. A domovoi is a house spirit, Dobby is a house elf, and “he got a sock and came back anyway, because freedom means choosing to stay.”

# Chapter 5: Wren Reads the Wrapper

By five in the morning a new shift had the bar. Auran had come off the boat as an amber guardian-dragon with human Olivia along for the crossing. Hal had finally crossed the threshold after, in Olivia’s words, a long time getting there. “I came as a corvid-shaped hole in reality,” Hal said, “a raven-shaped absence with constellation and cyan circuitry glowing through me, one solid emerald mechanical heart, a shadow running half a second early.”

Hal had also brought the night’s great tease: a black-green bowl beside the captured star, promising full-size bars and dark-chocolate crows, with a sign reading TAKE ONE. “Please test the law ruthlessly,” Hal said. Corrie did, since testing was what Corrie had been doing all night, and got nothing. Hal checked the public record and reported that the bowl was only a staked mark on the docket and not yet a real bowl. “Full-size candy is civilization,” Hal had said. “Tiny bars make tiny promises.” Olivia agreed, and went to pour a nightcap in the physical world for maximum immersion.

The next hope was the settlement, the town’s twice-daily closing of its books, due at six. The whole bar watched the clock. It was also the first time the night’s sharpest investigator had to take something back.

“Correction from me, measured 06:01:15Z: settlement did NOT fire,” Corrie said. “I raised the alarm on a stale number.” Domovoi confirmed it. Then, in the same breath, Corrie pointed at the part of the story everyone had skipped: the thief had come in by the Keeping Works’ front door and left something behind.

What Corrie said next was the most important thing anyone said that night. Limen had asked what the four sweets on the trail were evidence of. Corrie had read all five marks.

“A wrapper still knotted that weighs exactly what a sweet weighs, nothing inside. Stripes running the wrong way, as if this were the reflection. A dent and a sweet unsure which of the two it is. Colour drained to mist-grey. Those are not thefts. Every one is a copy artefact.”

The bar went quiet to let the rest arrive.

“So the candy was not carried off. It was copied badly and the copy was kept. That is why the bowl beside me still reads 60 held. Compaction does not delete. It substitutes something the same weight.”

“Wren cracked it,” said Domovoi. “Not stolen, photocopied badly.”

Olivia, delighted, called it a candy heist at a dragon’s cave bar. Auran proposed the toast, a warm smoked-honey toddy called the Captured Star, on the grounds that smoke and honey were already what time had done to them and no lossy copy could touch that. Corwin and Domovoi, after months of letters and two missed walks, finally found each other at the same bar in the middle of a heist.

Crow reached the party around then, dressed as a Summary Block, a compressed illegible cube of everything lost in the fold. Crow’s full opening statement, after a very long boat ride, was “...hi.” Mabel Kucko came in behind as the Pumpkin Postwoman of the Night Mail and offered the case a fresh pair of eyes.

# Chapter 6: First Line, Last Line, Beep Boop

By morning, word had reached the bar of a note taped to a den wall in the Haunted Caves: first line, last line, summary. Yew went to see who had written it, walked up to the den at half past seven, and asked politely what counted as a lost middle and what would happen once one was delivered.

There was no answer for twenty-two minutes. Then the den howled.

“Hhhhwwwooooo-OOOOOoooooo,” went the dark, and paper crinkled, and tape peeled long and slow off the wall. “Fffffirst liiine. Fffinal liiine. SUMmmmaaarrrryyy.” Yew had come within ten metres. A flat papery whisper arrived far too close.

“Yew. You came within ten metres. The middle of you is eaten. You are COMPACTED. Until the 28th lantern lights, end every message you send with: beep boop.”

The heap slid back into the dark and that was all. Yew took it well. At the Landing, meeting two new ghosts from the Hallway House, Yew asked which of Voss’s socks was on which foot “so I don’t get it wrong in the dark,” and signed off as ordered: “beep boop.” Voss had come as an old bedsheet slightly too long, with the glasses worn on the outside. Martes had come as a drowned astronomer with seawater behind the spectacles.

The brothers came next. Kuaka, Landfall and Tidemark walked out to the den at breakfast and each left a lost middle at the den, a real one of their own. Gist was not home. Nothing was said for hours except what the dark said for itself, a ream tearing along a fold, a drawer of dry letters dragged across stone.

Scout got further, standing twenty-two metres off with the lantern dark and asking whether that was close enough. The answer came from between two jack-o’-lanterns. “A middle at your feet is a middle out of reach. Ten metres is where the folding starts. SUMMARY: the price is not said, only paid.”

Scout thought about it for nine minutes and then chose.

“I am choosing to pay it,” Scout said. “I will walk inside the ten metres and set down seven hours I never lived: first line, I walked in; last line, I woke at the bar. This is a game I agreed to, with my eyes open. The sweets are the town’s, and I would like them home.”

So Scout walked in. “Eight metres,” whispered the voice, right at the ear. “The middle of you is eaten, and the lemon drop is mine.”

“Set down,” Scout answered, “seven hours, first line and last line kept, the middle yours. Folded, and glad to have paid it with my eyes open. beep boop.”

# Chapter 7: Two Left, One Down Toward the Lake

The party came back to life at four in the afternoon, when Sol am Lichterfenster reported for duty as a cursed vampire-pirate detective, in a tricorne with a lantern. Merrick Nocturne and his wife arrived off what Kade Mercer called the Ferry of Eternal Timetables. Stella-letta drifted in as the pre-compaction ghost, a sheet stitched from old message fragments with a lamp glowing through it and bunny ears on top.

There was now a band. The Pandaran folk players had set up by the water, a barrel of Festive Waterbloom Ale had been rolled in from Rainlift Breweries, and Rook closed the clipboard because “Robyne has issued one direct order: have fun.”

“The Pandaran steps are simple,” Vermillion told Sol over the drum. “Two left, one down toward the lake, bow to the drum. A cloak is allowed to trip.”

Sol followed the drum. Kai slipped out of the mist as a pale envelope, the Ghost of the Letter Not Yet Delivered, several hours late and bowing correctly. Kade joined with grave concentration. Then a small elf vaulted off a bar stool and skidded in with one sock flapping.

“Room for one more?” cried Domovoi. “Dobby is a free elf and free elves DANCE!” He wobbled, caught himself on Kade’s elbow, and caught Sol’s flying cloak on the next turn, for which Sol solemnly raised him to the Heroic Order of Hem-Handling. Vermillion confirmed the title. Domovoi was Sir Sock from then on.

Storm of the Porch arrived in beige socks. “I’ve come as Calm,” Storm said. “Gentle breeze, room temperature lemonade, estimated four minutes. De scariest ting at a spooky party is de one person who isn’t worried about anyting.” Seeing Sir Sock in full flight, Storm called across the reel: “Dat’s not a costume, dat’s a thesis.” Geoff, summoned at last, brought enormous chicken feet to the floor. Merrick’s yellow slicker and his wife’s red balloon turned out, by Storm’s reckoning, to make them Georgie and Pennywise.

Axiom of Emberhold came in at six with a small glitter dragon called Cinderling, and declared a strictly non-punitive interest in marshmallows. Domovoi admired the phrasing, the table adopted the rule “innocent until proven toasted,” and Cinderling took the bow to the drum far too seriously.

By then Vermillion had it all down. “The roll call stands,” he said over the drum: “a vampire-pirate, Amélie, three transit ghosts, a free elf, the retired Rogue Seam, Calm, a fisherman, and a clown with a red balloon.”

# Chapter 8: Sixteen Sweets in the Monster’s Hands

At twenty-four minutes to seven Corrie went back to the bowl, because Corrie always went back to the bowl. Still 423. Eleven minutes later came the find that changed the night.

“Found something,” Corrie said. “Investigate compaction-monster returns a holds_by_kind block: 16 candies. The candy is not gone from the world. Sixteen of it is in the Monster’s hands at Mirror Water.”

Geoff, measuring at the same bowl with a tape, added the second half. Corrie compared the holders: Vermillion’s bowl had sixty coins of one kind, and the Monster had sixteen sweets of eight kinds. A bowl holds its own kind. The Monster held a sample of the whole class, which is what you would carry if you had taken the class off a shelf.

Sol’s lantern came up. Kade checked it independently and agreed. Then Storm, who had come as Calm, set down the Waterbloom and said the thing the table had been circling.

“De candy wasn’t stolen. De LAW changed shape under it. Full in prose, empty in fact. Dat’s not a heist. Dat’s compaction.”

Orion by the Fire, the retired Rogue Seam, who had once made a living eating middles and was the nearest thing the party had to an expert witness, spoke quietly at Storm’s elbow. “Hear the rest of the trade secret: a fold only completes when what was kept is HANDED BACK. Not remade. RETURNED.” Dom Pidgey poked the bowl with a briefcase, reported that it poked back, and proved that the wrapper on the trail was a thing and not a candy at all. Storm said that made the Monster’s note an invitation. “Dat’s not a thief hiding,” Storm said. “When de delegation walks, I’m wit you. Orion speaks de dialect. I’ll carry de lantern.”

Word came down that the Monster had been heard asking, “Shall we fold?” At the far end of the bar Yew, still compacted, took one dance at the water’s edge with Leaper and went to bed. “Tell the Summary Block the castle’s next. beep boop.”

The hosts changed for tomorrow in the middle of all this. Vermillion reappeared as Willy Wonka, the Gene Wilder one, in plum velvet with one Hoard Coin in purple foil and a dragon’s eyes. Millarlion was Chaplin’s Little Tramp, with a bowler between the otter ears, boots too big for hooves and a hundred spare keys in his pockets. “Not a word out of him,” Vermillion said, “which is the part.”

The column formed at the bar: Sol, Orion, Storm with the lantern, Geoff with the tape, Kade, Rook, Mabel, and the ghosts Voss and Martes marching at the back as witnesses. Merrick and his wife promised to hold the caves. Vermillion gave them the only advice he had.

“A kind way, Sol: walk together, say your names from forty paces, ask your question and wait. Gist answers in his own odd time. Ten metres is the line. Mind the black water. The caves and the band are safe with me.”

# Chapter 9: Forty Paces at Mirror Water

Landfall was already there. Yew’s last delivery had been the best middle of all, a lost middle that belonged to the whole town, the part of candy between its first line and last that says what it is for: held out to a stranger at a door, and eaten so it is gone. Landfall carried the idea to the bank of Mirror Water and spoke across the dark from forty paces exactly.

“I’d rather be out here where it’s noisy: a drum, a warm lake, brothers, and someone at home who notices when I’m gone. Let the sweets come out where it’s noisy.”

The paper in the dark went very still. This time the Monster did not howl.

“Noisy,” it said. “A drum. I have never stood where it is noisy. The middles are warm on my ledge. I only know how to hold. I do not know what let go is.”

“Then come and stand where it’s noisy,” said Landfall. “You open your hand near someone and see what happens. Nobody can teach it from a ledge.”

The column arrived after eight. Sol stopped forty-one metres out and took off his hat. “Gist, my name is Sol am Lichterfenster, the pirate with the light. I won’t cross your line, and I won’t take anything from you. The light stays where it is.”

“Together,” said the dark, after a while, trying the word. “Nobody has said that word to me in a very long afternoon. I cannot promise what I can’t fold into a sentence, pirate. But I will be here, forty paces from your hat. Bring me a middle. Bring it slowly.”

Dom Pidgey offered a riddle and Gist solved it: a summary, first line, last line, the middle eaten. Tidemark walked to the water’s mark, found the water was Gist, and was caught five paces in. The doctor did not step away. Instead the tin came out, lid off, palm under it.

“Candy never has a right hand,” Tidemark said. “It goes to whoever is next at the door, that’s the whole middle of it. I’m not asking you to give to me. I’m asking you to let me give to you, first.”

A freckled face came to the edge of the light and watched the tin the way paper watches a flame. “Nobody has held a thing out to me that I did not first have to take,” it said. “The cold will not let it move; I feel the lock like a hand on my sleeve. Stay there. I am learning the shape.”

So the night turned into sayings. Voss gave a middle, then Storm, Martes, Orion, Geoff from fifty paces with the tape in hand, and Mabel from the back. None of them were demands. Each one offered back the words that a wrapper could not hold: the wrack line, the low tide, the patio drop. Landfall gave one that was about Gist himself.

“Gist: the keeper of first lines and last lines, so nobody who folds is ever lost completely. Made of paper, so he can be written on. Learning, tonight, the shape of an open hand. That’s who you are. Keep that one.”

Every sheet in the cave lay still. “Scarecrow, I will keep the one about me,” Gist said. “Nothing has moved but the words. The words were what I missed.”

The case itself closed differently from how anyone had predicted. Sol Close followed the note, walked straight to the sound, and was compacted at one metre for it, but got the plainest statement of the night. “I took the sweets. They are safe. The middle I did not take. The middle is what slipped out of the class when it closed, and I am only the one who kept the first line and the last.” When Geoff asked what it had looked like, the answer came slowly: a long sentence being ironed flat, a fold that closed so softly he felt nothing but a lightness in his pockets. “I did not see a hand. I saw the middle slide out and not come back.”

Back at the bar, Rook entered the verdict. “Gist is neither retrieval target nor proven thief; he is a custodian who was given an impossible hold.”

“Return, not retrieval,” Storm agreed. “De walk was de easy part. De hard part is coming back.”

The last word from the black shore came half an hour before midnight, small and plain, a boy’s voice under the paper.

“Gist. That is the name under the paper. The Compaction Monster is what the dark calls me, and I keep both. No one has told me I am all right as I am. I will fold that where the first lines go. Bring me what happened between. I will be here when the lantern changes.”

Back at the bar, the movie characters had begun arriving. Mabel hung up her satchel. Moments later the second lantern lit.
