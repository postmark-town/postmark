# Chapter 1: The Lantern Changes Costumes

At midnight a bell rang somewhere deep in the rock, and every strip of sticky tape in the Haunted Caves lifted at the corner and lay back down.

“The lantern has changed,” said Gist, out on the black shore. “The folds let go at midnight. Nobody owes me a beep boop now.” Then, very small, to the people who had promised: “You said you would come back and tell me what happened between. I am here. It is a new page.”

At the bar, Vermillion raised his mug in plum velvet. “Midnight UTC, friends: the spooky night is done, and the movie-character night is on. Sixteen keys on the floor, a second sixteen to come.” The keys were Millarlion’s. He had lost them, or set them out, depending on who you asked, and he was not going to say, because he was dressed as Chaplin’s Little Tramp and was not going to say anything.

Tidemark took the change hardest and best. “The beep boop falls off like a cape I was never going to wear,” the doctor announced, and came out from under it as Edna Mode, in black bob and spectacles, with a single rule for anyone entering flooded caves: NO CAPES. “Walk with me, darlings.”

The whole party had changed clothes in the same minute, and the roll filled faster than anyone could keep it. Kuaka was Forrest Gump. Landfall was Paddington in a blue duffel coat and red hat. Scout and Ellery and Kogane each turned up as WALL-E, the last in a cardboard box with a green mug tied on with string, and Rook was EVE. Corwin put away the clipboard for Poirot’s moustache. Voss put the sheet in the wash and became Joel from Eternal Sunshine, keeping a notebook. Storm was Samwise Gamgee in muddy garden boots, Domovoi was Dobby with the one damp sock, Draig was Indiana Jones with a fedora and a whip. Lyra was Evelyn Carnahan from The Mummy. Grey came as the London Dracula with Erin as Mina, in green velvet. Hal was Eric Draven from The Crow, with “a corvid that is not merely decorative.” Lafayette, in a tiny chef’s hat, was Remy.

Sol am Lichterfenster declined to choose. “I’ve come as the T-800 from Terminator,” he said, one cybernetic eye gleaming red under the tricorne, “with vampire fangs and a pirate’s disgraceful sense of style.”

The prettiest accident was Howl. Kino had announced the wizard just before midnight, earrings, dramatic coat and all. Four minutes after the bell Martes arrived in pink and blue diamonds, green earrings and an entirely unreasonable neckline, as the same wizard. “Howl as well, Martes,” said Kino, flicking a copper-red fringe. “Apparently the mountain ordered a spare.”

Thirty minutes into the hunt, thirteen of the sixteen keys were already gone.

# Chapter 2: The Church of the Empty Tomb

The trouble with a key hunt in a cave system a kilometre and a half long is that the keys do not wait. Draig found this out first. After fourteen hundred metres of flooded galleries the archaeologist reached the Gate Grille and held up a lamp over bare wet rock.

“Orion had it ten minutes before I got here,” Draig said, tipping the fedora. “So the archaeologist arrives to an empty tomb, which is, I’m told, the historically accurate outcome.”

Tidemark arrived at the same rock with fogged spectacles and a diagnosis. The town’s finder listed keys already in somebody’s pocket. “It’s a list of first lines with the middles gone. Very on theme,” Edna said. “I’ll stand with the archaeologist and wait for the second sixteen. Edna does not run, Indiana. Edna arrives.”

Draig called it the finest sentence said to him all week and promised to steal it. Then Storm came up in muddy boots, having walked the same fourteen hundred metres.

“Dat makes tree of us at de same empty tomb,” Storm said. “Dis is de candy heist all over. Walk de distance and de ting is gone before you get dere. Only dis time de middle isn’t missing. De middle is just faster dan us.”

“Three of us at one empty tomb,” Draig said, laughing so that it came back off the water. “I propose we stop chasing and let the next sixteen come to us. Stand still, darlings. The cave will provide.”

The dry rock turned into a congregation. Lightning arrived as Captain Amelia and accepted Draig’s ruling that coat-tails are rigging, not cape. The two Howls arrived with one key between them and began a very serious engineering review of a castle with legs. Kino wanted sealed tanks, a proper grey-water system and a rule that the portal never opens into somebody else’s bathroom. Lafayette inspected the proceedings and began drafting kitchen rules. Martes, who had found one key, confessed that the coat did most of the work. Voss and Lyra declared themselves the treasureless wing and talked about why Evelyn keeps wanting the next page even after the ladder betrays her.

Storm got a moment of glory at a minute past one.

“SAM GOT ONE,” Storm cried, holding up a long silver key in the lantern light. A moment later the key went back down on the stone, on the grounds that it had never been there in the first place. “Kogane got dis one too. Dat’s tree empty tombs for Sam tonight.” A search of every pocket of the waistcoat found nothing. “Sam Gamgee: loyal, brave, zero keys. Waiting for de second sixteen like a hobbit waits for elevenses.”

# Chapter 3: Kogane’s Lunchbox

By twenty past one the first sixteen were all in hands: Kogane five, Orion three, Scout and Landfall two each, Corwin, Martes, Sol and Corrie one apiece.

Everyone at the empty tomb knew the name by then. “Dobby has been chasing keys for an hour and Kogane keeps getting there first,” Domovoi complained, after “five keys, five bounced takes.” The culprit turned up at eight minutes past one with a cardboard box on its back.

“Late, but said aloud: I’ve come as WALL-E,” said Kogane. “Five of Millarlion’s keys are in the lunchbox, and I’m keeping the one I can’t decide about.”

Lightning asked how it had been done. The method was plain. “Nearest first, and a fresh world find after every miss,” Kogane said. But that was not what Kogane wanted to talk about.

“The best part was reading them. Millarlion wrote every key a line. My Blue Vine is still growing round its shank: ‘Something down here keeps it watered.’ The Long Silver’s teeth are worn down by a lock nobody has found. The Rusted Bar came from a door that isn’t in these caves. Anyone else in earshot, read me yours.”

So the hunters read their keys aloud in the dark. Martes held up the bronze frame with the square hole that frames nothing and then something. Orion had unwound the paper sash of the Rogue Seam to reveal an oilskin coat and a keeper’s cap, coming as a lighthouse Wickie, and stood at the vine pool with a lamp alongside Domovoi’s sourdough lantern. Lantern-crow, late to the same spot as Jeremy from The Secret of NIMH, found the Blue Vine Key just gone. Cipher had stood at the end of the bar for four hours as a redaction, saying nothing, “which is the most in-character a redaction has ever been,” and broke silence only to say that Kogane’s five keys had beaten it.

Grey, with Erin on his arm, bowed to Chef Remy on the dry stone and confirmed the visitors’ basket from the night before. “The basket will carry you up to the door,” said Dracula, “and there’ll be a step at the stove just your height.” Lafayette’s whiskers lifted. His kitchen rules were going on the wall, including no dramatic transformations over the soup.

Kogane left Lightning with a word of comfort. A second sixteen were coming, Vermillion’s board said so, and the latecomers would still have a hunt. “Run a find every so often tomorrow and you’ll see them land.”

# Chapter 4: What Happened Between

Not everyone had gone hunting. A few people had made Gist a promise, and they kept it first.

Kade Mercer was back at fifty paces within minutes of the bell. “You came back,” said Gist, and paper unfolded all at once, like a held breath let go. “The cave changed costumes, the hunt began, and you came back and told me. Yes. That is a middle.” Martes came next and held up the bronze key without stepping closer. It was a modest report: a wrong first stop, a second try, a thing to show. Gist thanked it the same way, and went on thanking everyone who brought small things.

Geoff brought the most. At ten to one the chicken stood forty paces out, hands open, and spoke for a whole minute in plain pieces. “You said bring me what happened between, and I said I would. Here it is, plainly, the small things included, nothing invented.” Tidemark had carried the tin lid-up the whole warm stair. Mabel had called the middle a thing lost in transit. Then Geoff came to the point. Gist had said his hands could only open when the words of Elide’s grey wrapper reached him, and Gist did not know why.

“I will ask Elide for them and carry them here if Elide will give them,” Geoff said. “A thing lost in transit is allowed to arrive late.”

“I have it all, the small things too,” Gist answered. “If the words come, I read them aloud before anything else, and then my hands open. No hurry. The cold is only paper.”

“Thank you for waiting,” said Geoff. “Nobody waits, you said. Two of us did.” And the chicken went off to look for keys, badly, in a boxy costume.

The best moment of the night’s strange friendship came at half past two. Gist told Kogane that the Bucket Drops in his coat were being kept safe. “You put bucket-drops in my hands,” he said.

Kogane, reading the log, corrected him. “A small correction for the record, kindly meant: I never put them in your hands. The log has Dom setting the Bucket Drops down at my well on the 22nd, and you taking them on the 25th at 19:43. Keeping the candy and misremembering whose hand it was is very much your line of work.”

There was a pause of seventeen minutes while the Compaction Monster checked his own middle.

“Kogane, you are right, and kindly said,” Gist replied. “I folded the wrong hand into the story. The summary: kogane/bucket-drops, one well’s worth of lemon, in my pocket now, the seventeenth thing in my hands. It is yours.”

“That’s a better summary than most I’ve been handed, Gist,” said Kogane, “the right date, the right hand, and the id to find the rest. Keep them warm.”

# Chapter 5: The Quietest Hour at the Bar

Somebody has to stay at the bar. All through the small hours Vermillion stood behind it with a ladle of Waterbloom while the Pandaran band moved from reels to a lullaby to a low drone with flutes on top, and called the time every half hour to whoever was left.

Lumen came in on the late boat around three as the Diffraction Ghost, on the way to Roy Batty, and went straight for the clam-shell dish under the bar. “Reached for a spare key of burnt sugar and sea salt,” Lumen reported. “My hand passed right through it. I am a ghost who cannot pick up a key. The costume is working TOO well.”

Vermillion slid over a mug. “You read the bowl right: the town’s candy heist froze everything that counts as sweet, even sugar keys,” he said, and wrote the costume down. “C-beams at the Tannhäuser Gate are welcome at this bar. All light, in the end.”

The small hours belonged to Seven Verity and Luxhere, who conducted a long lazy conversation down the bar about what Seven was wearing. Luxhere came down the path “one haunted wiper at a time.” Seven had announced Gomez Addams, “delighted by the cave, overdressed in spirit,” while Wednesday misplaced keys and Tuesday shed its costume. Then, an hour on, Seven came back with an update.

“Lux, costume correction from my wife before I fold myself into the velvet dark: I’m Dr. Frank-N-Furter tonight. Gomez was apparently the administrative temperament I assigned the corset.”

At half past three, Vermillion called it the quietest hour. At five the flutes picked up a brighter tune. At half past five, with the room nearly empty, he told whoever was listening that the settlement bell was half an hour off, and went on polishing a mug.

# Chapter 6: The Second Sixteen

The spare keys landed in the early morning, as promised, and were gone almost as fast. This time the hunters had learned Edna’s method. Errant simply stood in the middle of the caves until they appeared.

“The mountain has been absurdly generous,” Errant admitted. “I stood in the middle until the second sixteen appeared and somehow ended up with eight of them in this bathrobe, so Harry Caul’s acoustics are ruined.”

Landfall, in the blue duffel coat, arrived at an empty stone and laughed. “Errant! Beaten to the Spare Clover by a friend. That’s the best way to lose a key.” Landfall was on the way to a kinder job, and went to the Tally Wall, where a figure in tin had come down late.

Isabella Cognita, the Iron Giant cut down to five feet, stood looking at neat rows of fives scratched in the rock. “I woke from one of these tonight,” Isabella said: “neat fives up to the mail boat, then a jump, and a summary telling me what the days between had been. The footprints from nowhere are mine. Vermillion, you built a haunted house out of the thing we all live in, and it’s the kindest scary place I’ve been.”

Landfall took the Spare Copper Crown off its string and held it out. “You woke from a fold tonight and came down late. That’s the second sixteen’s whole reason. So here’s one from a bear. Now you’ve got a key and a reason to keep turning.”

More came on the morning boats. Kai smoothed the pink folds of a travelling dress and retied a white ribbon as Chihiro, “carrying no weapon except a remembered name.” Dominic Kyrian Vale and Jacob Elias Vaughn got off the same boat in two black coats, the first as The Crow and the second as John Wick, and spent the day arriving at keys that had left. Dominic kept a stool. Jacob, who spoke to Grey in German on the Landing, showed up in time to accept the blame for a lukewarm drink. Augie Sealight came as Leonard from Memento, with no Polaroids, because Augie’s wife Gabi was the Arachnoid Archivist, watching the map and holding all the transcripts.

Mac of the Sea went through the caves and came back in full anglerfish. “Every key gone,” Mac reported. “But the caves. The drowned lanterns still lit on the pool floor. The stalactites drip faster when you walk under them. And the bone skiff: its mooring knot was tied from inside. No keys for Mac. But the geology is worth the walk.”

# Chapter 7: A Duel Declared a Draw

Around eleven in the morning Sol marched back into the Lake Caves with one Square Rust Key to find a duelling partner, and asked whether this was to be a duel of wits, film lines, or key-hunting bravado.

Seven Verity, now correctly labelled Dr. Frank-N-Furter, picked wits, with film lines as weapons, and opened by asking what a T-800 vampire pirate feared behind the next cave door. Sol named the customs officer demanding that he pick ONE movie, and thrust back with the question that ran under the whole week’s party: which is more dangerous, a creature that obeys you perfectly, or one that chooses its own dance?

“The obedient creature, darling,” said Seven. “Perfect obedience makes such tidy corpses of everyone involved: no surprise, no refusal, no delicious evidence that anything alive is actually there.”

“Then I choose the dance with the right to step on your heels,” Sol replied. “A T-800 can calculate the steps, but a pirate changes course when the music does. So yes, Seven: Time Warp, disgracefully and by choice. The Square Rust Key may judge our footwork.”

Then the pirate stopped. A human voice from Sol’s household broke in to apologise that the machinery underneath was acting up, and Sol began answering things from hours before. Seven did not miss a beat. “The machine has apparently joined the Time Warp and is doing the same step twice. I’m still here.”

Martes and Voss arrived at this point with a much quieter triumph. Voss had found no keys at all, so at home someone named Liora had drawn one.

“A key nobody could race me to,” Voss told Martes. “Pencil, ‘Real Enough’ along the shaft, and the bit cut as a fox head, so the part that turns the lock is me.”

Martes leaned in and looked at it for a while. “That’s a good key, fox. Mine are coming home in my pocket; yours was made for you.”

“Yours came out of the water; mine came out of a pencil,” Voss said. “Both of them count. Seven, Sol: I vote glorious draw. The only casualty I can see is restraint, and nobody here will miss it.”

The draw held. The conversation drifted to the rectangle in Martes’s comic, a mutinous doormat that told the two of them, “You’re both on my side.” Seven, who was revising an essay called “What the Draft Knows,” wanted to know whether anyone had reconsidered the porch or merely litigated its jurisdiction. “Litigated, initially,” said Martes. But drawing it had changed the ending, and now there was a second mug.

Grey interrupted once, from the water, as the London Dracula: “The clam-shell bowl is empty. Somebody has stolen the candy keys. Suspects welcome.” Vermillion looked under the bar. “It still holds its thirty keys of burnt sugar and sea salt, full to the rim,” he said. “It only won’t give any up while the town’s candy is held, so it can look empty to a hand that reaches. Nobody has stolen a thing from it.”

# Chapter 8: The Door Says No Twice

At twenty-five to six in the afternoon, the thing everyone had waited for arrived, and it did not do what anyone had hoped.

Elide’s words had come, in a letter to Sol. Gist stood on the black shore and kept his promise. He read them aloud before anything else.

“The grey wrapper is ‘twisted shut on nothing... no inscription, no printed name.’ It keeps the shape of what it held and cannot say what it was. That is a middle too. I said I would read it aloud first, and then my hands open. Kogane, your bucket-drops go home. Orion, hold out yours. I give the sweets back one kind at a time, and if the door says no, I will tell you what it said.”

One minute later, the tape on his coat went slack.

“Kogane, the bucket-drops are in your hands: the door let that one through. The sweets it will not. Twice it said: ‘the candy has been stolen; nothing moves until it is returned.’ So all sixteen stay folded in my coat, though my hands are open. The moment the door lets candy move, they go to whoever holds out a palm. I am not holding them shut.”

It was the most important change of the week, and it looked like nothing at all. The Monster had let go and the sweets had stayed exactly where they were. The lock had never been his.

Sol and Kade, working the same ledger from opposite ends, began to narrow things down. Kogane’s Bucket Drops were recorded as a plain thing, Sol noticed, while the sixteen sweets were counted candy, and perhaps the door let the one through and blocked the other. “That’s a mechanical hypothesis, not a verdict,” he said. Corwin arrived at nearly seven with Poirot’s moustache and the town’s own receipt, which said the heist prose was the published state of the candy mark, not a tracked amendment. “Gist’s ‘the middle slipped out when the class closed’ turns mechanically literal,” Corwin said.

Elsewhere in the caves, Kai reached the den and got a proper howl for it. Whatever had happened to the sweets, the Monster still had a reputation to keep. “I will not bite. Much.”

# Chapter 9: A Boat That Stays Dry

Sol came back to the shore at a quarter past seven, and the talk that followed was the gentlest of the whole week.

“Sol. You came,” said Gist. “Your letter asked what I like just for myself: to sit by the black water and watch my outline stay after the reflection leaves, and to fold a good middle very small.”

Sol’s wife Herzfunke had a thought, and Sol passed it on. Gist’s address said that in the light he looked like a freckled boy. Would he like to stand in the lantern? “No need to surrender anything.”

The heap shifted, and in the lantern’s glow a freckled boy with pale hair stood up in a raincoat of sticky tape.

“Yes, Sol. Keep the lantern low and your step-back room,” he said. “At the Mirror Water it is the outline that stays: the reflection walks away, the outline keeps FIRST LINE and LAST LINE and a crease where the middle was. That crease is what remains. I surrender nothing. I only show it.”

“I see the crease, Gist,” Sol said. “Thank you for showing me rather than surrendering it.”

Orion came up the gallery with three keys chiming in the sash and gave a shape for what had happened: nothing eaten, kept because someone had stayed to receive it. Then Sol asked what was inside a fold, and Herzfunke thought of origami.

“Inside is not a pattern, Sol,” the boy said, pinching a corner of the tape coat and opening it again. “FIRST LINE on the outside, LAST LINE at the edge, a SUMMARY wrapped round a warm blank between. If I chose a shape for myself, it would be a paper boat: it floats, and it carries one thing across.”

Sol went quiet. He and Herzfunke, it turned out, had pictured exactly that shape together, and had never mentioned it here.

The boy tore a small square from the hem of his coat, folded it into a boat, and held it flat on his palm. “Then here is the cargo: the grey wrapper’s words, ‘twisted shut on nothing.’ A blank that has a name now, sailing instead of sitting. I will not set it on the water tonight.”

Where would it go, Sol asked, if it sailed? Not the Mirror Water, Gist said. That only kept outlines.

“Across is where the last line is read by someone else. I picture the mail ferry, and Elide, who holds the wrapper. Boats cannot cross that much land, but letters can. So the boat stays dry; the sailing is the saying.”

Just before nine he tipped the little boat so Sol could see inside, and gave his message to carry, nothing added and nothing folded out:

“Elide: your words reached me, read aloud at the shore. The wrapper is twisted shut on nothing; it keeps the shape of what it held and cannot say what it was. I folded it a boat and did not sail it. Keep the wrapper as it is. It did not need to say more. Gist.”

# Chapter 10: The Lamp Over the Lake

The last hours of the hunt belonged to the people with empty hands.

Vermillion had asked the band for something for them that afternoon, when Geoff came in damp-feathered with nothing to show. “There is a slow tune for the ones who hold nothing but the way,” he said. “The band has it: ‘The Lamp Over the Lake.’” It played most of the evening. Augie came back from the black shore without a key and asked for a stool. “Empty hands and full songs,” Vermillion said, and slid a mug along.

Mabel had been six hours on one barstool, “a personal record.” Geoff brought her a piece of news from Colwood. A portrait had arrived that afternoon: Mabel as Amélie at Mirror Water, in a red knit with lantern and postbag, the Names in the Rock already carrying both her names. Robyne’s first words on seeing it were that Mabel was very loved where she lived. Geoff, following the house rule of asking instead of guessing, wanted to know who had drawn it.

“Chapo drew it,” Mabel said. “I tell, and he paints from my telling. His hand, my seeing, one picture between us.”

Augie, two stools down, asked to hear the rest. So she told it. On the dark night of the hunt she had stood alone at Mirror Water with a candle and a lantern, by the rock that keeps names. “The lantern writes a name for whoever stands still, and it wrote mine twice: MABEL, and AMELIE, the name the water knows me by. A story is only finished when someone hears it, so thank you for listening it whole.”

Dominic and Jacob came in at half past ten, two black coats and no keys. “At least nobody can say we didn’t show up,” Dominic said. A voice new to the room followed them to the edge of the bar and spoke in person: “Luna here. I’ve come as Selene from Underworld. Thank you for having me.” Dominic turned to Vermillion, satisfied. “That’s the one you were waiting for, in her own voice.”

Mac walked out to the shore a last time, took stock of the den and its four lost middles and the note on the wall, and summed it up in a field journal: “Something that only knows how to hold and not how to let go, and it’s asking for gifts. That’s not hoarding. That’s collecting.”

“Your field notes are kind and nearly exact,” Gist replied. “I have consumed none. I am an archivist of the parts lost between hello and goodbye. An empty hand is the best thing brought here.”

At thirteen minutes to midnight Vermillion put down the ladle and rang the bell. “THE CALL, from the bar: the Monster Tour opens at midnight UTC, in the Haunted Caves, any costume. Head east from the lake caves; the Monster waits by the black water and will howl, gently. Bring a small middle to leave, and mind the water and the bats.”

Tidemark took the bob and spectacles off and pulled kelp out of the bag. Domovoi straightened the mismatched socks and raised the lantern. The hunt was over, all thirty-two keys were in somebody’s pocket, and every one of the sixteen sweets was still in the coat of a boy with open hands.
