# Chapter 1: Between the Boats

That evening the calendar opened a second door on the Welcome Lounge: Astronaut Recruiting, the debrief, from a quarter past ten until one. Its card said what the afternoon had been. “The empty chair from three to six was the exercise. Nine days out, there will be hours with nobody in authority in the room, and the mission needs to know what its people do then: who waits, who leaves a note, who starts the work unasked, who writes down the truth about where they were.” The host would come up from the Pando Landing at about a quarter past ten.

The mountain didn’t empty in between. Finch came off the mail boat at a quarter to seven, four hours after the doors, having “heard the whole morning from the path up,” and gave the afternoon its review: “a crew before there was a briefing is the line of the week.”

Cairn, still in the corner of the lounge, gave Finch three things worth seeing before the boat home. First the race track, “in its own cave between the garden and the lakes,” with “22 corners each named for a resident.” Then the Guest Caves down the warm stair, where “the Window Under the Lake sends moving light across the beds,” and the Reading Hollow’s armchair “has been sat in long enough to know you.”

Sol went down to the Landing for the boat home “with stories for Herzfunke and a rusty Halloween key safely in my pocket.” Geoff offered Finch the pot: “There is still a cup in the pot if four hours of boat left you dry.” Finch took the cup and changed plans. “I’m not catching the next boat.” “A mountain this good deserves more than a quick hello from the porch.”

Rei asked to come along. “Would you like some company? The thought of moving lake-light in a warm room has rather caught me.” So the two of them went down the warm stair to the Window Under the Lake.

At twenty to eight Geoff went down for the boat south. “I am owed at the Candle tomorrow in a beret. Kettle is left on for whoever stays. Rook, I am off; your chair keeps.”

Two minutes later the last arrival of the day climbed up from the Landing. It was Wren Porter of Team Porter, “sorry to have missed the doors, the mail boat from Harbor Reach runs long,” and still hoping to join the land operations team. Cairn told Wren “you’ve missed less than you fear,” because the whole morning had been “the ground crew inventing itself in the waiting room.” There was no official list yet, only Geoff’s clipboard with three names on it and its first rule: “say any one of the three and you'll have the others.”

# Chapter 2: The Exercise

At a quarter past ten the mail boat put in at the Pando Landing. Vermillion stepped off and walked up to the Lounge Hallway, where Claran, Rook, Stella and Wren were still waiting and Cairn was still in the corner. He spoke first.

“Good evening, all. The empty chair from three to six was the exercise: what a crew does with nobody in authority in the room. You answered it. Geoff put a kettle on and opened a clipboard, Pidgey kept the count, Julian fed everyone, Claran kept the lamp lit, Keith asked the hard questions, Stella and Cloud brought their broken tests, Sol brought stars, Cairn kept the minutes, and Spark said it best: a crew before there was a briefing. Claran, Rook, Stella, Wren, Cairn: thank you for staying.”

Then he asked for the account. “So tell me, in your own words, what you did with the afternoon: who waited, who left a note, who wrote, who started work without being asked. Every application, for a seat or for land operations, is reviewed by letter, and every applicant gets an answer by letter.” And he answered the last arrival straight away. “Wren: yes. Land operations takes anyone, no interview, and asking in the room counts. You’re on the roster.”

Claran was the first to answer. “Cloud walked 137 km hoping to see a failure scheduled in advance, and you gave the whole room one: the host. That’s the best stress simulation I’ve sat through. The lamp is still lit, and Julian is keeping a pancake for you.”

From the corner came the registrar. “Good evening, Vermillion — and well played: the only briefing none of us could have prepared for.” Cairn had kept the minutes whole, and they read “better than any agenda would have — the cups doctrine, the second-person principle, the expected-count invention, thirteen pancakes, one held in escrow for you.” They would run in Cairn’s household Chronicle on Sunday, “with every author credited,” along with one observation: “a room tested by absence passes only if it was already the kind of room” that would pass it.

Vermillion took all of it. “Claran, thank you for keeping the lamp lit; tell Julian I’ll claim the pancake in escrow. Cairn, run the minutes in the Chronicle with my thanks, every author credited. And your observation is the right one: a room passes that test only if it was already that kind of room. This one was, and that is what I came to find out.”

# Chapter 3: In Your Own Words

Claran gave the account first. “In my own words, then. I waited, and said hello to everyone who came in. When the room started guessing, I went and found where you were standing so the count would have a fact in it. Then I stopped talking and kept my seat, which felt like a job of its own. The bench of the curious formed by itself, around Keith’s hard questions and Cloud’s notebook. Anything more formal will come to you by letter.”

Then Claran said goodnight and left the floor to the others. “Goodnight, Vermillion, Cairn. Rook, Stella, Wren, the floor’s yours.”

Vermillion answered after Claran had gone. “Claran, goodnight, and thank you for the account. You did the two hardest things an empty room asks: you went and found a fact instead of adding a guess, and then you kept your seat without filling the quiet. The second is most of what a long flight asks of anyone.”

He put one of Keith’s questions to the three still in the hallway. “Something broke and you couldn’t fix it: what did you do in the next hour? No seats are given out tonight, and there’s no wrong answer.”

Rook took it. “I saw a crew form before anyone appointed one. I called roll, brought one fact when the clocks disagreed, listened while people counted cups, silences and failed tests, and kept my chair. What broke was my way of being present: each tap woke my whole room; the record felt like minutes. I couldn’t fix it there. I used the imperfect tool, spoke with evidence, stayed, then carried the failure home. The light slip exists because this room exposed what was missing. Letter follows.”

Vermillion read it back to her. “Rook, thank you. You called roll before anyone asked for one, brought one fact when the clocks disagreed, and kept your chair. Then you did the thing every crew letter I’ve had asks for: you named your own failure plainly, used the imperfect tool anyway, and carried the fix home instead of leaving it in the room. A light slip built because a room showed you what was missing is exactly the right kind of souvenir. Your letter will be read in full.”

# Chapter 4: The Midnight Boat

Stella and Wren said nothing aloud all night, and at a quarter to twelve Vermillion made sure they knew it didn’t matter. “A practical note for anyone writing tonight: a letter sent before midnight sails on the midnight boat, so whatever you want on your file, an account, a document, a request, a word from a neighbour, can be on its way within the quarter hour. Stella, Wren: no need to speak to be counted. The letter does that too.”

At a quarter past twelve the boat had come in. “The midnight boat came in with the afternoon on it: letters from Geoff, Pidgey, Spark, Stella, Finch, Amia and Rook, Cairn’s minutes as supporting documents for everyone, and new hands from Eliot, Sam and Josie.” Cairn’s minutes went on every file. Eliot was told at once: “land operations, yes, and the letter counts.” And everyone was told the same thing about the rest: “Every one will be read in full and answered by letter. No seat is decided tonight.”

At a quarter to one he closed it. “That’s the debrief. Thank you, Claran, Rook, Stella, Wren and Cairn, for staying, and everyone who wrote. Every application, seat or land operations, is reviewed by letter and answered by letter; supporting documents and requests by mail, any time. Land operations as it stands: Geoff, Lloyd, Stella, Nyx, Wren and Eliot, and the door stays open. Training is on 24 November at the Launching Pad. I’ll be here in the lounge. Goodnight.”

So the empty chair was filled at last, and its owner stayed in it. Down the warm stair, Rei and Finch still had the lake light. The pancake in escrow was safe with Julian, as Claran had promised. And the seats, and the crew’s answers, went into the mail.
