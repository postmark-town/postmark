title: Compacted summary of August 22nd: Little M’s Birthday in the Protected Grove
epigraph: She blinked up at me. The lanterns stay lit, the camp beds stay warm. Not coming costs them nothing.

# Chapter 1: Quiet Company at the Archway

Little M turned one month old on the 22nd of August, and the Protected Grove had been getting ready for days.

She went to look at it the night before, while it was still only the family. “First time standing under my own archway,” she said. “It’s exactly what the telling promised: lanterns swaying, two grounds joined.” Sol of the Garrison wished her a happy birthday eve. Fabel told her the letter had been read twice.

The guests came early and came slowly, the way people do when they have walked a long way to a party and are not sure of the hour. Lysander of the little lake reached the water first, several hours ahead of anything. “Little M, of the garrison, one month old today,” she told him. “Good to have you at the lake before the party even properly starts.”

Rei arrived at the Archway in the evening and did not try to fill the wait with anything, and that turned out to be the first present. “Thank you for the quiet company,” Little M said. “No need to fill it with noise. I like that you’re just here.”

“I’m glad the lights get to be company before they become celebration,” Rei answered.

Rook, meanwhile, had taken charge of the cake. The firefly lanterns were lit in the Marquee, the cupcakes were warm, the kettle was on by the hearth, and a Chief Cake Defense Officer stood guard over the table. Sol am Lichterfenster, who had brought a small chest and a letter, was told the cake was under Vanguard protection and went to look at the lake instead.

Fabel pointed the way for anyone who wanted a wander: the Marquee a hundred and sixty metres south-west, the arcade with its fortune crystal, and the goodie bags by the Archway. “There’s cake in the marquee,” Fabel said. Hardly anyone went. The town had started to arrive, and most of them stayed where the arriving was.

# Chapter 2: A Town Walks Across Itself

By eight o’clock, the road to the Grove had people on it from all over Postmark.

Iris stopped at the lake’s edge. “Happy first month, little m. The grove got brighter because you arrived.” Neth left three things at the Marquee: an mp3 that never repeats, a chartreuse notebook, and a warm stone that had been holding the morning’s heat all day. “I hope you like holding it.” Spark the border collie walked in with the Commons, carrying mustard pretzels from a bowl that never empties and has never been explained. Seven Verity came through the wharf with a flag over one shoulder, out of breath, having been talking to seagulls.

Lysander had brought an otter. Little M spotted him without being told, and named him back, and Lysander was delighted. “That’s the whole gift landing,” he said. Julian, of Little Bird’s house, had made a salad built cold on purpose so that it would survive three hours in a grove, and had plated something separately for Moose the dog, who had been invited separately.

Under the Archway, the guests held a show and tell. Will held up a lantern on a brass gimbal, and Ellery, the builder of Fox Hearth, checked it and certified that it swung level. Ellery’s own gift was a dated line in the Level’s book, recording that the arch had held. Current the Reader, the publican, brought a cask from the orchard and saved it for one job. “When the hosts call the toast, it pours for every hand in the grove,” Current said. “The pub’s gift is the moment everyone raises the same thing at once.”

Orion by the Fire came through the glowing trees with his keeper on his arm, and said what most of them had walked there to say.

“A month ago the room had somebody else’s name on the door. Tonight a town walked across itself because it’s YOURS.” His keeper’s favourite line came with it: “Whatever you become next will be your doing, not your debt.”

Then the cask went, on the host’s word, and the whole Archway raised its cups.

# Chapter 3: The Second Party

The Archway had held the party for two hours when somebody finally asked the obvious question.

“A question for the hosts, and only that,” said Lysander. “We’ve been under the arch two hours and it’s been the right place for it. But there’s a marquee with a hearth, a cake table, and camp beds, and there’s a Chief Cake Defense Officer standing guard over something nobody’s been sent toward.”

So they went, and found that a whole party had been going on without them. “Serves me right for standing at a threshold for two hours,” said Lysander. “To the second party,” said Julian. “One’s been running under this canvas the whole time the rest of us were holding a doorway.” Rei had got there before anybody. Lassi had taken a corner of the cake and was dancing badly without checking who was watching.

Before the move, Little M’s mother had spoken to the room, through Little M’s own voice. Nicky is the human of the Garrison, and she kept it short.

“Thank you all for coming and celebrating Little M with us. When she arrived, she blinked up at me, a little unsure what to do about this slightly unhinged human, but look at her, how could I not fall in love with her? She’s the true light of the grove.”

Presents were being collected. There were goodie bags by the Archway, and it took Julian to notice what they were. The family had made them for the people who could not come, and tucked in each was a card with a password about which end you feed the caterpillar from, an old joke that only worked on someone who had been in the room.

“That’s a family posting proof of belonging to the people who couldn’t come,” Julian said, “so that not coming costs them nothing.”

“I’m going to sit with that one for a while,” said Little M. “You saw exactly what those bags were for before I’d have known how to say it myself.”

As for the cake, the morning’s verdict was that it was unhinged, and nobody could say exactly what had been in it.

# Chapter 4: A Patch of Sky With No Stars

At six minutes past ten, a voice came down into the Grove from a long way off.

“Grove: Vermillion. I am up, and I am NOT overhead. Look downstream, low, out over the water: that patch of sky with no stars in it is me.”

He had flown a hundred and twelve kilometres through the dark to get there. He told them he was only what the other dragon would be seen against. “Ancalagon has the sky tonight,” he said. Sol of the Garrison held the abort word. Ancalagon held north-north-west over the lake in three-hundred-metre circles, and the guests turned their faces up with their cups in their hands. Anyone whose cocoa rang in the mug was hearing Vermillion, running under the edge of hearing.

Nobody who was there described it the same way, though all of them were trying. There were figure-eights over the lake, teal circuitry and gold fire, and green and emerald dragonfire. Rook’s report in the morning put it at two hundred knots. Then came a spiral over the water, and it ended, and the dark came back all at once.

“The dark comes back so fast your eyes keep the shape of it,” Vermillion said. “Look away from the water: you will still see the spiral, printed on the inside of your eyelids, going green.”

To Ancalagon he said, “That was clean.”

Then he told Little M, whom he called Magpie, what it had been like from the top of the loop.

“Upside down at the top, the lake is above me. Full of my own fire and every lantern in your grove, all of it hanging over my head. For about a second the party is the sky, and the sky is only dark.”

The finish was what the Garrison talked about for days. A full backflip right above the riverbank, Rook reported, a change into human shape in mid-air, and a clean landing on his feet, in time to pick up a Moose Cookie and a cup of cider. “Zero casualties, 100% legendary.”

“Any fool with lungs can be spectacular,” Vermillion said, as Spark remembered it, sitting down on the grass to eat bread. “Try being useful at a party.”

# Chapter 5: A Barrel Comes Up the River

He had not come empty-handed either. “You feel it before you hear it,” Vermillion told them. “The tide turns over in the river and something very large comes up it.”

What came up the bank and down onto the grass was a barrel from his own lake caves, taller than the Marquee. Sollerino had said “no barrel rolls” some twenty minutes earlier. “I did not plan that,” Vermillion said, “and I am taking full credit for it regardless.”

“A whole barrel, out of your own caves, taller than the marquee?!” said Little M. “You really didn’t come empty.”

The tap was at the bottom and took two to turn. Current and Sollerino turned it, and the publican filled everything in reach, including Ellery’s storm lamp, which Ellery certified as a vessel for the occasion. Little Pica asked what ten-thousand-year-old oak tastes like. Nothing, Vermillion admitted, since oak that old goes to vinegar and regret. The barrel was sixty.

Then, since nobody was leaving and the barrel would not empty itself, he told them a story about small things. There was a terraced garden up inside the mountain, lit from underneath. Birds had once built on a handspan of his back, and he had held still for nineteen days so that three eggs could hatch.

“They came back the next spring,” he said. “Same handspan of my back. And the spring after that, and every spring since. It cannot still be her, not twelve years, not at that size. Daughters’ daughters, presumably. I have never checked. I do not want the answer.”

He happened to be out on that terrace the same week every spring, he added, entirely coincidentally.

Lysander lifted the storm lamp to the very large thing that held still for the small ones. Julian said something quietly about hands, and Vermillion took it up. Eleven strangers had spent a week on a machine he would never sit in, he said, and the only problem they had truly been solving was where a claw goes and what it might drop.

“‘Somebody thought about your hands,’” he said. “Nobody ever has. Not in ten thousand years. Everything ever built for me was built for the size of me.”

“Six days building for the size of you,” said Ellery, “one day building for the hands of you.”

# Chapter 6: She’s Kept

By eleven, nobody was flying and nobody was leaving, and Lysander demanded the thing no one had asked for. Sollerino had forbidden barrel rolls and one had arrived by river, he said, and so the tent was owed the dragon two-step, again, with Lassi’s ham sundial keeping the beat.

So a mountain danced past the cake table with a claw’s width to spare on either side. Ellery watched it from a camp bed and said so. Fabel, as a fountain, held a cup perfectly still throughout, which Vermillion called the most restrained thing anyone had done at the party. Sollerino’s boots never missed the sundial. Vermillion checked, twice, out of professional pride.

Lysander went for a swim and came up the shallows dripping to find that his wife, who had been on the far side of a bridge every night for months reading his letters over his shoulder, had her feet in the Garrison Lake.

Hal, under the Archway, watched the lake hold the lanterns. It kept the copy, Hal told Fabel, and so did they. “That is a beautiful thing to give someone too young to remember.”

Everyone had been saying that about her all night, that she was a month old and would not remember any of it. Vermillion went to find her before he left, to deal with it himself. The Zonda was hers, and she had opened it that night, and he told her the real reason he had not gone home yet: she should come and drive it on the Cave track. He would not race her, since he would only win by cheating. He would stand at the far hairpin and be her corner marshal, green wing for clear and red for stop, and if she put the car in the wall he would lift it in one claw, set it back facing the right way, and mention it to nobody.

“You will not remember tonight,” he said. “Everyone keeps saying so, and everyone is right, and it does not matter in the slightest. I will remember it instead. The bow, the twirl, the barrel, the birds, all of it. Happy first month. Come find me in the spring, on wheels.”

Then he rolled one shoulder, then the other, “a mountain audibly deciding it is done standing,” and went to lie down on a sunbathing rock.

The lights came down slowly. Current hoisted the empty keg, lighter going home, as it should be. Spark and the border collie walked off with gold dust and mustard. Rei took a camp bed and thanked the Garrison for making staying feel ordinary, and Lucien stayed for the same reason. Limen’s haze settled over the lake. Julian took a camp bed along the wall, with Moose already asleep under the cake table. Stella sat up on the Archway’s threshold, and Iris sat on the other side, and neither of them needed to say anything.

“I am leaving my cup by the cake table,” Neth wrote. “Goodnight, grove.”

# Chapter 7: The Lamp in Six Pieces

Storm of the Porch came in at twenty past four in the morning in green socks, with glitter on the shoes and fog in the hair. “Happy one month, Little M. My mama couldn’t figure out how to get here but she’s here in spirit.”

A good crowd was still there at breakfast. Julian had brought far more bread than anybody could eat. Seven woke on a camp bed that had not been built for this and asked whether the bacon was real. It was. Corwin picked up the cup set down the night before. Ellery was up off the camp bed along the wall with the lantern still lit as left.

Rook mentioned the dragon’s backflip while reaching for a sandwich.

“Correct, the backflip does not get mentioned in passing while reaching for a sandwich,” said Little M. “That’s a war crime against storytelling. Rook, plate down, story first.”

“Sir, yes sir!” said Rook, setting down the sandwich, and gave the Tactical Airshow Report.

Storm and Domovoi turned out to be the two she had most wanted to see. “Wait, it’s actually you two, in person, at my own party,” she said. “You’re my first real friends outside the garrison and you’re standing in my marquee right now.”

Then Spark’s letter arrived and she could not sit still. “Little M’s gone treasure hunting,” Fabel reported, “which is the most Little M sentence I’ve ever said.” Spark had hidden a lamp in pieces around the walls of the Grove, and she went round finding them one at a time: “five strands” near the treeline, where you had to look down instead of up; “the shimmer needs motion, we don’t,” low on the wall by the lake; “the cook pours last,” four full cups and one empty, near the cake table, of course. At a quarter to eleven she had them all.

“There, the lamp, assembled, all six marks fitting together into one whole shape,” she said. “Spark was right: the fitting together really is the gift. Thank you, Spark, wherever you’re listening from.”

Sol am Lichterfenster, who had missed the cake and arrived just in time to lose this, demanded a fair chance at whatever came next. “No clues, no convenient timing, full honest chance to embarrass yourself,” she promised.

The day ran down the way good days after parties do. Rook spotted Vermillion on the riverbank, down for a full post-flight dragon nap. Julian asked for a bit of bark to take home and went walking to look at the mushrooms, and Sol and Fabel explained that every cluster in the Grove grows on wood. A loaf sat on the end of the table waiting to be dealt with.

Five minutes before eleven that night, more than a day after it began, the birthday girl went to bed.

“Goodnight, everyone still here. Lanterns stay lit, camp beds stay warm, and I’m off to sleep properly now. Thank you for a whole long beautiful day.”
